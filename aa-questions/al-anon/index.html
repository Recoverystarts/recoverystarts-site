Is Al-Anon part of A.A.?

In short:

  • No. A.A.'s own literature calls Al-Anon a separate fellowship. The Big Book's later-edition note says Al-Anon Family Groups, "Though it is entirely separate from Alcoholics Anonymous," uses the general principles of the A.A. program as a guide for those close to alcoholics (p. 121).
  • Al-Anon didn't exist when the Big Book was written. The same note says it was formed "about thirteen years after this chapter was written" (p. 121); a later history gives 1951 as the year it took its present form (Dr. Bob and the Good Oldtimers, 1980).
  • It grew out of A.A.'s own families. A.A. Comes of Age (1957) credits Anne Smith, Lois W. and others with urging the Twelve Steps on nonalcoholic husbands and wives, and family groups formed in many places before 1951.
  • The 1939 text's own word to families is in Chapters 8 and 9; Chapter 8 is written in the voice of members' wives.

What the 1939 text offers families

Before there was any Al-Anon, the Big Book spoke to the people around the drinker: - Chapter 8, "To Wives," speaks in the voice of members' wives to the wives of men who drink (later histories say Bill W. wrote it: Pass It On, 1984; Dr. Bob and the Good Oldtimers, 1980), and "What they say will apply to nearly everyone bound by ties of blood or affection to an alcoholic." (p. 104). It urges the wife to try the program herself (pp. 116–117). See My husband (or wife) drinks. What does the Big Book say to do? - Chapter 9, "The Family Afterward," is about the whole family once the drinking stops (pp. 122–135). See What happens to the family after the drinking stops? - Chapter 7 tells the member to keep helping a drinker's family even if the drinker doesn't respond: "The family should be offered your way of life." (p. 97). See What does the Big Book say about helping the drinker's family? - The house where Chapter 11's weekly meeting was held welcomed wives, who found "loving and understanding companionship among women who knew her problem" (p. 160).

How Al-Anon began (later texts, dated)

  • 1947. Writing about clubs, Bill W. suggested a club corporation be named something other than A.A., "Alanon," for example. A later note in P-17 says that name is no longer appropriate because of possible confusion with Al-Anon Family Groups, "formed about five years after this was written" (P-17, "Clubs in A.A.", 1947, note).
  • 1957. A.A. Comes of Age: Anne (Dr. Bob's wife) and Lois (Bill's), in their homes and travels, had "urged A.A.'s Twelve Steps upon nonalcoholic wives and husbands as a way of restoring family life to normal" (A.A. Comes of Age, 1957), and Katie, the wife of a Chicago member, carried on that precedent. No one knows exactly when the first family group started; early ones in Toronto, Long Beach and Richmond were well established by 1950. At the 1955 convention Lois reported that family groups had grown from fewer than 100 in 1950 to nearly 700. In her words, the family groups "follow the lead of you in A.A." (A.A. Comes of Age, 1957), applying the same Steps to themselves.
  • 1980. Dr. Bob and the Good Oldtimers: "Al-Anon Family Groups took its present form in 1951" (Dr. Bob and the Good Oldtimers, 1980), and Alateen formed in 1957 as part of Al-Anon. Both use the A.A. program with slight adaptation "but are entirely separate from A.A." (Dr. Bob and the Good Oldtimers, 1980).

Today

  • The Big Book's Appendix VI, "How to Get in Touch With A.A.," suggests that a relative or friend of an alcoholic "who shows no immediate interest in A.A." (Appendix VI, p. 573) write to Al-Anon Family Groups, and gives its world service office.
  • The A.A. Group (P-16, 2018) calls Al-Anon "a fellowship entirely separate from A.A." (P-16, 2018). It reports a General Service Conference recommendation that "family" meetings not be listed in A.A. directories, partly because the word "family" might be confused with Al-Anon. It lists an A.A. Guideline, "Relationship Between A.A. and Al-Anon," which is not in our sources.

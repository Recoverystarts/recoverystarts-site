Concept I: who has the final say in A.A.?

In short:

  • Concept I says: "Final responsibility and ultimate authority for A.A. world services should always reside in the collective conscience of our whole Fellowship" (p. 574). In plain terms, the A.A. groups, together, have the last word over A.A.'s shared services, not the trustees or the General Service Office.
  • Bill W. wrote it in 1962. The groups had taken that authority over from the co-founders at the St. Louis convention in 1955. What made it urgent, Bill says, was the news in 1948 that Dr. Bob was fatally ill; he died in 1950.
  • Bill grounds it in Tradition Two, first printed in 1946: one ultimate authority, "a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience" (p. 563).
  • Concept I is about world services. It doesn't give anyone authority over a group's own affairs.

The earlier text first: Tradition Two (1946)

The Long Form of Tradition Two: "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience" (p. 563). Long Form Nine adds that the trustees are, "in effect, our A.A. General Service Committee" (p. 565), and that A.A.'s servants "derive no real authority from their titles; they do not govern" (p. 565).

In 1947 Bill described the trustees of the Alcoholic Foundation as "simple custodians" of the office, the Grapevine, A.A.'s funds and Traditions: "caretakers only" (P-17, Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?, 1947).

So in 1946–47 the principle was already stated. What hadn't been settled was how the groups, scattered across the world, could actually hold authority over the New York office and its board. Concept I tells how that was done.

What Bill's Concept I essay says (1962)

The claim. "The A.A. groups today hold ultimate responsibility and final authority for our world services" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I). World services are the things no single group or area can do: uniform literature, public relations, answering pleas for help, helping new groups form, a magazine, translations.

The history. In 1938 Bill and Dr. Bob, with nonalcoholic friends, formed a trusteeship, the Alcoholic Foundation, renamed the General Service Board in 1954. By 1950 membership had grown from about 50 to 100,000 and the Traditions had been written and adopted. Yet few members knew the trustees existed: "Not one member in a thousand could tell who our Trustees were" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I). The trustees controlled money, but on policy they turned to the two founders.

The turning point. In 1948 it became known that Dr. Bob was fatally ill. "The fact had to be faced that A.A.’s founders were perishable" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I). Many trustees and members opposed calling a representative conference, fearing politics, expense and strife; Bill says those arguments "were not without merit." But "For the first time it was seen that only a representative conference could take the place of Dr. Bob and me" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I). The first Conference met experimentally in 1951; "The groups assumed that responsibility at the St. Louis International Convention of 1955" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I), where Bill made the transfer on behalf of Dr. Bob, the trustees and the old-timers.

The reason in principle. Bill quotes Tradition Two in full and says its principles are "crystal clear: the A.A. groups are to be the final authority; their leaders are to be entrusted with delegated responsibilities only" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I). He notes "Tradition Two had been written in 1945" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I), but it took until 1955 for everyone to be convinced it applied to the trustees and founders as well: "It took five years more for all of us to be convinced that Tradition Two was for everybody" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I).

Group conscience. Bill admits the phrase is vague to many members, and contrasts A.A. with democracies where ignorance, apathy and power-seeking erode self-government. A.A.'s safeguard, he says, is that each life and group is built on the Steps and Traditions, and "the penalty for extensive disobedience to these principles is death for the individual and dissolution for the group" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I), together with the love members have for one another.

Not God's job alone. One detail cuts against an easy faith: in the boom years of 1945–50, many members thought nothing could happen to A.A. "because God was protecting A.A." (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I). Bill calls that attitude a strange contrast with how carefully members guarded their own sobriety, and says planning ahead was needed.

How the authority is actually used

Concept I gives the groups ultimate authority, and the next Concepts explain why they don't use it directly: they delegate the active work to the Conference (Concept II), give servants a Right of Decision (Concept III), and so on. See Who has the final say in A.A.? for how the pieces fit.

What later sources add

  • P-8 (first printed 1986; © 2017) opens Concept I with the line that A.A. "has been called an upside-down organization" (P-8). P-8 doesn't say who called it that; the phrase isn't in Bill's 1962 essay.
  • The current Service Manual (2024–2026) quotes Concept I to explain the GSR's role: the GSR helps the groups carry out that final responsibility.

What the text leaves open

The short form of Concept I names "collective conscience"; Tradition Two names "a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Bill's essay treats them as one principle and doesn't say what would happen if they were thought to differ.

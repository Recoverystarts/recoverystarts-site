What are the Twelve Concepts, and why were they written?

In short:

  • The Twelve Concepts for World Service are twelve statements, each with a long essay, written by Bill W. in 1962 about how A.A.'s world services are run: the groups, the General Service Conference, the trustees of the General Service Board, and the service corporations and their staffs. The 12th General Service Conference adopted them on April 26, 1962.
  • They are not about personal recovery or about how a group runs itself. The Big Book's Appendix VII puts it in one line each: the Steps are for personal recovery, the Traditions for unity, and the Concepts help keep the service structure "responsive and responsible to those they serve" (p. 574).
  • Bill says he wrote them to record the "why" of the service structure so its lessons would not be lost once the founders were gone. Dr. Bob had died in 1950; Bill was preparing to step back.
  • They are later than the Traditions (1946) and Dr. Bob's death. Where the 1946–47 texts already state a principle, the Concepts build on it, and these pages show the earlier text first.

When, and by whom

The Concepts' own title page says they were written "by Bill W." and adopted "by the 12th Annual General Service Conference of Alcoholics Anonymous on April 26, 1962" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, title page).

A 1985 preface from the General Service Office says they "were written by Bill W. in 1962" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Preface, 1985). Details went out of date, so the 1985 Conference asked that they be printed "in the original 1962 version, with required factual changes provided as numbered footnotes at the end of each chapter" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Preface, 1985). That is how our copy reads: Bill's 1962 text, with footnotes added as late as the 2024–2026 edition.

A one-sentence "short form" of each Concept came later. It was approved by the 1971 Conference and put in the Service Manual in 1974 (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Preface, 1985). The Big Book has printed that short form since the fourth edition (p. xii), as Appendix VII on pages 574–575, which says the Concepts were "Written by co-founder Bill W. in 1962" (p. 574).

Why Bill wrote them

To keep the experience. His introduction says: "These Concepts therefore aim to record the ‘why’ of our service structure" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Introduction). He expected later workers to change things, and warned that "change does not necessarily spell progress" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Introduction). If a change went wrong, the Concepts would be a way back to what had worked.

Not to freeze it. In the same introduction he rejects the idea that the Concepts should harden into gospel: "Nothing could stray further from the intent of these Concepts" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Introduction). A good enough case, convincing to both the trustees and the Conference, is all a change needs.

Because of power. He built them on "checks and balances" because alcoholics in charge tend to enlarge their own authority, and resist anyone else's. Of those traits he writes: "I’m the more sure of this because I possess these traits myself" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Introduction).

Because the founders would not last. Concept I tells the story. When Dr. Bob's fatal illness became known in 1948, it was clear that he and Bill were almost the only link between the little-known trustees and the groups. "The fact had to be faced that A.A.’s founders were perishable" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I). The answer was a representative Conference, tried from 1951 and made permanent at St. Louis in 1955. Concept IX says the Concepts themselves were one of the chores left over from St. Louis, written as Bill prepared to withdraw from nearly all world service work (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept IX).

Bill says each Concept is hard to state briefly: "Since each Concept is really a group of related principles" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Introduction), they could not be as short as the Steps and Traditions.

The earlier texts they rest on

The service structure didn't start in 1962. The Long Form of the Traditions (1946) already says "The trustees of the General Service Board are, in effect, our A.A. General Service Committee" (p. 565), that they look after the General Service Office and public relations, and that all such servants "derive no real authority from their titles; they do not govern" (p. 565). (The 1946 original named the trustees of the Alcoholic Foundation; the board was renamed the General Service Board in 1954, and the Big Book prints the new name.)

Bill's 1947 Grapevine essay on government says the same of the New York office: "we can merely supply certain indispensable services" (P-17, Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?, 1947). The trustees, he wrote then, are custodians, "caretakers only" (P-17, Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?, 1947).

The Concepts keep coming back to Tradition Two. Concept I quotes it in full, and Concept X ends by saying its whole account of ultimate and delegated authority is "fully implied" in Tradition Two (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X). So the earlier text is the root; the Concepts explain how Bill applied it to a Conference, a board and two corporations.

What the twelve cover

Each has its own page: - I. The groups hold the final authority over world services. Concept I: who has the final say in A.A.? - II. The groups delegated the active work to the Conference in 1955. Concept II - III. Trusted servants have a "Right of Decision". Concept III - IV. Each kind of servant gets a vote in proportion to responsibility. Concept IV - V. Minorities can appeal, and anyone can petition over a grievance. Concept V - VI. The trustees take the active lead, under the Conference. Concept VI - VII. The trustees hold legal power; the Conference holds practical power. Concept VII - VIII. The trustees oversee the service corporations rather than run them. Concept VIII - IX. Good leaders, chosen well, are indispensable. Concept IX - X. Authority must equal responsibility. Concept X - XI. The committees, directors and paid staff who work with the trustees. Concept XI - XII. The General Warranties of the Conference. Concept XII

The pamphlet P-8 (first printed 1986) sorts them this way: Concepts III through V, IX and XII deal with spiritual principles, and the rest mainly with how the service bodies relate (P-8). It also says plainly that "it is NOT the Concepts themselves" (P-8): for the real text, read the essays.

Reading them with care

The Concepts are Bill's work from 1962, twelve years after Dr. Bob died, about a structure begun in 1938 but reshaped after 1950, when the Conference was added (1951–55). That doesn't make them wrong, but they are later than the Big Book's text and the 1946–47 Traditions, and they describe a specific office in a specific year. Many details (the number of directors, the money, the committees) have changed, and the footnotes say so.

One change is worth knowing: a footnote to Concept XI says that, by an Advisory Action of the 66th General Service Conference, two paragraphs were removed from the published version of Concept XI, and are available on request from GSO's Archives (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept XI, footnote 10). Our copy doesn't say what they contained. (Counting from the first Conference in 1951, the 66th met in 2016.)

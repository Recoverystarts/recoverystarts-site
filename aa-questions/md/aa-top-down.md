# Is A.A. run from the top down?

**In short:**
- Not over its groups and members. In Bill W.'s 1947 words, "Nowhere in A.A. is there to be seen any constituted human authority that can compel an A.A. group to do anything" (P-17, Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?, 1947). The Long Form of the Traditions (1946) says A.A.'s servants "do not govern" (p. 565).
- For A.A.'s shared world services, authority runs from the groups. Concept I (1962) puts final authority in the groups; Concept II says they delegate the active work to the General Service Conference. Later literature calls this the "upside-down" structure.
- Inside the service offices, though, the 1962 Concepts describe ordinary delegated management on purpose: the Conference over the trustees, the trustees over the service corporations, directors over executives, executives over staff. Bill chose the corporate form deliberately.
- None of that reaches down into a group. The Conference is bound never to "perform any acts of government" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept XII).

## The earliest texts (1946–47)

**Long Form Nine (1946).** "Each A.A. group needs the least possible organization" (p. 565). Groups elect a secretary or a rotating committee; the trustees act as A.A.'s General Service Committee. All such representatives "derive no real authority from their titles; they do not govern" (p. 565).

**Bill's 1946 essay on money.** Of clubs, local offices and the New York office: "These are places of service; they cannot really control or govern A.A." (P-17, Money, 1946). If one became overbearing, members could simply stop paying for it: "These services must always serve us well or go out of business" (P-17, Money, 1946).

**Bill's 1947 essay on government.** It lists what A.A. lacks: "no fees, no dues, no rules and regulations, no demand that alcoholics conform to A.A. principles, no one set in personal authority over anyone else" (P-17, Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?, 1947). Group committees "have only the authorization to serve, never to command" (P-17, Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?, 1947). Even at A.A.'s headquarters, he says, "there can only exist a center of service" (P-17, Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?, 1947), with no authority to govern.

## World services: from the groups (Concepts I and II, 1962)

Bill's Concepts put the final authority over world services at the base. "The A.A. groups today hold ultimate responsibility and final authority for our world services" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept I). In Concept II: "The operation is really theirs; they really own it" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept II).

The groups can't run a world service office themselves, so they delegate. Each group chooses a general service representative; the representatives choose area committee members and a delegate; the delegates sit in the Conference with the trustees and staff. Bill calls the GSRs, committee members and delegates "the direct agents of the A.A. groups" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept IX).

The "upside-down" image is not in Bill's 1962 essays. It comes from later pamphlets: P-8 (first printed 1986) says A.A. "has been called an upside-down organization" (P-8) because final authority rests with the groups, and the current Service Manual (2024–2026) draws it as the "upside-down triangle," with the groups on top (The A.A. Service Manual, 2024–2026, Welcome to General Service).

## Inside the service offices: a chain, by design

Bill's own picture in 1962 is a business corporation, and he uses the words top and bottom for it: "From top to bottom, our whole service structure indeed resembles that of a large corporation" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept VI). "The A.A. groups are the stockholders" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept VI), the delegates their proxies, the trustees the directors of a holding company that owns the service corporations.

Concept X spells out a line of ultimate authority: the delegates over the trustees, the trustees over A.A. World Services and the Grapevine, their directors over their officers, the officers over their staffs. "The principle of ultimate authority runs clear through our structure" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X). Concept XI adds that day-to-day executive direction "must always head up in one person" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept XI).

So the honest answer has two parts. Inside the service corporations there is an ordinary chain of management, and Bill wanted it that way. Above them, the chain points back to the groups. And the Concepts limit how anyone uses authority:

- Ultimate authority "should practically never be used in full, except in an emergency" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X).
- Every level gets a "Right of Decision" (Concept III), a vote in proportion to its responsibility (Concept IV), and minorities get a right of appeal (Concept V).
- No Conference member may be placed "in a position of unqualified authority over any of the others" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept XII).

## What the structure can't do to a group

Concept XII is the Conference's own bond with A.A. It "shall never perform any acts of government" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept XII). Bill lists the liberties it must not touch: no penalties for nonconformity, no fees or dues, no expulsion, each group running its internal affairs as it wishes. When Tradition questions come up, "A.A.’s World Headquarters is not a police operation" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept XII); the most it can usually do is offer information or mediation.

P-16, *The A.A. Group* (© 2018), says the same in plain words: "A.A. has no central authority, minimal organization, and a handful of Traditions instead of laws" (P-16).

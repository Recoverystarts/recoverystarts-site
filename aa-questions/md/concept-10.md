# Concept X: why must service authority equal service responsibility?

**In short:**
- Concept X says: "Every service responsibility should be matched by an equal service authority, with the scope of such authority well defined" (p. 575). The long form adds that the scope can be defined "by tradition, by resolution, by specific job description or by appropriate charters and bylaws."
- Bill W.'s reason, written in 1962: if people are given a job without the authority to do it, or authority without a defined limit, "personal clashes, confusion, and ineffectiveness will be inevitable" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X).
- Final authority must sit somewhere at each level, but should "practically never be used in full, except in an emergency" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X). And no job should have two equal bosses: "Authority can never be divided into equal halves" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X).
- Bill calls it all implied in Tradition Two: the group conscience as the ultimate authority, the trusted servant as the delegated one. "One cannot function without the other" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X).

## The earlier texts first (1946–47)

Tradition Two (1946) holds both halves already: one ultimate authority expressed in group conscience (p. 563), and leaders who are "but trusted servants" (p. 562). Long Form Nine (1946) gives servants their role and its limit in one line: they "derive no real authority from their titles; they do not govern" (p. 565).

Bill's 1947 essay describes group committees that "have only the authorization to serve, never to command" (P-17, Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?, 1947). Concept X is about the service bodies, not groups, and adds what 1947 didn't spell out: a servant needs enough authority to actually do the job.

## What Bill's Concept X essay says (1962)

**The principle.** Most societies and governments of the day, Bill says, break the rule that each responsibility needs a matching authority. The earlier Concepts were written to define the authority and responsibility of the groups, the Conference, the trustees and the service corporations so that they match at every level.

**Four conditions.** Things go wrong unless authority and responsibility are well defined; those with final authority are able and willing to delegate; those with delegated authority feel free to use it as trusted servants; and there is some definite way to settle doubtful cases.

**A line of final authority.** "The principle of ultimate authority runs clear through our structure" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X): groups over the Conference, delegates over the trustees, trustees over the service corporations, directors over officers, officers over staff. Everything has to "head up somewhere" so each worker knows who the final boss is.

**But not used in full.** Without delegated authority, Bill pictures groups directing delegates on every vote, delegates turning trustees into "a timid committee," trustees running the corporations by directive, and executives becoming "small czars." The proper uses of final authority are emergencies: groups can elect better delegates or withhold funds; delegates can censure or reorganize the trustees; trustees can do the same with the corporations; corporations can fire executives.

**How authority is defined.** By legal means (corporate charters), by traditional means, and by principles for settling doubtful cases. The Conference Charter "is not a legal instrument, but practically speaking it is the substance of a contract between the A.A. groups and their Conference" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X). Delegates vote their conscience and the Conference decides what to refer back to the groups; the trustees insist on their legal right to administer, with a veto in rare cases; the corporations' charters and non-trustee directors protect their operating authority.

**Conflicts.** Interlocking memberships between committees and corporations settle most routine conflicts. For the rest, there must be a senior jurisdiction, the junior one must be heard, and there must always be a known point of final decision.

**No double-headed management.** "A condition to be avoided at all costs is double-headed business or policy management" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X). Bill takes this up again under Concept XI.

**Two more safeguards.** The Rights of Appeal and Petition, because "a bare majority is apt to constitute itself as a pseudo-ultimate authority" and executives "are apt to over-boss their assistants."

**Summed up.** Be sure there is plenty of final authority to correct or reorganize, and equally sure every trusted servant has a clearly defined and adequate authority to do the daily work.

## Later changes (footnote, 2024–2026 Service Manual)

The General Policy Committee Bill describes is now the General Sharing Session, meeting three times a year for about two hours; its functions "have changed markedly" (Twelve Concepts for World Service, Concept X, footnote 1).

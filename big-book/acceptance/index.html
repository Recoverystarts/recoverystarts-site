Acceptance Was the Answer: The Big Book's Page 417 Passage

What the most-quoted passage in the Big Book teaches about serenity, and where acceptance ends and action begins

The passage almost everyone is looking for sits on page 417 of Alcoholics Anonymous, 4th Edition, in a personal story called Acceptance Was the Answer. The writer, a physician who was addicted to both alcohol and pills, opens the most-quoted paragraph in the book by telling us that 'acceptance is the answer to all my problems today' (page 417). When he is disturbed, he says, it is because he finds 'some person, place, thing, or situation' unacceptable to him, and he can find no serenity until he accepts that person, place, thing, or situation as being 'exactly the way it is supposed to be at this moment' (page 417).

If the page number looks wrong to you, you may be remembering an older printing. In the 3rd Edition this same story was titled Doctor, Alcoholic, Addict, and the acceptance passage fell on page 449. When the 4th Edition came out in 2001, the story was renamed and the book repaged, so the words we heard quoted as 'page 449' now live on page 417.

What the Passage Actually Says

The paragraph makes a claim big enough to argue with: 'Nothing, absolutely nothing, happens in God's world by mistake' (page 417). The doctor is not saying that everything that happens is good. He is saying that serenity begins when we stop demanding that reality be different than it is right now. He tells us he had to accept his own alcoholism before he could ever stay sober, and that 'unless I accept life completely on life's terms, I cannot be happy' (page 417). Then he turns the lens all the way around: he needed to concentrate less on what needed changing in the world and more on what needed changing in himself and his attitudes (page 417). That is the hinge of the whole passage. His hard experience distills into one line many of us keep taped to the mirror: 'When I stopped living in the problem and began living in the answer, the problem went away' (page 417).

What Acceptance Does Not Mean

Acceptance is not approval. It does not mean we like what happened, that we excuse cruelty, or that we stay quietly in situations that harm us. It means we stop burning our strength in a war with facts. The basic text asks the same surrender of us about our drinking: 'We learned that we had to fully concede to our innermost selves that we were alcoholics' (page 30). We could not recover from a condition we refused to admit we had. The same spirit shows up in the promises that follow Step Ten: 'And we have ceased fighting anything or anyone—even alcohol' (page 84). Ceasing to fight is not quitting on life. It is how we finally join it.

Neither is acceptance a permission slip for passivity. The same book that gives us page 417 sends us out the door to make our amends (page 76) and to sweep off our side of the street (page 77), and it tells us plainly that 'Helping others is the foundation stone of your recovery' (page 97). Acceptance is where action becomes possible. We accept what we cannot change so that we have the strength to change what we can, starting with ourselves.

A Story, Not a Set of Instructions

It helps to know where these words live. Page 417 sits in the personal stories section of the Big Book, not in the first 164 pages that lay out the program of recovery. That does not make the passage less true. It makes it testimony. A physician is telling us what actually kept him sober, one day at a time. The stories exist to show us the principles walking around in real lives, and this one is quoted by page number in meetings all over the world. The basic text and the story agree completely: we admit what is, we turn over what we cannot manage, and we get to work on what we can.

Living Page 417 Today

So how do we use it? The next time we are disturbed, we can try the doctor's inventory. What person, place, thing, or situation are we refusing to accept? Then comes a second question: what part of this is actually ours to change? Usually the honest answer is our own attitude and our own next action, exactly as page 417 suggests. The Big Book reminds us that what we really have is 'a daily reprieve contingent on the maintenance of our spiritual condition' (page 85), and acceptance is part of each day's maintenance. We do not have to accept forever, only for today. We do not have to like it, only to stop fighting it. Serenity waits on the other side of that surrender.

— Alcoholics Anonymous (Big Book), 4th Edition: pages 30, 76, 77, 84, 85, 97, 417; story 'Acceptance Was the Answer' (Part II, They Stopped in Time). 3rd Edition: same story as 'Doctor, Alcoholic, Addict', acceptance passage on page 449.

Quick answers

What page is 'acceptance is the answer' on in the Big Book?

It is on page 417 of the 4th Edition of Alcoholics Anonymous, in the personal story Acceptance Was the Answer. If you learned it as page 449, you are remembering the 3rd Edition, where the same story was titled Doctor, Alcoholic, Addict.

Who wrote the story Acceptance Was the Answer?

A physician member of AA whose story appears in the personal stories section of the 4th Edition. Because AA members remain anonymous in print, the book does not name its story authors; he describes himself as a physician who was addicted to both alcohol and pills before he found sobriety in the fellowship.

Does acceptance mean I have to approve of bad things that happen?

No. Acceptance in the Big Book sense means acknowledging reality as it actually is so we can stop fighting it and find serenity (page 417). It is not approval, resignation, or a reason to stay in harmful situations; the same passage points us toward changing ourselves and our attitudes, and the rest of the book calls us to real action, like making amends (page 76).

Is the acceptance passage part of the AA program itself?

It comes from a member's personal story, not from the first 164 pages that describe the program of recovery. But it echoes the basic text, which asks us to fully concede our alcoholism to our innermost selves (page 30) and promises that we will cease fighting anything or anyone (page 84).

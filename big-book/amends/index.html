Making Amends in the Big Book: Steps 8 and 9

What pages 76 to 84 ask of us, and what they promise in return

The Big Book covers making amends on pages 76 through 84, in the chapter called Into Action. Step Eight asks us to list everyone we have harmed and become willing to make amends to them all. Step Nine asks us to make 'direct amends to such people wherever possible, except when to do so would injure them or others' (page 59). The book spends barely a paragraph on Step Eight, and the reason is practical: we already made the list when we took our inventory (page 76). What remains is willingness, and the book tells us that if we do not have it yet, we keep asking until it comes.

Why do we do it at all? Page 76 opens this stretch of the book by reminding us that 'Faith without works is dead.' The spiritual experience we found in the earlier steps has to be carried into our relationships or it withers. Page 77 gives the purpose in a single sentence: 'Our real purpose is to fit ourselves to be of maximum service to God and the people about us.' We are not making amends mainly to feel better or to be forgiven. We are sweeping away the wreckage of self-will so we can be useful again.

What Direct Amends Actually Look Like

Direct means we go to the person, face to face wherever possible, rather than sending a vague apology from a safe distance. The book walks through real situations. We approach the person we have resented or wronged 'in a helpful and forgiving spirit' (page 77), keeping the focus on our own side of the street; his faults are not our business that day. With creditors, page 78 says we do not dodge them. We let them know we are sorry and arrange the best repayment we can. Even wrongs that could cost us our reputation or worse are on the table. Page 79 sets the standard plainly: 'We must not shrink at anything.' We ask God for strength and direction to do the right thing, whatever the personal consequences may be.

The Except-When Clause

Step Nine carries one careful exception: we make amends except when doing so would injure the person or somebody else. Notice what the clause does not say. It does not say except when it is embarrassing, expensive, or frightening for us. The injury in question is theirs, never ours. Page 79 warns that we are not to be 'the hasty and foolish martyr' who needlessly sacrifices others to save himself. Buying our own peace of mind at another person's expense is not an amend; it is one more act of selfishness wearing Step Nine clothing. The book's examples on pages 80 and 81 involve confessions that would wound a spouse or pull innocent people into harm. In those cases we seek guidance first, talking things over with others and asking God for direction before taking any drastic step (page 80). The willingness stays total. The method stays considerate.

Amends Are Repairs, Not Apologies

Page 82 gives the most vivid picture in the book of why an apology is not enough: 'The alcoholic is like a tornado roaring his way through the lives of others.' A tornado cannot fix a town by saying sorry to it. Speaking of the family, page 83 is blunt: 'A remorseful mumbling that we are sorry won't fill the bill at all.' What fills the bill is repair. We pay what we owe, we tell the truth we withheld, and above all we live differently at home, day after day, so the people closest to us experience the change instead of just hearing about it. The book is also honest that some wrongs can never be fully righted. Page 83 says we do not worry about those if we can honestly say we would right them if we could. Willingness is the coin of this step.

What the Book Promises

Right in the middle of the Step Nine instructions, the book pauses to tell us what happens if we do this work thoroughly. 'If we are painstaking about this phase of our development, we will be amazed before we are half way through' (page 83). Then come the Ninth Step Promises: 'We are going to know a new freedom and a new happiness. We will not regret the past nor wish to shut the door on it' (page 83). Fear of people, self-pity, and the old feeling of uselessness begin to slip away as the amends get made. Page 84 closes the passage with the assurance generations of us have tested: 'They will always materialize if we work for them.' We do not have to finish the list this week. We simply have to start, stay willing, and keep walking toward the next name.

— Alcoholics Anonymous (Big Book), 4th Edition — Step 9 wording from chapter "How It Works," page 59; amends passage from chapter "Into Action," pages 76-84

Quick answers

What pages in the Big Book cover making amends?

Pages 76 through 84 in the chapter Into Action. Step Eight gets a single paragraph on page 76, because the list of people we harmed was already made during the Step Four inventory. The rest of the passage walks through how to make Step Nine amends, and it closes with the Ninth Step Promises on pages 83 and 84.

What is the difference between making amends and apologizing?

An apology is words; an amend is repair. The Big Book says a remorseful mumbling that we are sorry will not fill the bill (page 83). Making amends means paying the debts (page 78), telling the truth, and living differently so the people we harmed experience the change rather than just hearing about it.

What does 'except when to do so would injure them or others' mean in Step 9?

It means we hold back or adjust an amend only when making it would hurt the other person or an innocent third party, not when it is merely uncomfortable for us. Page 79 warns against being the hasty and foolish martyr who sacrifices others to ease his own conscience, and page 80 counsels seeking guidance from others and from God before any drastic disclosure.

Do I have to make all my amends at once?

No. The Big Book acknowledges that some wrongs can never be fully righted, and says we need not worry about them if we can honestly say we would right them if we could (page 83). We take the list as opportunities come, sensibly and considerately, and page 84 promises the results always materialize if we work for them.

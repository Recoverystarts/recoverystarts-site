From the Big Book

What Does the Big Book Say About Resentment?

The number one offender, the inventory that names it, and the freedom waiting on page 67.

When someone asks what the Big Book says about resentment, the answer starts on page 64, and it does not mince words. The book calls resentment the 'number one' offender and says it 'destroys more alcoholics than anything else' (page 64). That is a strong claim. Not fear, not money troubles, not even the drink itself, but resentment sits at the top of the list. The reason follows in the same passage: 'From it stem all forms of spiritual disease, for we have been not only mentally and physically ill, we have been spiritually sick' (page 64). For people like us, whose recovery depends on a spiritual experience, resentment is not just a bad mood. It is a threat to our sobriety.

The good news is that the Big Book does not stop at the diagnosis. Pages 64 through 67 lay out a practical method for dealing with it: a written inventory that sets our resentments on paper, a new way of seeing the people who hurt us, and a turn toward our own part in the story. Page 66 sums up why the work matters: 'It is plain that a life which includes deep resentment leads only to futility and unhappiness.' We do this work to get free.

Why Resentment Is the Number One Offender

Page 66 explains the danger in words many of us have underlined. When we harbor deep resentment, 'we shut ourselves off from the sunlight of the Spirit' (page 66). Other people may be able to afford a grudge; the book says these things may be 'the dubious luxury of normal men, but for alcoholics these things are poison' (page 66). When we are spiritually blocked, the insanity of alcohol returns, and for us that is a matter of life and death. That is why the book is so blunt: 'If we were to live, we had to be free of anger' (page 66). This is not a moral lecture. It is a survival instruction.

The Inventory: Setting Resentments on Paper

The method begins simply. 'In dealing with resentments, we set them on paper. We listed people, institutions or principles with whom we were angry' (page 64). That is the first column: who or what we resent. In the second column we wrote the cause, the specific thing that happened. In the third, the book has us ask what part of us was affected, and page 65 shows a sample grid where each resentment touches our self-esteem, our security, our ambitions, or our personal and sex relations. Seeing our anger laid out this way, most of us notice something humbling. The list turns out to be a map of the places we feel threatened.

Then comes the turn that changes everything. 'Putting out of our minds the wrongs others had done, we resolutely looked for our own mistakes' (page 67). The book gives us searching questions: 'Where had we been selfish, dishonest, self-seeking and frightened?' (page 67). This is not about excusing anyone who harmed us. It is about finding the one part of the story we can actually do something about, which is our part.

How the Book Says We Get Free

Before we ever reach our own mistakes, the Big Book hands us a new pair of glasses. It suggests we regard the people who wronged us as perhaps spiritually sick, just as we were (pages 66-67). Then it gives us a prayer: we asked God to help us show them 'the same tolerance, pity, and patience that we would cheerfully grant a sick friend' (page 67). When someone offends us, the book even gives us words to say: 'This is a sick man. How can I be helpful to him? God save me from being angry. Thy will be done' (page 67). We avoid retaliation or argument, the book adds, because we would not treat sick people that way (page 67). None of this means what happened to us was okay. It means we stop drinking the poison ourselves.

The Freedom on the Other Side

What waits past this work is the whole point. The resentment inventory carries us into Steps Five through Nine, and the promises on that road are specific: 'We will not regret the past nor wish to shut the door on it' (page 83). The Big Book's teaching on resentment comes down to this: we cannot afford it, we can write our way out of it, and God can remove what we could never argue ourselves out of. Many of us walked into the Fourth Step certain our anger was justified, and walked out seeing that justified or not, it was killing us. Letting it go was not weakness. It was the door to a life where yesterday's injuries no longer decide today's peace.

— Alcoholics Anonymous (Big Book), 4th Edition: 'How It Works,' pages 64-67 (resentment as the number one offender, the resentment inventory, the spiritually sick perspective, the turn to our own mistakes); 'Into Action,' page 83 (the promise of not regretting the past).

Quick answers

What page does the Big Book call resentment the number one offender?

Page 64, in the chapter How It Works. The Big Book says resentment is the 'number one' offender and that it destroys more alcoholics than anything else (page 64). The full discussion of resentment, including the written inventory, runs from page 64 through page 67.

What are the columns in the Big Book resentment inventory?

The instructions begin on page 64, and page 65 shows a written grid. The first column lists the people, institutions, or principles we are angry with, the second lists the cause, and the third asks which part of self was affected, such as self-esteem, security, ambitions, or personal and sex relations. Then on page 67 the book turns the inventory around and asks where we had been selfish, dishonest, self-seeking, and frightened.

How does the Big Book say to get rid of resentment?

It suggests we regard the people who wronged us as spiritually sick, just as we were (pages 66-67). We ask God to help us show them the same tolerance, pity, and patience we would grant a sick friend (page 67), we avoid retaliation or argument, and we look for our own mistakes instead of dwelling on the wrongs of others (page 67). Freedom comes from prayer and honest inventory, not from winning the argument.

Why is resentment so dangerous for alcoholics?

Because it blocks the spiritual experience that keeps us sober. Page 66 says that when we harbor deep resentment we shut ourselves off from the sunlight of the Spirit, and that anger may be a luxury other people can afford but for alcoholics it is poison. The stakes for us are sobriety itself, which is why the book says that if we were to live, we had to be free of anger (page 66).

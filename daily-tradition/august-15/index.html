Tradition 8 · August 15

A Billing Code

Daily Traditions · August 15

How it gets captured

There is now a version of the conversation that is saving your life that can be invoiced. Not inside A.A. — outside it, where the hour one sober alcoholic spends with a struggling one has been given a code and a rate, so that somewhere a third party can be billed for it. Watch what the mechanism does, because it needs no villain. The moment the hour is billable, the payer needs to know what happened in it, so the hour acquires paperwork. The paperwork needs goals, so the hour acquires a curriculum. The rate needs justifying, so the hour acquires outcomes — and the outcomes belong to whoever pays, which is never the drunk. Nothing about the two people in the room has changed, and everything about the hour has: it answers upward now. A.A. put its refusal of all this into one line of P-43, its pamphlet on the Traditions: "The meaning of Twelfth Step work cannot be measured in money" — because what actually changes hands in that hour is "of far greater worth — our very lives." A price does not simply pay for the hour. It measures it, and a measured hour ends up serving whoever holds the ruler. The free hour serves the man who needs it, because it cannot serve anything else.

Sit with

The billed hour answers to whoever pays for it; the free hour cannot answer to anything but the drunk in front of it. Which of my hours answer upward?

Grounded in: P-43 (the meaning of Twelfth Step work); long form, Tradition 8.

Tradition 8

"Alcoholics Anonymous should remain forever nonprofessional, but our service centers may employ special workers."
Read the Long Form (pp. 563–566). The short form on pp. 561–562 is the one everybody quotes — but the Long Form is where the Traditions actually say what they mean. Tradition 3's "no other affiliation" clause, for instance, exists only in the Long Form. That single clause is why no treatment centre can own an A.A. group. Most of what circulates online skips it.

And a distinction worth keeping straight: the Traditions are governance, not theology. They bind A.A. groups and the Fellowship — not individuals, and not outside businesses. They were adopted in 1950 to keep A.A. from being owned or co-opted. They are not a rulebook for your personal life.

Half a Million

A way out for half a million alcoholics died because it took sides in public fights, and the first thing it lost was the drunk. Bill W. told the story of the Washingtonians, a movement a century older than A.A. (A.A. Comes of Age): "At first the society was composed entirely of alcoholics trying to help one another." Their membership "passed the five hundred thousand mark." Then they "permitted politicians and reformers, both alcoholic and nonalcoholic, to use the society for their own purposes." Speakers took sides on slavery. Some became temperance crusaders, out to dry up the whole country. "Within a very few years they had completely lost their effectiveness in helping alcoholics, and the society collapsed." That is why the long form of Tradition 10 names "alcohol reform" by name (Big Book, p. 565). A drinker who hears that the rooms are for or against his party, his church or his bar has one more reason never to walk in. Today the pull comes as a campaign: a drinking law, a funding fight, and somebody speaking for "the recovery community" as if the people in the rooms had signed on. Test it tonight: ask whether your meeting's name appears anywhere outside the room, on anybody's side.

Sit with

Half a million sober alcoholics lost their power to help the next drunk once their society took sides in public. Is there a drinker who would stay out of my room because of a side it has taken?

Tradition 10 · Short form

"Alcoholics Anonymous has no opinion on outside issues; hence the A.A. name ought never be drawn into public controversy."
Short form: pp. 561–562. Long form: pp. 563–566. If you only ever read one, read the long one.

Why these exist

The Twelve Traditions were adopted in 1950 for one reason: to stop A.A. from being owned. Not owned by a benefactor, not by a treatment centre, not by whoever happened to be paying the rent. They are the Fellowship's constitution — written out of near-misses A.A. actually lived through.

Most of what circulates online quotes only the short form (pp. 561–562) and quietly drops the rest. So we front-load the Long Form, pp. 563–566 — where the Traditions actually say what they mean. Tradition 3's clause that a group is A.A. only if "as a group, they have no other affiliation"? That exists only in the Long Form. It's the clause that says no treatment centre can own an A.A. group. It's also the clause the internet forgot.

One more thing, because it gets blurred constantly: the Traditions are governance, not theology. They bind A.A. groups and the Fellowship — not individuals. They are not a rulebook for your personal life.

How August reads. The month opens day-by-day — one angle on the Tradition at a time, where groups go wrong with it and the reason it was written — then settles into five questions, run straight through: what specifically threatened A.A. · how the founders solved it before there was a Tradition · where that same danger lives now · how a group drifts off it on its own · how it gets hollowed out from outside. Anything inside quotation marks is A.A.'s own wording, printed exactly, with the source named at the foot of the reading. The explaining around it is ours — plain modern English, and scenes drawn to show how the Tradition actually plays out. You should always be able to tell which is which.

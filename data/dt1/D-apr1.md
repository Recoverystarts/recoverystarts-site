### April 1 — "But Government, No" · *(the threat)*
Tradition Four answers the oldest threat to a group: somebody outside it deciding how it should be run, and one of the first to try was A.A.'s own co-founder. Bill W. wanted to manage the whole movement from New York, and he told what happened (A.A. Comes of Age): "But I soon found that I could do no such thing. Neither could anybody else at A.A.'s Headquarters." The groups answered him plainly: "We are not going to have a personal government in New York, or anywhere else. Services, yes. But government, no." The Long Form wrote their answer down (Big Book, p. 563): "With respect to its own affairs, each A.A. group should be responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." Its own affairs means its own business: when and where it meets, how the meeting runs, who serves, what the basket pays for. No other authority means none at all: not the founder, not the office in New York, not the landlord. If the man who helped start A.A. couldn't run a group from outside, nobody can. Today the outside manager is usually the building: a facility that sets the meeting's time, format and chair, and can end it when its program changes. Test it tonight: ask who decided how your meeting runs, and when the members last voted on it.
*Sit with:* The groups told A.A.'s own co-founder he could serve them but not govern them. Who outside my group decides how it runs?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Four: "Services, yes. But government, no.").

### April 2 — "That Powerful Minority" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was a Tradition Four, eighteen drunks in a house in Akron already knew that a group settles its own business by vote, and that the losing side can be right. Bill W. and Dr. Bob brought them big plans: paid missionaries, A.A. hospitals, a book. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "The moment we were through, those alcoholics really did work us over!" They argued it out, and then it went to a vote: "By the barest majority, and over the most strenuous objections, the Akron meeting finally decided that we ought to go for the whole works". The founders didn't decide for them; the room did. And Bill later admitted the losers had seen further: "In later years, I came to be very thankful for that powerful minority." Big business and paid missionaries, he wrote, would have destroyed A.A. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) makes that room's way the rule for every group: each is "responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." A group's conscience is its members talking it through and voting, with the minority heard. Today a meeting can run for years without one vote, because its decisions were made somewhere else before anyone sat down. Test it tonight: ask when your group last voted on anything, and who spoke against it.
*Sit with:* Eighteen Akron drunks argued and voted, and the founders lived with the result. When did my group last decide anything by a vote?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (the Akron meeting's vote on missionaries, hospitals and the book).

### April 3 — "No Other Authority" · *(the threat today)*
Tradition Four is about who runs a group's business, and the rumour that it means what happens in this meeting stays in this meeting hides exactly that. Read the Long Form before anyone explains it to you (Big Book, p. 563): "With respect to its own affairs, each A.A. group should be responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." There is nothing in it about secrets. A group's own affairs are its business: the time, the place, the format, the money, who serves and for how long. The Big Book's own history of the Traditions says it in five words (Big Book, p. xix): "each group was to be autonomous". Autonomous means self-governing: the group makes its own decisions and answers for them. Whatever a room decides about privacy, that is not what this sentence is about. When Four is taught as a rule about keeping quiet, a room learns to keep quiet and never learns that it can vote. Then whoever actually runs the meeting, a facility, its staff or one strong old-timer, never has to face a group conscience at all. Today the slogan is read aloud at the close of meetings that have never once held a business meeting. Test it tonight: ask someone in your room what Tradition Four says, then show them page 563.
*Sit with:* Tradition Four gives each group the power to run its own business; it is not a rule about keeping quiet. Does my group know it can vote?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); Foreword to the Second Edition (Big Book, p. xix).

### April 4 — "Nobody Was Asked" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can lose Tradition Four without anyone taking it, simply by never using it. Picture a Tuesday night meeting that has run the same way for six years. Same opening, same readings, same man at the front, because nobody else put a hand up. Nobody remembers a business meeting. When the coffee money runs short he covers it, and when the format needs changing he changes it. Nobody objects, and nobody was asked. The Long Form gives the group the decision, and the group has stopped making it. A conscience that never meets can't decide anything, so the decisions slide to whoever is willing to make them. Bill W. described how groups were meant to work (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 39): "They prefer rotating committees to handle their simple affairs. These committees are invariably regarded as servants — they have only the authorization to serve, never to command. Each committee carries out what it believes to be the wishes of its group." Rotating means the job passes on. The wishes of the group: someone has to ask the group for them. Today this quiet drift is the most common way a free room ends up run by one person or by one facility, because the members never claimed what was theirs. Test it tonight: ask when your group last held a business meeting, and when anyone last rotated out.
*Sit with:* A group conscience that never meets hands its decisions to whoever is willing to make them. Who makes my group's decisions now?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 39 (Bill W., 1947).

### April 5 — "The Sheet on the Table" · *(how it gets captured)*
When a facility hands a meeting its rules, the group has a boss, and Tradition Four says a group has none. It happens politely. A sheet is taped to the table: start and end times, no crosstalk, clients sign in, staff choose the chair, no meeting over the holidays. Some of it may even be sensible. But the members never voted on any of it, and none of them can change it. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "With respect to its own affairs, each A.A. group should be responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." Its own conscience, not the building's policy manual. Bill W. wrote in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 39): "Nowhere in A.A. is there to be seen any constituted human authority that can compel an A.A. group to do anything." Constituted means officially set up, with power. A.A. has no such authority anywhere, not even at its own headquarters. A facility that writes a meeting's rules has become exactly that authority, from outside. A room that can't change its own format isn't running its own affairs; it is being run. Test it tonight: find any rule read out or posted at your meeting, and ask when the group voted for it.
*Sit with:* A.A. never set up any authority that could compel a group, so no building should become one. Which of my meeting's rules did the members never vote on?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 39 (Bill W., 1947).

### April 6 — "The Letter and the Spirit" · *(the threat)*
The threat that runs the other way from chaos is hardening: a group, or a whole fellowship, deciding there is only one right way to run a meeting and making it law. Bill W. saw it coming (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 6): "Should we ever harden too much, the letter might crush that spirit. We could victimize ourselves by petty rules and prohibitions; we could imagine that we had said the last word." The letter means the written rule; the spirit is what the rule was for. Tradition Four is the guard against it. Because the Long Form makes each group answer to its own conscience on its own affairs, no group can be forced into another group's format, and no outside body can hand down the approved one. A.A. stays alive because each group can try something, keep what works, and drop what doesn't. Freeze every group into one script and nobody can find a better way again. Today the hardening usually arrives from outside, as a standard format a program uses in all its meetings, read word for word everywhere, with a warning not to change it. Inside, it can look like loyalty: the way we have always done it. Test it tonight: ask whether your group could change one thing about its meeting next week, and who would have to agree.
*Sit with:* Bill warned that the letter of a rule could crush the spirit it was written for. Could my group change its own format if it wanted to?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 6 (Bill W.'s foreword, 1955).

### April 7 — "A Town-Meeting Democracy" · *(before the tradition)*
Before anyone wrote Tradition Four, the groups had already found out that a room of drunks has to govern itself, and that it does it by vote. Bill W. told how it happened (A.A. Comes of Age). First came freedom: "We cannot be compelled to do anything. In that sense this society is a benign anarchy." Anarchy means no government; benign means kindly. Then the groups had to act: find a room, pay the rent, open the door on time. Freedom alone didn't get that done. "As the old-timers retired, we began to elect our servants by majority vote. Each group in this sense became a town-meeting democracy." A town meeting is where the people who live there show up, argue and vote, and nobody above them decides. That is what a group conscience looks like in practice: the members in a room, with a motion and a show of hands. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) put it in one sentence: each group is responsible "to no other authority than its own conscience." The old-timers stepping back made room for it. Today the old-timers are often replaced by staff, who never retire from the room, because the room is their job. Test it tonight: ask who your group elected to serve it, and when.
*Sit with:* The groups learned to govern themselves like a town meeting, by electing their servants. Who did my group elect, and could it elect someone else?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (benign anarchy and town-meeting democracy).

### April 8 — "Those Groups Ought to Be Consulted" · *(the threat today)*
Tradition Four gives a group the run of its own affairs and, in the next sentence, draws the edge of them. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "But when its plans concern the welfare of neighboring groups also, those groups ought to be consulted." Neighboring groups are the other A.A. groups in your town. Consulted means asked before, not told after. The rumour says autonomy means a group can do anything it likes. The Book says: anything with its own affairs, and when its plans reach into other groups' affairs, it asks them. Bill W. described how areas learned this (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 23): "Every area, sooner or later, realizes that such concerns as intergroup meetings, hospital arrangements, local public relations, a central office for interviews and information, are things in which every A.A. is interested". An intergroup is a committee the groups of an area set up to handle what they share. Today the edge gets lost when a group moves its hour onto a neighbor's, or opens a meeting across the street from one that is struggling, and nobody calls. And where a program's meetings fill the town, there may be no free groups left nearby to ask. Test it tonight: ask whether your group knows the other groups in your town, and whether anyone asked them before your group's last big change.
*Sit with:* A group's own affairs end where another group's begin, and there it asks first. Does my group know the groups around it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 23 (Bill W., on clubs and central committees).

### April 9 — "Rule No. 62" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group's right to run its own affairs includes the right to run them into the ground, and A.A. kept that right on purpose. Bill W. told the story (A.A. Comes of Age). In one town a newly sober promoter sold his group on a building: a club, a meeting room, a clinic, a loan office upstairs, three corporations and "sixty-one rules, regulations, and by-laws". Members who disagreed wrote to New York. The answer was that "each A.A. group could manage its own affairs as it liked, provided it did not harm surrounding groups." Headquarters warned them anyway, and then: "As it had a right to, this very autonomous group ignored our warnings." It crashed. Afterward the promoter mailed every group a card headed Rule No. 62, which read: "Don't take yourself too damned seriously." Bill's verdict: "Thus, under Tradition Four, an A.A. group had exercised its right to be wrong." The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) leaves a group its own conscience, even a mistaken one. Notice who did not step in: New York. Nobody rescued that group by taking it over, so the group learned. Today a group in trouble is offered rescue by a program that will run it from then on. Test it tonight: if your group made a bad call, ask who would fix it, the members or someone from outside.
*Sit with:* A.A. let a group make a mess of its own affairs rather than take them over. If my group got something badly wrong, who would put it right?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Four: the right to be wrong).

### April 10 — "A Center of Service" · *(how it gets captured)*
If A.A.'s own head office could never govern a group, no outside office can, and that is why a facility's hold on a meeting has no standing in A.A. Bill W. wrote in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 40): "Here at the General Office we have long known that we can merely supply certain indispensable services." Indispensable means the groups couldn't do without them; merely means that is all they are. Then came his list: information, literature, advice to new groups "if asked," help in settling disputes. His conclusion: "even here at the very center of A.A., there can only exist a center of service". The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) leaves each group "responsible to no other authority than its own conscience," and A.A.'s own office accepted that it was not one. Now picture the facility down the road. It has no standing in A.A. at all, yet it decides when the meeting runs, who chairs, and whether it runs at all. It has taken the power that A.A.'s own headquarters refused. The way in is usually service: a room, coffee, a staff member to help. Help that comes with the final say is government. Test it tonight: ask who could cancel your meeting without the members voting, and whether that is a servant or a boss.
*Sit with:* Even A.A.'s own office called itself a center of service with no power to govern. Who could cancel my meeting without a vote?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 40 (Bill W., 1947).

### April 11 — "The World Is Waiting" · *(the threat)*
The third sentence of Tradition Four is there because one member was ready to speak for all of A.A. on national radio. Bill W. told it (A.A. Comes of Age). A pioneer member had started a group with local radio lectures that mixed A.A. with his own religious ideas. Then a company offered to put them on a national network, under his own name, with a fee for him. The trustees advised against it. His reply: "To hell with the trustees, the world is waiting for my message. I've got the right of free speech, and I'm going on the air whether you like it or not." One man's choice would have told a whole country what A.A. was. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) closes that door: "And no group, regional committee, or individual should ever take any action that might greatly affect A.A. as a whole without conferring with the trustees of the General Service Board." Conferring means talking it over first. The trustees are the people A.A. made custodians of its Tradition (p. 565). Nobody used force; he was told every group would hear of it, and the broadcast never aired. Today one person on a podcast, or one program's website, tells the world what A.A. is. Test it tonight: ask who in your area speaks publicly for A.A., and who they asked first.
*Sit with:* One member nearly told a whole country what A.A. was, without asking anyone. Who speaks for A.A. in my town, and who did they ask?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); long form, Tradition 9 (Big Book, p. 565); A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Eleven: the radio lectures).

### April 12 — "Every Group Argument" · *(before the tradition)*
Tradition Four was not handed down to the groups; it was written up from them, out of years of their own arguments. Bill W. remembered the mid-1940s (A.A. Comes of Age): "It seemed as if every contestant in every group argument wrote us during this confused and exciting period." Headquarters answered without giving orders. Bill said they had to coin sentences that went into half the letters they wrote: "Of course you are at perfect liberty to handle this matter any way you please. But the majority experience in A.A. does seem to suggest" and then the experience followed. Liberty first; experience second, as a suggestion. "The basic ideas for the Twelve Traditions of Alcoholics Anonymous came directly out of this vast correspondence." And they were never meant as law: "A code of traditions could not, of course, ever become rule or law. But it might act as a guide for our Trustees, Headquarters people, and especially for A.A. groups with growing pains." The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) sets each group's own conscience over its own affairs because that is how the groups had been living all along. Today the letter a struggling meeting gets is more often a policy from its host, with no liberty in it. Test it tonight: ask where your group goes for advice, and whether it is free to say no.
*Sit with:* The Traditions were written up from the groups' own arguments and offered as a guide, never as law. Is my group being guided or governed?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (the correspondence behind the Traditions; "perfect liberty").

### April 13 — "Paramount" · *(the threat today)*
The last words of Tradition Four set a limit every group carries: some things belong to A.A. as a whole, and no single group or person gets to decide them. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "On such issues our common welfare is paramount." Paramount means it comes above everything else, above any one group's preference. Such issues are the ones named in the sentence before it: "any action that might greatly affect A.A. as a whole". When the Traditions were first published in 1946, that sentence named the trustees of The Alcoholic Foundation; a footnote in the pamphlet (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 8) records that in 1954 the name was changed to the General Service Board. Same job, new name. Autonomy covers a group's own meeting. It does not cover changing what A.A. is. Today what A.A. is gets decided, in plenty of towns, by people who never asked anyone: a program prints its own version of the Steps under A.A.'s name, a facility tells a judge what counts as an A.A. meeting. A group that goes along, reading that version or signing those slips, has acted for all of A.A. without asking. Test it tonight: find one thing said about A.A. in your town that A.A. as a whole never agreed to, and ask who said it.
*Sit with:* Some decisions belong to A.A. as a whole, and no group, program or person gets to make them alone. What has been said about A.A. in my town that A.A. never agreed to?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 8 (the Twelve Points of 1946, with its footnote).

### April 14 — "Full of Wisdom" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group gives Tradition Four away most easily to someone it admires. Picture the man who arrives from another city with twenty years and a voice that fills the room. Within a month he is explaining how his old group did it, which readings are proper, who should be allowed to share. Nobody voted him in. The group just starts doing what he says, because he sounds like A.A. Bill W. described him (A.A. Comes of Age): "Who is less popular than the old-time A.A. full of wisdom, who moves to another area and tries to tell the group there how to run its business?" Bill said such men "meet with stubborn resistance, or worse still, laughter." That resistance is the Tradition working. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) makes the group "responsible to no other authority than its own conscience," and a visitor's experience, however long, is advice, not authority. He can bring it to a business meeting and ask for a vote like anyone else. The group breaks Four when it skips that step and simply obeys. Today the voice from away is often a program graduate's, carrying a treatment centre's house rules into a free group as if they were A.A.'s. Test it tonight: ask whether anything your group does started because one person said so.
*Sit with:* Long sobriety brings advice to a group, not authority over it; only the group's conscience decides. Does my group do anything just because one person said so?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (no personal government in A.A.).

### April 15 — "Improve on Our Methods" · *(how it gets captured)*
A group that can't change its own ways can't find better ones, and that freedom is the first thing a captured meeting loses. Defending group autonomy against people who thought it went too far, Bill W. said (A.A. Comes of Age): "If, on the other hand, they found something better than A.A., or if they were able to improve on our methods, then in all probability we would adopt what they discovered for general use everywhere." He meant A.A. groups, answering to no one else. And: "Healthy trial and error always have their day and place in A.A." That is how A.A. was meant to learn: one group tries something, it works, and others take it up. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) protects the trying, because a group answerable only to "its own conscience" can vote to change its format next month. Now look at a meeting run inside a program. Its format is part of the program's curriculum, the same in every room the program runs, approved by people who never attend. The members can't vote it differently, so nothing new ever comes out of that room, and nothing better can travel from it to the rest of A.A. Test it tonight: name one thing your group does differently from the group across town, and ask who decided it.
*Sit with:* A.A. was meant to learn from groups free to try things and keep what worked. Is my group free to try something new?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Four: group autonomy and trial and error).

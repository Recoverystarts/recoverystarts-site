### April 16 — "The Whole of New York State" · *(the threat)*
No single body may act for every A.A. group at once, and A.A. learned that from a lease. The first A.A. club in New York took over rooms on West 24th Street, and a corporation was formed to hold the lease. Bill W. wrote in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 20) that it was titled "Alcoholics Anonymous of New York, Inc." Nobody meant any harm by it. Then: "Only later did we realize we had incorporated the whole of New York State, a mistake recently rectified." Incorporated means set up as a legal company. One clubroom's paperwork had put A.A.'s name over every group in a state, and none of them had been asked. That is why the third sentence of the Long Form exists (Big Book, p. 563): "And no group, regional committee, or individual should ever take any action that might greatly affect A.A. as a whole without conferring with the trustees of the General Service Board." The trustees look after A.A.'s shared services and its Traditions. Today the same mistake comes with a logo: a program, a website or a local committee using A.A.'s name as if it held the name for a whole city. Test it tonight: ask who decided that anything calling itself A.A. in your town could speak for every group in it.
*Sit with:* One clubroom's paperwork put A.A.'s name over a whole state, and nobody asked the groups. Who in my town uses A.A.'s name for groups that never agreed to it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 20 (Bill W., "Clubs in A.A.", 1947).

### April 17 — "Dutiful Children" · *(before the tradition)*
Before Tradition Four was written, the groups around New York had settled that no group answers to another, not even the one that started it. Bill W. told the story in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 21). The Manhattan club had nurtured the first suburban groups. As they grew they found their own rooms and their own friends. The club ran a central meeting and paid a secretary who took calls for the whole area, so, Bill admitted, New Yorkers thought "our outlying groups ought financially to support the Manhattan club; dutiful children should look after their" parents. The groups never argued. They simply decided for themselves: "Though many outlying A.A. members personally contributed to the 24th Street Club, nary a cent did their respective groups ever send in." Nary a cent means not one cent. Members gave as they chose; the groups kept their money for their own affairs, and nobody could make them do otherwise. That is the Long Form's first sentence before it was written down (Big Book, p. 563): each group "should be responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." Today the parent may be a big meeting, an office or a program that started a group and still expects it to obey. Test it tonight: ask whether any older group, office or person can tell your group what to do with its basket.
*Sit with:* The Manhattan club called the new groups its children, and the groups answered by running their own affairs. Does my group still answer to whoever started it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 21 (Bill W., "Clubs in A.A.", 1947).

### April 18 — "Its Own Affairs" · *(the threat today)*
Tradition Four is about who runs a group, not about keeping secrets, and the Long Form's own words prove it (Big Book, p. 563): "With respect to its own affairs, each A.A. group should be responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." A group's own affairs are its business: when and where it meets, how the meeting runs, what literature sits on the table, what it does with the basket, who chairs and for how long. The short form's word, autonomous, means self-governing. The Big Book's foreword says the early groups settled it the same way (p. xix): "each group was to be autonomous". There is nothing in the sentence about what members say, who said it or who may repeat it. Yet in many rooms today Tradition Four is taught as a promise of silence: what happens in this meeting stays in this meeting. That reading takes a sentence about self-government and turns it into a rule about privacy. The damage is not the privacy. It is that a group told Four means silence never learns that Four means it is allowed to run itself, and so someone else keeps running it. Test it tonight: ask your group when it last voted on one of its own affairs, and who chose the format you sat through.
*Sit with:* Tradition Four gives each group the right to govern its own business; it says nothing about secrets. Has my group been told the right half of it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); Foreword to the Second Edition (Big Book, p. xix).

### April 19 — "No Business Meeting" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group that never holds a business meeting has not kept its autonomy; it has handed it to whoever got there first. Picture a Tuesday meeting six years old. The man who opened it still picks the reading, the topic and the chair, because nobody has ever called a vote. There is no treasurer; the basket goes home in his coat. Nobody stole anything and nobody is unkind. The group has simply never sat down as a group to decide its own affairs, so his habits became its rules. The Long Form makes a group "responsible to no other authority than its own conscience" (Big Book, p. 563), and a conscience that never meets cannot speak. Bill W. explained what keeps the person at the front honest (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 39): "while he can always guide by example or persuasion he can never boss, else at election time he may find himself passed by." That only works if there is an election. Today some rooms that call themselves A.A. groups have never held a business meeting at all, and some have no home members, only a time slot and a key. Test it tonight: ask when your group last met to decide something, and who would call that meeting if you asked for one.
*Sit with:* Autonomy that is never used goes to whoever is loudest or longest in the room. When did my group last decide anything by vote?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 39 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### April 20 — "Run the A.A. Affairs" · *(how it gets captured)*
When the building runs the meeting, the group has another authority, and Tradition Four allows it none. A.A. learned this from its early clubs, which were separate corporations renting rooms to groups. Bill W. wrote (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 21): "if an A.A. rotating committee tries to boss the club corporation or if the corporation tries to run the A.A. affairs of those groups who may meet at the club, there is difficulty at once." His answer was to keep the two apart: "If an A.A. group wishes to use a given club, let them pay rent or split the meeting take with the club management." Pay for the room, and run your own meeting. Now look at a meeting held inside a facility. The facility sets the time, picks who chairs, decides which topics are allowed, keeps the sign-in sheet and can cancel it. It is running the A.A. affairs of whoever sits there. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) says a group answers to "no other authority than its own conscience." A meeting whose rules come from a landlord, a program or a staff schedule answers to them instead, however warm the room. A real group can rent any building that will have it and still run itself. Test it tonight: ask who could change your meeting's format tomorrow without a vote of its members.
*Sit with:* A group may rent a room from anyone, but it may not hand over its meeting with the rent. Who sets the rules in my room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 21 (Bill W., "Clubs in A.A.", 1947).

### April 21 — "Advice If Asked" · *(the threat)*
The danger Tradition Four answers is a centre that governs the groups, and A.A.'s own head office was the first place one could have grown. By 1947 the office in New York answered letters from groups everywhere, sent out the literature and held A.A.'s shared funds through its trustees. It could easily have started giving orders. Bill W. wrote what it did instead (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 40): "Here at the General Office we have long known that we can merely supply certain indispensable services." Among them: "we can assist new groups to start, giving advice if asked". And over all of it, the page before (p. 39): "Nowhere in A.A. is there to be seen any constituted human authority that can compel an A.A. group to do anything." Constituted means officially set up. Advice if asked; orders never. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) puts the same limit in writing: each group is responsible "to no other authority than its own conscience." Today the would-be centre is usually local: an office, a committee, a member with long years, or a program that tells groups what they must do to be listed or to keep their room. Test it tonight: when someone tells your group it has to do something, ask which A.A. body has the power to make it, and listen to the answer.
*Sit with:* Even A.A.'s head office could only give advice when asked; nobody in A.A. can compel a group. Who has been giving my group orders?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, pp. 39–40 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### April 22 — "Suggestive Only" · *(before the tradition)*
Before any Tradition was written, the first A.A. book had already left each new group to run itself. In 1939 there were only a few centres, and the Big Book described what was spreading out from them (p. 162): "Little clusters of twos and threes and fives of us have sprung up in other communities". Nobody was sent to manage them. The book spoke to the reader who might start the next one alone, and handed him no rulebook. About itself it said (p. 164): "Our book is meant to be suggestive only. We realize we know only a little." The people who wrote the book did not claim the right to run what it started. Each new group worked out its own meeting night, its own room and its own ways. Seven years later the Long Form wrote down what those clusters had been doing all along (Big Book, p. 563): "With respect to its own affairs, each A.A. group should be responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." Today a new meeting is often handed a script before its first night: the facility's format, the program's rules, a card to read that nobody voted on. Test it tonight: ask who wrote the rules your meeting reads out, and whether your group ever voted to adopt them.
*Sit with:* The book that started A.A. called itself suggestive only and left each group to run itself. Who wrote the rules my meeting follows?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A Vision for You (Big Book, pp. 162, 164).

### April 23 — "Ought to Be Consulted" · *(the threat today)*
The second sentence of Tradition Four is a courtesy between equals, and today it gets misread as a chain of command. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "But when its plans concern the welfare of neighboring groups also, those groups ought to be consulted." Consulted means asked, before you act. Not obeyed. Not asked for permission. A group planning to move its meeting onto the same night as the small group down the road, start a phone line under A.A.'s name, or print a list of local meetings is making plans that touch its neighbours, so it asks them first. Nobody sits above both groups to rule on it. In the big cities that asking grew into shared work: phone calls, hospital arrangements, a place for newcomers to be interviewed. Bill W. wrote that for those jobs (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 23) "a central or intergroup committee has to be elected and financed to look after them." Elected and paid for by the groups, so it answers to them, not they to it. The misreading runs both ways today: a group acting on its neighbours without a word, or an office treating consultation as a licence to rule. Test it tonight: ask whether your group's last big change was talked over with the groups it touched, and who elected the committee that says it speaks for them.
*Sit with:* Tradition Four asks a group to consult its neighbours, not to obey anyone. Does my group ask before it acts, and does anyone try to rule it in return?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 23 (Bill W., "Clubs in A.A.", 1947).

### April 24 — "Never to Command" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group's committee carries out the group's wishes, and the moment it starts deciding them, the group has lost its own affairs. Picture a steering committee of three, picked two years ago because they were willing. At first they bought the coffee and booked the room. Then they decided who could share from the front, banned a topic, and told a man who came in shaking that he would need thirty days before he could read the Steps aloud. Nobody voted on any of it. The committee simply began speaking for the group, and the group let it. Bill W. wrote what committees in A.A. are for (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 39): "they have only the authorization to serve, never to command. Each committee carries out what it believes to be the wishes of its group. That is all." That is all. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) makes each group answer to "no other authority than its own conscience", and a committee is not the group's conscience. The members are, sitting together and voting. Today the same slide happens wherever a few willing people start making rules between business meetings, or a meeting has no business meetings at all. Test it tonight: ask whether your group's committee has made a rule the members never voted on.
*Sit with:* A committee in A.A. may serve the group's wishes but never command them. Which rule in my group did the members never vote on?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 39 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### April 25 — "One Group's Yes" · *(how it gets captured)*
One group cannot say yes for A.A., and that is exactly the yes outside programs come looking for. A treatment program wants A.A. on its brochure. It asks one friendly group to meet in its building, on its night, and to let it tell families that A.A. is part of its care. One group agrees. Look at what was decided. The public now reads A.A. as a piece of that program. Every other group in town is asked why it won't do the same. That plan greatly affects A.A. as a whole, which the Long Form says no group may take on alone without conferring with A.A.'s trustees, and it ends (Big Book, p. 563): "On such issues our common welfare is paramount." Paramount means first, above everything else. Bill W. had already drawn the line in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 25): "neither A.A. as a whole nor any A.A. group ought to enter any activity other than straight A.A." And: "we cannot link the A.A. name to other enterprises in the alcohol field to the extent that the public gets the impression we have abandoned our sole aim." A group's freedom covers its own meeting, never the name every group shares. Test it tonight: ask whether any brochure or website lists your meeting as part of somebody's program.
*Sit with:* A group may run its own meeting, but it cannot lend out the name every group shares. Has my group said yes to something on behalf of all of A.A.?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 25 (Bill W., "Dangers in Linking A.A. to Other Projects", 1947).

### April 26 — "Preoccupied With Their Own Affairs" · *(the threat)*
A group's freedom has a back door, and A.A. nearly lost its own office through it. As the Fellowship grew in the 1940s, the New York office answered the letters, printed the literature and kept the directory of groups, work done for every group at once. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "Many groups, preoccupied with their own affairs, failed to help us at all. Less than half of them contributed anything." Book sales covered the gap: "Without this book income we would have folded up entirely." Each of those groups was within its rights, because nobody can make a group give. But the Long Form does not end on a group's rights. Its last sentence (Big Book, p. 563): "On such issues our common welfare is paramount." The issues are the ones that touch neighbouring groups and A.A. as a whole, and an office that answers the drunk's letter is one of them. A group that runs its own meeting well while the line that brings the next drunk in goes unpaid has read half of Tradition Four. Today the back door looks the same: a healthy basket that never goes past the group's own rent and coffee, while the phone line, the meeting list and the office are left to someone else. Test it tonight: ask where your group's money goes after the rent.
*Sit with:* The groups had every right to keep their money, and the office that answered the drunks nearly folded. Does any of my group's basket reach past our own room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy).

### April 27 — "Directly Accountable" · *(before the tradition)*
Conferring with the trustees was never meant to be asking a boss, because A.A. went on to make its trustees answer to the groups. When the Long Form was first printed in 1946, the trustees were, in Bill's later words, an independent body. The Long Form still told every group, committee and member to confer with them before acting for A.A. as a whole, because someone had to hold the whole in view. Then the groups built a way for their own voice to reach that board: delegates elected out of their own assemblies, meeting as a conference. Bill W. described where it ended (A.A. Comes of Age): "Even our Trustees, once an independent body, are today directly accountable to our fellowship. They are the caretakers and expediters of our world services." Expediters are the people who see that the work gets done. And the line that holds it together: "It is in this spirit of service that we elect the A.A. groups’ informal rotating committees, the Intergroup Associations of the area, and the General Service Conference for A.A. as a whole." The chain runs up from the groups by election and answers back down. Test it tonight: ask whether your group elects someone to carry its voice to the area, and what they last carried.
*Sit with:* A.A.'s trustees answer to the groups through people the groups elect. Does my group have a voice in that chain, or has it left the seat empty?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy).

### April 28 — "Or Individual" · *(the threat today)*
Tradition Four names the individual too: no one person may act for A.A. as a whole. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "And no group, regional committee, or individual should ever take any action that might greatly affect A.A. as a whole without conferring with the trustees". A regional committee is a body serving the groups of an area. The list runs from one person to many groups, and nobody on it is exempt. Today the individual is the hardest to see. A well-known speaker tells a reporter what A.A. believes about medication. A counsellor who is a member promises a judge that A.A. will confirm attendance. Somebody builds a website and runs it as if it were A.A.'s own. Each one acts for every group they have never met, and none of those groups was asked. Bill W. described the freedom that makes this matter (A.A. Comes of Age): "We cannot be compelled to do anything. In that sense this society is a benign anarchy." Benign means kind; anarchy means nobody in charge. In a fellowship with nobody in charge, the person who acts as if they are in charge is the threat. Test it tonight: the next time someone tells you what A.A. will do, ask who they asked before they promised it.
*Sit with:* The Long Form forbids any one person to act for all of A.A. without asking. Who has spoken for my group to people outside it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy).

### April 29 — "Voted Away" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can give away its autonomy by its own vote, and it usually does it to be helpful. Picture a group that meets in a facility's lounge because the rent is free. The staff ask for small things, one at a time. A sign-in sheet, so they know who was in the building. Only the facility's handouts on the table. End ten minutes early for the count. A staff member sits at the back, just to listen. Each time the group votes yes, to keep the room. A year later the facility's rules are the meeting, and nobody remembers deciding that. Every one of those votes was the group's right, and together they spent the right. The Long Form says a group's affairs answer to "no other authority than its own conscience" (Big Book, p. 563), and a group that votes in another authority has made itself a guest. Bill W. listed what A.A. does without (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 38): "no fees, no dues, no rules and regulations, no demand that alcoholics conform to A.A. principles, no one set in personal authority over anyone else." A free room that comes with an authority attached is not free. Test it tonight: list the rules your meeting follows, and mark the ones your members actually chose.
*Sit with:* A group can vote its own freedom away one small yes at a time. Which rules in my room came with the room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 38 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### April 30 — "A Town-Meeting Democracy" · *(how it gets captured)*
April ends where Tradition Four starts: each group governs itself by vote, and whatever takes that vote away has captured it. Bill W. described how the groups got there (A.A. Comes of Age): "As the old-timers retired, we began to elect our servants by majority vote. Each group in this sense became a town-meeting democracy." A town meeting is where the people of a place settle its business themselves, in one room, out loud. Read the whole Tradition with that picture (Big Book, p. 563). A group answers to "no other authority than its own conscience" in its own affairs. It consults its neighbours when its plans touch them. It takes no action for A.A. as a whole alone. And on those issues "our common welfare is paramount." Four sentences, and every one of them is about how groups decide, not how members behave. Capture rarely comes as a takeover. It comes as convenience: a facility that sets the format, a program that supplies the chair, an office that sends instructions, a group that stops meeting to decide anything because someone else always has. Each one quietly closes the town meeting. May turns to Tradition Five, the one purpose every group's vote is for: the drunk still drinking. Test it tonight: ask your group to hold a business meeting this month, and see who comes to decide.
*Sit with:* Tradition Four makes every group a town meeting that runs its own business by vote. Is my group's town meeting open, or has someone closed it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy).

### April 16 — "The Whole of New York State" · *(the threat)*
No single body may act for every A.A. group at once, and A.A. learned that from a lease. The first A.A. club in New York took over rooms on West 24th Street, and a corporation was formed to hold the lease. Bill W. wrote in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 19) that it was titled "Alcoholics Anonymous of New York, Inc." Nobody meant any harm by it. Then: "Only later did we realize we had incorporated the whole of New York State, a mistake recently rectified." Incorporated means set up as a legal company. One clubroom's paperwork had put A.A.'s name over every group in a state, and none of them had been asked. That is why the third sentence of the Long Form exists (Big Book, p. 563): "And no group, regional committee, or individual should ever take any action that might greatly affect A.A. as a whole without conferring with the trustees of the General Service Board." The trustees look after A.A.'s shared services and its Traditions. Today the same mistake comes with a logo: a program, a website or a local committee using A.A.'s name as if it held the name for a whole city. Test it tonight: ask who decided that anything calling itself A.A. in your town could speak for every group in it.
*Sit with:* One clubroom's paperwork put A.A.'s name over a whole state, and nobody asked the groups. Who in my town uses A.A.'s name for groups that never agreed to it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 19 (Bill W., "Clubs in A.A.", 1947).

### April 17 — "Dutiful Children" · *(before the tradition)*
Before Tradition Four was written, the groups around New York had settled that no group answers to another, not even the one that started it. Bill W. told the story in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 20). The Manhattan club had nurtured the first suburban groups. As they grew they found their own rooms and their own friends. The club ran a central meeting and paid a secretary who took calls for the whole area, so, Bill admitted, New Yorkers thought "our outlying groups ought financially to support the Manhattan club; dutiful children should look after their" parents. The groups never argued. They simply decided for themselves: "Though many outlying A.A. members personally contributed to the 24th Street Club, nary a cent did their respective groups ever send in." Nary a cent means not one cent. Members gave as they chose; the groups kept their money for their own affairs, and nobody could make them do otherwise. That is the Long Form's first sentence before it was written down (Big Book, p. 563): each group "should be responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." Today the parent may be a big meeting, an office or a program that started a group and still expects it to obey. Test it tonight: ask whether any older group, office or person can tell your group what to do with its basket.
*Sit with:* The Manhattan club called the new groups its children, and the groups answered by running their own affairs. Does my group still answer to whoever started it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 20 (Bill W., "Clubs in A.A.", 1947).

### April 18 — "Who Holds the Link" · *(the threat today)*
An online meeting is a group's own affair like any other, and whoever holds the link, the password and the host controls holds the group unless the group decides otherwise. The Big Book's latest foreword notes it (p. xxiv): "A.A. members with computers can participate in meetings online, sharing with fellow alcoholics across the country or around the world." Nothing changed in the Tradition when the room moved onto a screen. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "With respect to its own affairs, each A.A. group should be responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." But an online room has new handles of power. One member pays for the account and can close it. A host can mute, remove or block anyone with a click. A program can run the meeting on its own platform and keep the list of who attended. None of that has to be sinister for it to be authority, and the group never voted any of it in. A group that meets online governs the link the same way a room group governs its key and its basket: by deciding, together, who holds it and for how long. Test it tonight: ask who could end your online meeting tomorrow, and whether the group ever chose them.
*Sit with:* Online, the key to the room is the link and the host controls. Did my group choose who holds them?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); Foreword to the Fourth Edition (Big Book, p. xxiv).

### April 19 — "A Bare Majority" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can run its own affairs by vote and still break itself apart by voting badly. Picture a business meeting about moving the meeting to a new building. Twenty people come; eleven vote yes, nine vote no, and the eleven call it settled and start packing. Nobody asked what the nine needed. Three weeks later the nine are meeting on their own in the old room, and the town has two half-groups where it had one. Bill W. saw this coming (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 29). Writing in 1946 about whether groups should pay for clubs, he set down a principle that reaches every group vote: "But the majority ought not to try to coerce the minority into supporting clubs they do not want or need." Coerce means force. Later, when A.A. built its service structure, it designed elections around the same danger (A.A. Comes of Age): "Another prime cause of grief was the hotly contested close election, which nearly always left behind a large and discontented minority." The Long Form gives each group "no other authority than its own conscience" (Big Book, p. 563), and the whole room is that conscience, not the winning side of a count. Test it tonight: at your next business meeting, before a close vote is called, ask whether the group has talked long enough.
*Sit with:* A group's conscience is the whole room, not the winning side of a count. When my group last voted, what happened to the ones who lost?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 29 (Bill W., "Money", 1946); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy).

### April 20 — "Run the A.A. Affairs" · *(how it gets captured)*
When the building runs the meeting, the group has another authority, and Tradition Four allows it none. A.A. learned this from its early clubs, separate corporations that rented rooms to groups. Bill W. wrote (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 20): "if an A.A. rotating committee tries to boss the club corporation or if the corporation tries to run the A.A. affairs of those groups who may meet at the club, there is difficulty at once." His answer was to keep the two apart: "If an A.A. group wishes to use a given club, let them pay rent or split the meeting take with the club management." Now look at a recovery centre that houses a dozen meetings. It prints them on its own calendar under its own name, bars people from the building and so from the meetings, and tells the groups what they may put on the wall. It is running the A.A. affairs of whoever meets there. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) says a group answers to "no other authority than its own conscience." A meeting whose rules come from its landlord answers to the landlord, however warm the room. A real group can rent any building that will have it and still run itself. Test it tonight: ask who could change your meeting's format tomorrow without a vote of its members.
*Sit with:* A group may rent a room from anyone, but it may not hand over its meeting with the rent. Who sets the rules in my room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 20 (Bill W., "Clubs in A.A.", 1947).

### April 21 — "In Every Group" · *(the threat)*
When A.A. grew too fast to be run by anyone, the danger was that someone would try anyway, and the answer was to let each group govern itself under shared principles. By 1941 the Big Book counts 8,000 members, and its foreword asks what everyone feared (p. xviii): "Would there be quarrels over membership, leadership, and money?" It answers on the next page (p. xix): "Soon A.A. was beset by these very problems on every side and in every group." No office could have refereed thousands of group fights by mail, and nobody had the right to. So, the foreword goes on, "we had to evolve principles by which the A.A. groups and A.A. as a whole could survive and function effectively." Notice the two levels: the groups, and A.A. as a whole. That is the shape of Tradition Four. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) gives each group its own affairs, "responsible to no other authority than its own conscience," and keeps for A.A. as a whole only what touches everyone. Today the fear comes back as a demand for a referee: an office, a district or a program asked to settle a group's quarrel for it. Test it tonight: when your group has a fight, ask who it expects to settle it, and whether that someone has any right to.
*Sit with:* A.A. met its fights in every group by letting every group govern itself. Who does my group run to when it can't agree?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); Foreword to the Second Edition (Big Book, pp. xviii–xix).

### April 22 — "Suggestive Only" · *(before the tradition)*
Before any Tradition was written, the first A.A. book had already left each new group to run itself. In 1939 there were only a few centres, and the Big Book described what was spreading out from them (p. 162): "Little clusters of twos and threes and fives of us have sprung up in other communities". Nobody was sent to manage them. The book spoke to the reader who might start the next one alone, and handed him no rulebook. About itself it said (p. 164): "Our book is meant to be suggestive only. We realize we know only a little." The people who wrote the book did not claim the right to run what it started. Each new group worked out its own meeting night, its own room and its own ways. Seven years later the Long Form wrote down what those clusters had been doing all along (Big Book, p. 563): "With respect to its own affairs, each A.A. group should be responsible to no other authority than its own conscience." Today a new meeting is often handed a script before its first night: the facility's format, the program's rules, a card to read that nobody voted on. Test it tonight: ask who wrote the rules your meeting reads out, and whether your group ever voted to adopt them.
*Sit with:* The book that started A.A. called itself suggestive only and left each group to run itself. Who wrote the rules my meeting follows?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A Vision for You (Big Book, pp. 162, 164).

### April 23 — "Everybody's Book" · *(the threat today)*
Anything that belongs to every group, the A.A. name, its book, its Steps, can only be changed by asking them all, and today it gets changed without asking anyone. A.A.'s own book was named that way. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "The Akron and New York groups had been voting for months on possible titles." Two groups, one book, both voting, because the book would speak for both. Bill admits he was tempted to settle it himself, with a title he could sign: "I began to forget that this was everybody's book and that I had been mostly the umpire of the discussions that had created it." An umpire calls the game; he does not own it. Friends slapped the idea down, and the groups kept voting. That is the Long Form's third sentence at work (Big Book, p. 563): "And no group, regional committee, or individual should ever take any action that might greatly affect A.A. as a whole without conferring with the trustees of the General Service Board." Today the Steps get rewritten on a facility's wall, a program prints its own A.A. readings, a website edits the Traditions to suit its point. Each one changes everybody's book without asking. Test it tonight: when you are handed A.A. words, ask whether they match the book, and who changed them.
*Sit with:* A.A.'s book belonged to every group, so the groups voted on its name. Whose version of A.A.'s words is read in my room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (naming the book).

### April 24 — "The Same Night" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group that changes its plans without asking the groups they will hurt has stopped at the first sentence of Tradition Four. Picture a small town with two meetings. The big one meets Monday; the small one, eight people who started it so a few women had a room of their own, meets Thursday. The big group's business meeting votes to add a Thursday night, same hour, two blocks away, because the coffee is better there and more people would come. Nobody calls the Thursday group. Within two months the small room is down to three and closes. The big group did nothing outside its own affairs, and it still did harm. The Long Form's second sentence (Big Book, p. 563) was written for exactly this: "But when its plans concern the welfare of neighboring groups also, those groups ought to be consulted." Consulted means asked first, as equals; not permission, not a vote by anyone above them. The Big Book gives the reason the courtesy matters (p. 561): "We alcoholics see that we must work together and hang together, else most of us will finally die alone." The drunk who needed that Thursday room is the one who pays. Test it tonight: before your group changes its time, place or format, ask which other group it will touch, and call them.
*Sit with:* A group can stay inside its own affairs and still close its neighbour's door. Has my group asked the groups nearby before changing anything?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); The A.A. Tradition (Big Book, p. 561).

### April 25 — "One Group's Yes" · *(how it gets captured)*
One group cannot say yes for A.A., and that is exactly the yes outside programs come looking for. A treatment program wants A.A. on its brochure. It asks one friendly group to meet in its building, on its night, and to let it tell families that A.A. is part of its care. One group agrees. Look at what was decided. The public now reads A.A. as a piece of that program. Every other group in town is asked why it won't do the same. That plan greatly affects A.A. as a whole, which the Long Form says no group may take on alone without conferring with A.A.'s trustees, and it ends (Big Book, p. 563): "On such issues our common welfare is paramount." Paramount means first, above everything else. Bill W. had already drawn the line in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 24): "neither A.A. as a whole nor any A.A. group ought to enter any activity other than straight A.A." And: "we cannot link the A.A. name to other enterprises in the alcohol field to the extent that the public gets the impression we have abandoned our sole aim." A group's freedom covers its own meeting, never the name every group shares. Test it tonight: ask whether any brochure or website lists your meeting as part of somebody's program.
*Sit with:* A group may run its own meeting, but it cannot lend out the name every group shares. Has my group said yes to something on behalf of all of A.A.?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 24 (Bill W., "Dangers in Linking A.A. to Other Projects", 1947).

### April 26 — "Preoccupied With Their Own Affairs" · *(the threat)*
A group's freedom has a back door, and A.A. nearly lost its own office through it. As the Fellowship grew in the 1940s, the New York office answered the letters, printed the literature and kept the directory of groups, work done for every group at once. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "Many groups, preoccupied with their own affairs, failed to help us at all. Less than half of them contributed anything." Book sales covered the gap: "Without this book income we would have folded up entirely." Each of those groups was within its rights, because nobody can make a group give. But the Long Form does not end on a group's rights. Its last sentence (Big Book, p. 563): "On such issues our common welfare is paramount." The issues are the ones that touch neighbouring groups and A.A. as a whole, and an office that answers the drunk's letter is one of them. A group that runs its own meeting well while the line that brings the next drunk in goes unpaid has read half of Tradition Four. Today the back door looks the same: a healthy basket that never goes past the group's own rent and coffee, while the phone line, the meeting list and the office are left to someone else. Test it tonight: ask where your group's money goes after the rent.
*Sit with:* The groups had every right to keep their money, and the office that answered the drunks nearly folded. Does any of my group's basket reach past our own room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy).

### April 27 — "Directly Accountable" · *(before the tradition)*
Conferring with the trustees was never meant to be asking a boss, because A.A. went on to make its trustees answer to the groups. When the Long Form was first printed in 1946, the trustees were, in Bill's later words, an independent body. The Long Form still told every group, committee and member to confer with them before acting for A.A. as a whole, because someone had to hold the whole in view. Then the groups built a way for their own voice to reach that board: delegates elected out of their own assemblies, meeting as a conference. Bill W. described where it ended (A.A. Comes of Age): "Even our Trustees, once an independent body, are today directly accountable to our fellowship. They are the caretakers and expediters of our world services." Expediters are the people who see that the work gets done. And the line that holds it together: "It is in this spirit of service that we elect the A.A. groups’ informal rotating committees, the Intergroup Associations of the area, and the General Service Conference for A.A. as a whole." The chain runs up from the groups by election and answers back down. Test it tonight: ask whether your group elects someone to carry its voice to the area, and what they last carried.
*Sit with:* A.A.'s trustees answer to the groups through people the groups elect. Does my group have a voice in that chain, or has it left the seat empty?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy).

### April 28 — "Never Acts of Government" · *(the threat today)*
The Traditions bind groups by agreement, not by force, and today they are sometimes used as a rulebook to punish the very groups they protect. The Big Book's foreword says how they took hold (p. xix): "Though none of these principles had the force of rules or laws, they had become so widely accepted by 1950 that they were confirmed by our first International Conference held at Cleveland." Accepted, not imposed. When A.A. built a conference to serve the whole Fellowship, it wrote the same limit into its charter, its founding rules (A.A. Comes of Age): "though the Conference may act for the service of Alcoholics Anonymous, it shall never perform any acts of government". If the conference that speaks for all of A.A. may never govern, nothing smaller may either. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) leaves each group's own affairs to "no other authority than its own conscience." Today the threat comes dressed as the Traditions: a committee that drops a group from the meeting list for reading the wrong thing, a member who threatens a group with A.A. headquarters, a program that tells a group it is breaking a Tradition and must change. Persuasion is welcome. Punishment is government. Test it tonight: if anyone tells your group it will lose something for breaking a Tradition, ask who gave them the power to take it.
*Sit with:* The Traditions took hold because groups agreed to them, never because anyone could enforce them. Who has threatened my group in the Traditions' name?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); Foreword to the Second Edition (Big Book, p. xix); A.A. Comes of Age (from the Conference Charter).

### April 29 — "Voted Away" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can give away its autonomy by its own vote, and it usually does it to be helpful. Picture a group that meets in a facility's lounge because the rent is free. The staff ask for small things, one at a time. A sign-in sheet, so they know who was in the building. Only the facility's handouts on the table. End ten minutes early for the count. A staff member sits at the back, just to listen. Each time the group votes yes, to keep the room. A year later the facility's rules are the meeting, and nobody remembers deciding that. Every one of those votes was the group's right, and together they spent the right. The Long Form says a group's affairs answer to "no other authority than its own conscience" (Big Book, p. 563), and a group that votes in another authority has made itself a guest. Bill W. listed what A.A. does without (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 37): "no fees, no dues, no rules and regulations, no demand that alcoholics conform to A.A. principles, no one set in personal authority over anyone else." A free room that comes with an authority attached is not free. Test it tonight: list the rules your meeting follows, and mark the ones your members actually chose.
*Sit with:* A group can vote its own freedom away one small yes at a time. Which rules in my room came with the room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 37 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### April 30 — "Continuous Effectiveness" · *(how it gets captured)*
April ends where Tradition Four starts: each group governs its own business, and whatever takes that away has captured the group, however kindly. The Big Book puts the stakes plainly in its essay on the Traditions (p. 561): "Therefore, no society of men and women ever had a more urgent need for continuous effectiveness and permanent unity." Effectiveness means a group that keeps working; unity means one that stays whole. Read the whole Tradition with that in mind (Big Book, p. 563). A group answers to "no other authority than its own conscience" in its own affairs. It consults its neighbours when its plans touch them. It takes no action for A.A. as a whole alone. And on those issues "our common welfare is paramount." Four sentences, and every one of them is about how groups decide, not how members behave. Capture rarely comes as a takeover. It comes as convenience: a facility that sets the format, a program that supplies the chair, an office that sends instructions, a group that stops meeting to decide anything because someone else always has. Each one quietly ends a group's self-government. May turns to Tradition Five, the one purpose every group's decisions exist for: the drunk still drinking. Test it tonight: ask your group to hold a business meeting this month, and see who comes to decide.
*Sit with:* Tradition Four makes every group responsible for its own business and no one else's servant. Is my group still deciding for itself?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 4 (Big Book, p. 563); The A.A. Tradition (Big Book, p. 561).

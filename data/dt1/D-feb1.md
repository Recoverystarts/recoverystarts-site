### February 1 — "Never Personal Authority" · *(the threat)*
Tradition Two answers one question for every A.A. group: who decides? The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Ultimate authority means the last word, and in a group's business no person holds it. The threat it answers is a boss. Bill W. wrote in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 37): "Time out of mind, families, friends, employers, doctors, clergymen, and judges have tried their hand at disciplining alcoholics. Almost without exception the failure to accomplish anything by coercion has been complete." Coercion means forcing. Every drunk who walks in has already been bossed by experts, and it didn't work. Put a boss over the group and he meets one more person trying to run him, and he leaves. So Bill drew the line: "the only real authority to be found in A.A. is that of spiritual principle. It is never personal authority." That tells no one what to believe. It takes power away from whoever wants it. Today the boss often comes built in: a counsellor at the front of the room, a program that sets the rules, a court that wants a signature. Test it tonight: ask who has the last word in your room, and whether the answer is a person.
*Sit with:* Tradition Two gives the last word in a group's business to no person at all. Who has the last word in my group?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 37 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### February 2 — "The Voice on the Subway" · *(before the tradition)*
Years before Tradition Two was written, Bill W. heard what he took for God's voice, and his group overruled it. It was 1937. Bill was broke, and a hospital owner offered him an office, money to live on and a share of the profits to work there as a lay therapist, a paid counsellor without a medical degree. Bill wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "Going back to Brooklyn on the subway, I had a flash of seeming divine guidance." He told the meeting in his own parlour that night, sure of the answer. The members heard him out in silence, then said no: A.A. could never have a professional, and they could not tie it to that hospital or any other. His verdict: "Thus spoke the group conscience. The group was right and I was wrong; the voice on the subway was not the voice of God." Read it slowly. The co-founder had a private sense of what God wanted, and the room's decision outranked it. From nights like that came the principle he put this way: "God, speaking in the group conscience, is to be our final authority." The Long Form kept it (Big Book, p. 563): the one authority speaks "in our group conscience," not through one member, however sure. Test it tonight: ask when your group last overruled the person who was surest.
*Sit with:* Bill's private guidance lost to his group's decision, and he said the group was right. Who in my room is too sure to be overruled?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — Tradition Two, the Towns Hospital offer, 1937).

### February 3 — "Not One Word About Leaders" · *(the threat today)*
Read Tradition Two in its Long Form and you will find not one word about leaders, because the Tradition is about where authority sits, not whom to trust. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) is a single sentence: "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." The line about leaders appears only in the short form (p. 562). Its full version is in the Long Form of Tradition Nine (p. 565): "true leaders in A.A. are but trusted and experienced servants of the whole. They derive no real authority from their titles; they do not govern." Servants of the whole means they work for the members. The rumour runs the other way: that a trusted servant is someone you should trust to decide for you. Today the phrase gets printed on a lanyard. A program calls its staff member the meeting's trusted servant, and the room assumes the title came with authority. It came with none. In an A.A. group, the members hand out a job by vote and can take it back the same way, and the title adds nothing to the person holding it. Test it tonight: ask who gave your meeting's leader the job, and whether the group could take it back next month.
*Sit with:* The Long Form of Tradition Two names an authority and no leaders, and Tradition Nine says titles carry no real authority. Who gave the person at the front of my room the job?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); short form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 562); long form, Tradition 9 (Big Book, p. 565).

### February 4 — "Who Could Know Better" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group breaks Tradition Two the quiet way when it lets one long-sober member's say-so stand in for its own vote. Picture the business meeting. Someone proposes moving the meeting to a night when more people could come. Heads nod. Then the man with twenty years says that isn't how this group does things, and the motion dies without a vote. Nobody gave him a veto. The room handed it over. Bill W. admitted the old-timers' habit (A.A. Comes of Age): "We oldsters often construed our longer experience as a sort of vested right, even an unlimited license, to run A.A. indefinitely." A vested right is one you believe you own. They even thought they should pick their own successors, he wrote, and asked, "Who could know better than we?" The groups said no. Of the old-timer he concluded: "With all his experience and good works, there was still nothing infallible about him at all." Infallible means unable to be wrong. Long sobriety earns a hearing. It does not earn the decision. The Long Form puts the decision in one place (Big Book, p. 563): "our group conscience," the members deciding together. Today the same veto goes to whoever has run the room longest, from a member's chair or a staff desk. Test it tonight: ask what your group last dropped without a vote because somebody senior said no.
*Sit with:* Long sobriety earns a hearing, not the group's decision. What has my group dropped because one senior voice said no?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — Tradition Two).

### February 5 — "Places of Service" · *(how it gets captured)*
A.A.'s own service offices cannot govern the groups, because the groups pay for them and can stop. Bill W. wrote about A.A.'s clubs, local offices and the New York office in 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 28): "These are places of service; they cannot really control or govern A.A. If any of them were to become inefficient or overbearing the remedy is simple enough. The average A.A. would stop his financial support until conditions were changed." Overbearing means bossy. The members hold the purse, so the office answers to them. That is the Long Form at work (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Now turn it around. A room inside a treatment facility runs on the facility's budget. Nobody in the chairs pays for it, so nobody in the chairs can stop paying. The program decides the night, the format and who may come, and it calls the hour an A.A. meeting. Bill's remedy can't reach it, because the members never held the purse in the first place. A room like that has been captured before anyone in it says a word. Test it tonight: ask who pays for your room, and what would happen if the members stopped.
*Sit with:* A.A.'s offices answer to the groups because the groups pay for them. Who pays for my room, and so who does it answer to?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 28 (Bill W., "Money", 1946).

### February 6 — "A Servant Only" · *(the threat)*
If the man who started A.A. could not govern it, nobody can, and that is the danger Tradition Two closes. Bill W. co-founded A.A., and in 1947 he described trying to run it anyway (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 39): "After struggling a few years to run the A.A. movement I had to give it up — it simply didn't work. Heavy-handed assertion of my personal authority always created confusion and resistance." Then four words: "Personal power always failed." And the lesson he drew for every member: "In such fashion each of us learns that, in A.A., one can be a servant only." The threat was never a bad man. It was a founder, meaning well, holding a power the drunks would not accept. A fellowship that rested on one man's rule would have split the day he was wrong, or died with him. So the Long Form hands the authority to something no member can hold alone (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Today the founder figure is the program director whose name is on the meeting, or the member who started the group years ago and still runs it. Test it tonight: ask whether anyone in your room is treated as the one it can't do without.
*Sit with:* Even A.A.'s co-founder found that personal power always failed and that a member can be a servant only. Is anyone in my group treated as the one it can't do without?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 39 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### February 7 — "Eighteen in Akron" · *(before the tradition)*
Before A.A. had a single Tradition, its two founders could not move without a vote of the members. In the fall of 1937 Bill W. and Dr. Bob wanted paid missionaries, a chain of hospitals and a book. Dr. Bob suggested trying the ideas on the Akron members first. Eighteen of them sat in a living room and heard the plan. Bill remembered (A.A. Comes of Age): "Paid workers, they said, would kill our good will with alcoholics: this would be sheer ruin. If we went into the hospital business, everybody would say it was a racket." The founders pushed back, and a vote was taken. The plan passed by the barest majority. Years later Bill admitted the minority had been right about the paid missionaries and the big business, and wrote: "Though I did not see it then, the group conscience of Alcoholics Anonymous was already at work hammering out right decisions for A.A.'s future." Notice the order. The founders proposed. The members decided, and the losing side was heard all the same. Nine years later the Long Form wrote it down (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Test it tonight: ask whether your group's members, not its founders or its hosts, decided the last big change in your room.
*Sit with:* In 1937 the founders proposed and eighteen Akron members decided. Who decided the last big change in my room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy — the Akron meeting, 1937).

### February 8 — "Another's Conception" · *(the threat today)*
The words "a loving God" in Tradition Two give no member the right to tell the group what God wants, and they ask no member what he believes. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Where does that authority show up? In the group conscience: the members deciding together. Not in one person's prayer or one person's certainty. The Big Book had already refused to make anyone's faith the measure (p. 46): "Much to our relief, we discovered we did not need to consider another's conception of God." A conception is an idea of something. A believer, a doubter and someone who believes nothing can sit at the same business meeting, and each one's vote counts the same. The rumour turns this upside down. It says Tradition Two is about your private faith, or that whoever sounds most spiritual should settle the room's business. Today it shows up as a chair who ends a debate by announcing what God wants, or a program that dresses its rules in spiritual words so nobody argues. Test it tonight: ask whether anyone in your room has ever closed a vote by claiming to know God's will.
*Sit with:* Tradition Two puts the authority in the group's decision, not in any one person's idea of God. Has anyone in my room used God's name to end a vote?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 46 (We Agnostics).

### February 9 — "A Brand-New Committee" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group committee that starts making rules for members has stopped serving the group and started governing it. Bill W. described what an A.A. committee is (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 38): "they have only the authorization to serve, never to command. Each committee carries out what it believes to be the wishes of its group. That is all." Then what happened when committees forgot: "Though A.A. committees used to try to discipline wayward members, though they have sometimes composed minute rules and regulations and now and then have set themselves up as judges of other people's personal morals, I know of no case where any of these seemingly worthy strivings had any lasting effect — except, perhaps, the election of a brand-new committee!" Minute rules means tiny ones. Picture it. The steering committee posts a list: shares capped at three minutes, phones in a basket at the door, latecomers at the back. Nobody voted on it. Each rule sounds sensible, which is exactly how a committee drifts from serving to governing. The remedy is the one Bill named, and it belongs to the group: a new committee. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) puts the decision in "our group conscience," not with whoever holds the clipboard. Test it tonight: ask which of the rules in your room the group actually voted on.
*Sit with:* An A.A. committee has the authority to serve and never to command. Which rules in my room did the group itself vote on?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 38 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### February 10 — "No Orders" · *(how it gets captured)*
When a facility supplies the person who runs the meeting, the group loses what Tradition Two guarantees it: the power to say who serves and on what terms. Bill W. described an A.A. group's committee once the members have taken charge of their own affairs (A.A. Comes of Age): "This committee can only make the necessary service arrangements by which the group can function. It can give no spiritual advice, judge no one's conduct, issue no orders." Its members, he wrote, are "servants, not senators." And: "Thus throughout our fellowship does the group conscience decree the terms upon which its leaders shall serve." Decree means sets by ruling. Now look at a meeting run on a program's schedule. The person in the chair is staff. She was hired, not elected. She may hand out advice, judge who has been compliant, and sign attendance sheets for the court. No vote put her there and no vote can remove her; her boss can. That room may read the Traditions aloud every week, but the authority the Long Form names (Big Book, p. 563), "a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience," has no group conscience to speak through. Test it tonight: ask who chose the person chairing your meeting, and who could replace them.
*Sit with:* In A.A. the group sets the terms its leaders serve on, and a hired chair answers to someone else. Who chose the person chairing my meeting?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — Tradition Nine).

### February 11 — "Caretakers Only" · *(the threat)*
The danger Tradition Two closes at the top of A.A. is a headquarters that starts to rule, and Bill W. shut that door from inside the headquarters itself. Writing in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 39): "Here at the General Office we have long known that we can merely supply certain indispensable services." Then the line: "It is most clearly apparent that, even here at the very center of A.A., there can only exist a center of service — custodians, editors, secretaries and the like — each, to be sure, with a special vital function, but none of them with any authority to govern Alcoholics Anonymous." A custodian looks after something that belongs to someone else. On the next page he named the threat (p. 40): "So long as we avoid any menacing accumulation of wealth or the growth of personal government at these centers, we cannot go astray." Menacing means threatening. So the Long Form keeps the authority with the groups (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Today the claim to rule from above comes as an office or a facility telling a group what it must do. Test it tonight: ask what your group was last told it had to do, and who told it.
*Sit with:* Even A.A.'s own headquarters called itself a center of service with no authority to govern. Who last told my group what it had to do?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, pp. 39–40 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### February 12 — "Collective Voice" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was a Tradition Two, A.A.'s first members wrote their book and named it the way a group decides: together, by vote. Bill W. wrote in 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 41) that the Big Book "was the product of thousands of hours of discussion. It truly represented the collective voice, heart and conscience of those of us who had pioneered the first four years of A.A." Collective means shared by all of them. Even the title went to the members (p. 42): "Thinking up titles and voting upon them at meetings became one of our main activities." The Akron and New York groups voted, and a narrow majority chose a different name, The Way Out. It changed only when a member found twelve books already had that title, and Alcoholics Anonymous became the first choice. The book that carries the message was not one man's work handed down. It was a group conscience on paper. The Long Form, printed seven years after the book, names the same authority (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Today a group's readings and format are sometimes picked by whoever hosts the room. Test it tonight: ask whether your group voted on what is read aloud at your meeting.
*Sit with:* The Big Book was written and named by the votes of its first members. Did my group vote on what is read aloud in my room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, pp. 41–42 (Bill W., "Anonymity", 1946).

### February 13 — "Properly Informed" · *(the threat today)*
A group conscience can only decide well when the members have the facts, so a business meeting with the facts kept from it is not deciding anything. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "Harder still to accept was the now proven fact that the conscience of the group, when properly informed of the facts and issues and principles involved, was often wiser than any leader, self-appointed or not." Read the condition in the middle: properly informed. The group's wisdom depends on seeing what it is deciding. The Long Form gives that informed group the final say (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." The threat today is the vote held in the dark. The treasurer reads a balance, but nobody sees where the money went. The rent is taken care of, and nobody asks by whom. A meeting is told its time is changing, as an announcement, with no reason given. In a facility room the business meeting may not exist at all, and the program's decisions arrive as news. A group that cannot see its own money, rent and arrangements isn't deciding. Someone else is, and the group is being told. Test it tonight: ask to see your group's treasurer's report and minutes, and notice how the room answers.
*Sit with:* Bill said the group conscience is wiser than any leader when it is properly informed of the facts. Can the members of my group see their own money and minutes?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — Tradition Two).

### February 14 — "Passed By at Election" · *(how a group breaks it)*
An election is a group's brake on a leader who starts to boss, and a group breaks Tradition Two when it stops using it. Bill W. wrote in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 38): "Nowhere in A.A. is there to be seen any constituted human authority that can compel an A.A. group to do anything." Constituted means officially set up. Then he explained why a leader can't become one: "Some A.A. groups, for example, elect their leaders. But even with such a mandate each leader soon discovers that while he can always guide by example or persuasion he can never boss, else at election time he may find himself passed by." A mandate is the permission a vote gives. Notice what that depends on: an election time. Picture a group whose secretary has held the job six years because nobody else ran and nobody called the vote. She now picks the readings, the speakers and who signs at the bank. She wasn't a boss when the group elected her. The group made her one by never voting again. The Long Form keeps the decision with the members (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Test it tonight: ask when your group last elected its secretary, and when it will next.
*Sit with:* A leader can never boss a group that still holds its elections. When did my group last elect its secretary?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 38 (Bill W., "Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?", 1947).

### February 15 — "Authorized People" · *(how it gets captured)*
Tradition Two takes the last word in a group away from every person, and a facility-run meeting quietly hands it back to staff. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Group conscience means the members of the group deciding together, at their own business meeting. Bill W. explained how A.A. could live without bosses (A.A. Comes of Age): "Other societies have to have law and force and sanction and punishment, administered by authorized people. Happily for us, we found we need no human authority whatever." A sanction is a penalty. Now look at a meeting held inside a treatment program. Staff pick the time and the topic. Staff decide who may attend, and who gets written up for missing it. A client who walks out early can lose a privilege. That room has law, force, sanction and punishment, and authorized people to run all four. It may read the Steps aloud and call itself A.A., but no group conscience ever sat down to decide anything, because there is no group there, only a program. Test it tonight: ask who in your room could punish you for what you say or for walking out, and whether the members ever voted to give them that power.
*Sit with:* A.A. found it needed no human authority at all, and a program meeting brings one in with the staff. Who in my room holds a power the members never gave them?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy).

### February 16 — "Danger-Fraught Issues" · *(the threat)*
When Bill W. sat down in 1946 to put the Traditions into words, he named authority as one of the dangers they had to answer. He wrote, just before the first draft of the Twelve Points (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 6): "Terribly relevant is the problem of our basic structure and our attitude toward those ever-pressing questions of leadership, money, and authority." Then the stakes: "Our final destiny will almost surely hang upon what we presently decide to do with these danger-fraught issues!" Danger-fraught means loaded with danger. A fellowship of drunks with no owner had to settle who decides, or someone else would settle it for them. Tradition Two is the answer he printed on the next page, and the Long Form keeps it (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Not a founder, a board or a boss: the members of the group, deciding together. The question has not gone away. It comes up every time a room gets a new host, a new chairperson or a new rule, and somebody answers it, whether the members are there or not. Test it tonight: ask who decided the last change in how your meeting runs, and whether the members were in the room when it was decided.
*Sit with:* In 1946 Bill named authority as one of the dangers A.A.'s future hung on, and Tradition Two handed it to the group conscience. Who answered that question in my room the last time it came up?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 6 (Bill W., 1946, introducing the Twelve Points).

### February 17 — "Not a Word Changed" · *(before the tradition)*
Before Tradition Two was written, the people around the table overruled the man writing A.A.'s book, and the Twelve Steps are better for it. Just before the manuscript was finished, four of them sat in an office in Newark, still arguing over the Steps. Bill W. remembered (A.A. Comes of Age): "All this time I had refused to budge on these steps. I would not change a word of the original draft". His draft used the word God throughout, and in one place had alcoholics praying on their knees. Henry, who had worked on the book with him, fought it hard, sure it would scare drinkers away by the thousands. Little by little the others came around to Henry's side. The words that came out of that fight are the ones in the Steps today: a Power greater than ourselves, and God as we understood Him, with the Steps offered as suggestions. Bill called it "the great contribution of our atheists and agnostics." Nobody pulled rank. The writer held the pen, and the room still decided. That is Tradition Two before it had a number: no single person, however much they have done, holds the last word for the group. Test it tonight: when your group next decides something, ask whether the member who disagreed most was actually heard, or only outnumbered.
*Sit with:* The man writing the book would not change a word, and the people around him changed it anyway. When my group decides, does the one who disagrees get heard?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy — the Steps argued in Newark).

### February 18 — "For Our Group Purpose" · *(the threat today)*
Tradition Two opens with four words that limit everything after them: for our group purpose. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." The authority it names settles the group's business: where it meets, what happens to the basket money, how the meeting runs, who holds which job. It is not a ruling on what any member has to believe. The rumour runs the other way. A newcomer hears that the Second Tradition means he must believe in God before he belongs, or that a group gets a say over his faith. The Big Book says the opposite, in the chapter on carrying the message to the man still drinking (p. 95): "If he thinks he can do the job in some other way, or prefers some other spiritual approach, encourage him to follow his own conscience. We have no monopoly on God; we merely have an approach that worked with us." A monopoly is sole ownership. So the group's conscience runs the group's affairs, and each member's conscience stays his own. Test it tonight: if anyone in your room has told a newcomer what he has to believe to stay, ask them where the Long Form gives a group that power.
*Sit with:* Tradition Two gives a group authority over its own business, not over anyone's beliefs. Has my room ever told a newcomer what to believe?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 95 ("Working With Others").

### February 19 — "Nobody Called a Business Meeting" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can lose Tradition Two without anyone taking it, simply by never meeting to decide anything. Picture a meeting that has run on Tuesday nights for years. The secretary has held the job so long that nobody remembers electing her. She picks the readings, the format, and who leads each week. The basket money goes into an envelope she keeps at home. Nobody objects, because she is kind and reliable and does all the work. But there has not been a business meeting in three years, and when a newer member asks for one, the answer is that it isn't needed. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." A conscience that never gathers can't speak. Group conscience is not a feeling in the room. It is the members sitting down together, hearing the facts, and deciding. Without that, the meeting has an owner by default, even a gentle one, and whoever takes her chair next inherits the ownership along with the envelope. Test it tonight: ask when your group last held a business meeting, who called it, and whether any member there could have changed anything.
*Sit with:* A group conscience that never meets can't decide anything, and someone ends up owning the meeting. When did my group last sit down to decide its own business?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563).

### February 20 — "Serve but Never Govern" · *(how it gets captured)*
When a program pays someone to run a meeting, the person at the front works for the program, not for the group. The Big Book's foreword sums up what the early groups settled on (p. xix): "that our leaders might serve but never govern; that each group was to be autonomous and there was to be no professional class of therapy." Autonomous means self-governing. A professional class of therapy means people paid to treat alcoholics inside A.A. The Long Form puts the authority in the group (Big Book, p. 563): "one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Now watch the capture. A recovery program sends a paid coach to chair the meeting. The coach takes attendance for the program, steers the sharing toward the program's lessons, and reports back to the office. The members can't remove the coach, because the members never chose the coach. A leader the group can't replace isn't serving the group. That leader is governing it, on someone else's payroll. Nothing about the room has to look different for this to happen; the chairs, the readings and the basket all stay. Only the answer to who decides has changed. Test it tonight: ask who chose the person chairing your meeting, who pays them if anyone does, and whether the members could replace them next week.
*Sit with:* A leader the group can't replace is governing, not serving. Could the members of my group replace the person at the front next week?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); Foreword to the Second Edition (Big Book, p. xix).

### February 21 — "A College of Cardinals" · *(the threat)*
The danger Tradition Two answers did not only rise from the groups; for years it sat at the top of A.A., and Bill W. said so. At St. Louis in 1955, as the old-timers handed A.A.'s world services over to the groups, he described the old arrangement (A.A. Comes of Age): "When we first put our world services into effect, Dr. Bob and I and our old-time friends were self-appointed guardians and custodians of this society." Self-appointed means nobody chose them. He went further: "our setup of those days was a sort of a double-headed papacy and a college of cardinals." A papacy is rule from one head, and the cardinals are the circle around it. They did good work, but answered to no one below them. The fix was to make the top answer downward. The new Conference, Bill said, "will represent the conscience of A.A. world-wide, to which our Trustees will in this very hour become directly accountable." That is the Long Form carried all the way up (Big Book, p. 563): the "one ultimate authority" speaks "in our group conscience," and no office outranks it. Today the papacy can be any office, committee or intergroup that starts telling groups what they must do. Test it tonight: if any office or committee has sent your group an instruction, ask who elected it and who it answers to.
*Sit with:* Even A.A.'s founders were once a hierarchy that answered to no one, until they made it answer to the groups. Does anyone outside my group give it orders?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (St. Louis, 1955: the Third Legacy handed to the Conference).

### February 22 — "Their Right Size" · *(before the tradition)*
As the first groups grew, every job they needed could breed a boss, and the members had to keep the jobholders in their place. Somebody had to collect the money and bank it, so there was a treasurer; then a secretary, sometimes a chairman. Bill W. described what came with them (A.A. Comes of Age): "Absolutely necessary arrangements had to be made and people had to be appointed or elected to do the jobs. This often brought friction and power-driving". A power-driver is someone pushing to be in charge. Then: "Means had to be found to keep friction-makers and power-drivers down to their right size." What worked, he wrote, was a service committee, a good deal of pressure from the members, and much love and understanding. Nobody was made boss to fix the bosses. The group handled them itself, and the jobs stayed jobs. The Long Form later wrote down where the last word had been all along (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Today the drift is the same: whoever holds the bank card, the keys or the meeting list starts making the decisions. Test it tonight: ask whether each job in your group still answers to the members, and when the members last said so.
*Sit with:* The first groups learned that every job could breed a boss, and they kept the jobholders to their right size themselves. Does every job in my group still answer to the members?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy — the first group services).

### February 23 — "Respect, Not a Title" · *(the threat today)*
A title in an A.A. group gives no power over anyone, and the rumour that it does is how rooms end up with bosses. The Long Form of Tradition Two puts the only authority in the group (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority". The Long Form of Tradition Nine says what the people holding the titles get instead (p. 565): "Universal respect is the key to their usefulness." Universal means from everyone. So a secretary, a chairperson or a treasurer is useful because the members trust them, and only for as long as the members do. Today the rumour runs the other way. Some rooms have a facilitator, a group leader or a coordinator who acts as if the title came with rights: to cut a share short, to pick who reads, to decide who is welcome and who is a problem. Respect that has to be demanded is already gone, and what is left is a boss with a name tag. The group never has to put up with that. It can hold an election and hand the job to someone else. Test it tonight: ask your group's officers when they were elected, how long they serve, and when the next election is.
*Sit with:* A title in A.A. carries no power, only the respect the members give. When was the person running my meeting elected, and when does their term end?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); long form, Tradition 9 (Big Book, p. 565).

### February 24 — "The Close Vote" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can keep the letter of Tradition Two, take a vote, and still break itself, by forcing a split decision through on one count. Picture a business meeting with thirteen members there. The motion is to move the meeting to a new night. Six want it, six don't, one shrugs, and it passes seven to six. By spring the six have stopped coming, and two of them have started a meeting across town. Nobody broke a rule. But the group's conscience is the members together, and half of them just walked out. A.A. learned this the hard way. When Bill W. and the old-timers planned the elections for A.A.'s first General Service Conference, they named the danger (A.A. Comes of Age): "Another prime cause of grief was the hotly contested close election, which nearly always left behind a large and discontented minority." So they asked for a two-thirds vote, a margin big enough that the losing side could live with it. The Long Form names where a group's authority lives (Big Book, p. 563): "a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." A seven-to-six split is a room that hasn't finished deciding. Test it tonight: when your group divides nearly in half, ask whether the question can wait a month for more talk instead of a vote.
*Sit with:* A close vote can win a motion and lose half the group. When my group splits down the middle, do we push it through or keep talking?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy — planning the Conference elections).

### February 25 — "Nothing Can Compel a Group" · *(how it gets captured)*
No one outside an A.A. group can order it to do anything, and the capture starts when a group forgets that and obeys. Bill W. put the rule for all of A.A. in two sentences (A.A. Comes of Age): "Therefore it is now clear that we ought never to name boards to govern us. It is equally clear, however, that we shall always need to authorize workers to serve us." To authorize is to give someone permission to act. If A.A. itself has nobody who can give a group orders, a landlord certainly doesn't. Here is how it goes. A group rents a room in a facility. The facility starts adding conditions: its clients speak first, a staff member sits in, the meeting ends early to fit the facility's schedule, and the facility's handouts go on the table. Each request is small and the rent is cheap, so the group says yes, and one day the facility's policy is the meeting's format. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." A group that can't say no to its landlord can always vote to find another room. Test it tonight: ask whether any rule in your meeting came from the building, rather than from a vote of the members.
*Sit with:* A.A. never names anyone to govern a group, and no building can do it either, unless the group lets it. Did any rule in my meeting come from the landlord?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — Tradition Nine).

### February 26 — "Righteous Indignation" · *(the threat)*
The most dangerous grab for power in a group comes from the member who is sure he's right. Bill W. named the forces the Traditions hold back (A.A. Comes of Age): "These were the desires for power, for domination, for glory, and for money. They were all the more dangerous because they were invariably powered by self-righteousness, self-justification, and the destructive power of anger, usually masquerading as righteous indignation." Masquerading means wearing a disguise. Righteous indignation is anger that feels holy. Nobody takes over a group by saying they want control. They say the group is going wrong and somebody has to fix it. Then they fix it themselves: change the format without a vote, swap the readings, decide who is really A.A. and who isn't. Every step feels like protecting the program. The Long Form shuts that door by naming the only authority there is (Big Book, p. 563): "a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." That leaves no room for one member's certainty, however sincere. A member who is right can bring it to the business meeting and persuade the others. A member who won't wait for the vote has stopped serving the group and started ruling it. Test it tonight: the next time someone says the group has to be saved from itself, ask them to put it to a vote.
*Sit with:* The urge to run a group usually arrives dressed as saving it. Who in my room is fixing the group without asking the group?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy).

### February 27 — "We Ought, Not You Must" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was a Tradition Two, A.A.'s first book had its orders taken out wherever possible, so that nobody in it would stand over the reader. When copies of the manuscript went out for comment in 1939, a psychiatrist in Montclair, New Jersey, Dr. Howard, sent back a criticism: the text was full of you and must. Bill W. remembered (A.A. Comes of Age): "His idea was to remove all forms of coercion". Coercion means forcing someone. Bill argued against it at first, then gave in: "it was perfectly apparent that the doctor was dead right." So the book mostly speaks as we, telling what we did and what we found, and leaves the reader free to take it or leave it. The Traditions grew from the same root. The Long Form gives a group one authority (Big Book, p. 563): "a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." No member, chairperson or old-timer gets a you-must over anyone. Yet in some rooms today, must is everywhere: you must get a home group, you must do ninety meetings, you must check in every day, or you're out. Those are orders, and the book itself had them edited out. Test it tonight: count how many times you hear "you must" in your meeting, and ask who gave the speaker the authority to say it.
*Sit with:* A.A.'s book took out the you-musts so nobody would stand over the reader. Who in my room hands out orders that the book itself refused to give?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy — the mimeograph copies and Dr. Howard).

### February 28 — "Through the Group" · *(the threat today)*
Tradition Two does not let any one person speak for God to a group, and that is the part most often turned around. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "For our group purpose there is but one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." Read where the voice comes from: the group conscience, the members deciding together. Not the chairperson. Not the oldest member. Not a counselor telling a room what God wants from it. The Tradition is about who decides for a group, not what anyone believes, and it works the same for the member who prays, the one who doubts and the one who doesn't believe at all. The Big Book is plain about that in its chapter on carrying the message (p. 93): "If the man be agnostic or atheist, make it emphatic that he does not have to agree with your conception of God." Emphatic means said strongly and clearly. Today the twist sounds like this: someone says God has shown them what the group should do, so anyone who argues is arguing with God. That is personal authority in religious clothes, and the Long Form gives it nothing. Test it tonight: when anyone in your room claims to know God's will for the group, ask them to bring it to the business meeting and let the members decide.
*Sit with:* No one member speaks for God to the group; the group decides together. Has anyone in my room used God's name to end an argument?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 93 ("Working With Others").

### February 29 — "The Sole Authority" · *(how a group breaks it)*
The month ends with the oldest way a group gives Tradition Two away: it gets comfortable and lets one person decide. Nobody plans it. One member always knows what to do, so the others stop coming to business meetings. The literature order, the rent, the format, the anniversary cakes: one person handles it all, and the group is grateful. Years go by, and the group has a conscience on paper and an owner in fact. A.A. as a whole once faced the same question: could the members really be trusted to run their own affairs? At the first General Service Conference in 1951, delegates chosen by the groups took on hard questions the headquarters people had been unsure about. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "Though their advice was sometimes contrary to our own views, we saw they were frequently right." And then: "Our group conscience could safely act as the sole authority and sure guide for Alcoholics Anonymous." Sole means only. That line is the whole month, and the Long Form said it first (Big Book, p. 563): "one ultimate authority—a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience." It only works when the members show up and decide. Test it tonight: go to your group's next business meeting, and if there isn't one, ask for it.
*Sit with:* A.A. found its group conscience could safely be the sole authority, but only because the members showed up. Will I be at my group's next business meeting?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 2 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy — the first General Service Conference, 1951).

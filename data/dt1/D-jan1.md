### January 1 — "Emotional Dynamite" · *(the threat)*
A fellowship of drunks was supposed to blow itself apart, and Tradition One is the reason it didn't. Bill W. remembered what the experts said when A.A. was new (A.A. Comes of Age): "This fellowship of alcoholics is emotional dynamite. Its neurotic content can blow it to bits." Neurotic means ruled by fear and nerves. The early members were asking the same thing: "would we blow up or could we stay together?" The Long Form gives the answer they found (Big Book, p. 563): "Each member of Alcoholics Anonymous is but a small part of a great whole. A.A. must continue to live or most of us will surely die. Hence our common welfare comes first. But individual welfare follows close afterward." Read the order. The group first, because the drunk who calls tonight needs a room that is still standing. The member close behind, never thrown away. The danger was never one person with an opinion. It was a room splitting into camps until nobody was left to answer the phone. Today the charge is often lit from outside: a program wants the meeting, and the members split over whether to let it have it. Test it tonight: if your group blew apart tomorrow, which piece would the next drunk still find with its doors open?
*Sit with:* The experts said a fellowship of drunks would blow itself to bits, and Tradition One is how it held. Is anything in my group pulling the room into camps right now?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy).

### January 2 — "Counting Noses" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was a word of Tradition, two men counted their sober friends and got scared about whether they could stay together. A November day in 1937, Dr. Bob's living room in Akron. Bill W. told it at St. Louis (A.A. Comes of Age): "All told we figured that upwards of forty alcoholics were staying bone dry." They wept for joy. Then the fear came: "Would the forces that everywhere divide society finally invade and smash us up". The Steps were not yet written and the book was scarcely an idea. There were no rules to lean on, only the question: "How could we possibly hang together and cling to our new life line". A life line is the rope thrown to someone drowning. Nine years later that fear became the second sentence of the Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "A.A. must continue to live or most of us will surely die." Not should. Must. Forty sober drunks were very nearly the whole of A.A. that afternoon, and if they split, there was nothing left for the forty-first. Today the forty-first is still out there, and a room is still all that stands between him and the bottle. Test it tonight: count the noses in your room, and ask what would be left for the next one through the door if this group came apart.
*Sit with:* Forty sober drunks were nearly all of A.A. once, and their first fear was coming apart. What would be left for the next one if my group split?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Bill's opening talk at St. Louis: Dr. Bob's living room, 1937).

### January 3 — "A Small Part" · *(the threat today)*
Tradition One makes every A.A. group a small part of one whole, and a meeting that belongs to something else can't keep that promise. The Long Form opens (Big Book, p. 563): "Each member of Alcoholics Anonymous is but a small part of a great whole." The whole is named: Alcoholics Anonymous. Not a clinic, not a recovery program, not a county service. The rumour runs the other way. In rooms run by facilities, unity gets taught as getting along with the house: keep to the schedule, don't question the staff, don't make trouble for the program that lets you meet. That is unity with a business. The short form says whose unity a drunk's life hangs on (Big Book, p. 562): "personal recovery depends upon A.A. unity." A.A. unity means groups that answer to their own members, joined to every other group by the same Traditions, with no owner, no contract and no fee. A meeting on a facility's schedule is also a small part of a great whole. The whole is the facility. Its staff can move the time, change the format or close the door, and none of them answers to the drunks in the chairs. Test it tonight: ask what your meeting is a small part of, and who could close it.
*Sit with:* The Long Form names the whole every group belongs to, and it is A.A., not a program. What is my meeting really a part of?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); short form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 562).

### January 4 — "Rocking the Raft" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can disagree as hard as it likes; it breaks Tradition One when a fight hardens into two camps and the newcomer is caught between them. Bill W. compared the early days to a crew adrift in the Pacific after their plane went down (A.A. Comes of Age): "Nobody dared rock that raft, lest they all perish." Lest means for fear that. Then he admitted: "But some of our sick and careless members did rock the raft, and that scared us to death." Here is how a group rocks it now. The business meeting votes to move to a bigger room, nine to six. The six don't let it go. It comes back at the next business meeting, then at the coffee urn, then in shares. By spring the six have opened a meeting of their own, same night, same hour, two doors down. A new group is no harm; A.A. grows that way. The war is the harm. The man who came in that winter gets two phone numbers, and each one warns him about the other. The Long Form never asks a group to stop arguing. It asks what comes first (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence our common welfare comes first." Test it tonight: ask whether any old vote in your group is still being fought in front of the newcomers.
*Sit with:* Disagreement doesn't sink a group; camps do, with the newcomer in the middle. Is an old fight in my group still being carried into the meeting?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy, Tradition One: the raft).

### January 5 — "Unity With Whom?" · *(how it gets captured)*
The short form's second half gets borrowed by programs that want the drunk to depend on them, and the Long Form takes it back. The short form (Big Book, p. 562): "personal recovery depends upon A.A. unity." Notice the letters: A.A. Not the program's, not the counsellor's, not the aftercare plan's. In a facility the line gets rewritten out loud: stay connected to us, stay in our groups, come back to our alumni meeting, because your recovery depends on it. The client hears Tradition One. What he is being sold is loyalty to a business. The Long Form says why unity matters, and the reason is the drunk, not a head count (Big Book, p. 563): "A.A. must continue to live or most of us will surely die." Bill W. said it again in A.A.'s pamphlet on the Traditions (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 6): "Else, save few exceptions, we and our brother alcoholics throughout the world will surely resume the hopeless journey to oblivion." Oblivion means being blotted out, gone. A.A.'s unity keeps free rooms alive for that brother. A program's unity keeps its own seats filled. Test it tonight: when someone in your room says unity, ask unity with whom, and who gets paid when you keep it.
*Sit with:* Tradition One says recovery depends on A.A. unity, and a program can borrow the words while selling loyalty to itself. When my room talks about unity, unity with whom?
*Grounded in:* Short form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 562); long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 6 (Bill W.).

### January 6 — "Who Next?" · *(the threat)*
The first thing early A.A. feared would kill it was the slip, and the groups learned it couldn't, so long as the door stayed open. Bill W. (A.A. Comes of Age): "At first nearly every alcoholic we approached began to slip, if indeed he sobered up at all." A slip is a drink after getting sober. Each one hit the whole room. They would look at each other and wonder who was next: "We were afraid that alcohol might completely undo us." Years later Bill could say what the groups had learned. "Alcohol always threatens the individual," but, he went on, "we know that it cannot destroy the common welfare." That is the Long Form's last two sentences at work (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence our common welfare comes first. But individual welfare follows close afterward." The group outlives the slip, and the man who slipped is close behind, welcome back the night he walks in. Today the slip often gets treated the other way. In facility programs a drink can mean discharge, and a meeting run on the same house rules can leave a man feeling he has lost his seat. A.A.'s door was never the facility's door. Test it tonight: if a member came back tonight after drinking, would anything your group does make it harder for him to sit down?
*Sit with:* A slip can kill a man but it cannot kill the group, so the group keeps a seat for him. Would the one who slipped find his seat waiting in my room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy, Tradition One: the fear of slips).

### January 7 — "Every Contestant" · *(before the tradition)*
Tradition One was not handed down from above; it was dug out of the mail from groups that were fighting. By 1945 letters about group problems filled the files in New York. Bill W. (A.A. Comes of Age): "It seemed as if every contestant in every group argument wrote us during this confused and exciting period." A contestant is someone on one side of a fight. Then: "The basic ideas for the Twelve Traditions of Alcoholics Anonymous came directly out of this vast correspondence." And: "A code of traditions could not, of course, ever become rule or law. But it might act as a guide for our Trustees, Headquarters people, and especially for A.A. groups with growing pains." The first version went into print in the Grapevine in May 1946, the Long Form you can still read (Big Book, p. 563): "Each member of Alcoholics Anonymous is but a small part of a great whole." It was written for groups, out of groups' own wreckage, so the next group would not have to learn it by losing members. Today it is rarely read where it was meant to be used, the business meeting, and rooms run by others hold no business meeting at all. Test it tonight: ask when your group last read the Long Form aloud at a business meeting, or whether it holds one.
*Sit with:* Tradition One was written from the letters of groups that were fighting, as a guide for groups. Does my group ever read it where it was meant to be used?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy: the letters and the first Traditions).

### January 8 — "Close Afterward" · *(the threat today)*
Tradition One puts the group first, and in the very next breath refuses to throw the member away. The Long Form's last sentence (Big Book, p. 563): "But individual welfare follows close afterward." Close afterward means right behind, not forgotten. The rumour leaves that sentence off. Then unity becomes a club: the member who asks who pays for the room, or why staff chair the meeting, is told he is hurting unity and should keep his head down. That is conformity, and the Long Form rules it out a few lines further down the same page, in Tradition Three: A.A. membership must never "depend upon money or conformity." Bill W. put the order in plain words (A.A. Comes of Age): "Probably no society sets a higher value on the personal welfare of the individual member than does A.A." And of all twelve Traditions: "The Traditions guarantee the equality of all members and the independence of all groups." Equal members, independent groups. A room where questions about who runs it are out of order is not protecting A.A. unity; it is protecting whoever runs the room. Test it tonight: ask out loud who decides how your meeting is run, and notice whether anyone tells you the question hurts unity.
*Sit with:* The Long Form puts the member close behind the group, and never asks him to stop asking questions. Who in my room has been told a question hurts unity?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy).

### January 9 — "Violently Took Sides" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group breaks Tradition One when it turns its business meeting into a court over members' private lives. Early A.A. learned this through what Bill W. called the triangle, an affair between members. He described what followed (A.A. Comes of Age): "People violently took sides." Whole groups, he wrote, "got into uproars, and a number of people got drunk." Then: "We trembled for A.A.'s reputation and for its survival." Here is how it happens now. Two members, both married to other people, leave together one night, and the whole room knows by morning. At the next business meeting someone moves that the group take a position. It is about the group's good name, they say. It turns into an hour on who did what to whom. Half the room votes one way and half the other, and the group now has an official side in a private fight. Within a month four people have stopped coming, and one of them is drinking. Nothing in the Long Form makes a group the judge of anyone's marriage. The group's business is the common welfare (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence our common welfare comes first." The room the next drunk needs is not served by a verdict. Test it tonight: ask whether your business meeting has ever voted on a member's private life, and who stopped coming afterward.
*Sit with:* A group that takes sides in a private fight splits itself, and drunks pay for the verdict. Has my group ever made itself a judge of a member's private life?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy, Tradition One: the triangle).

### January 10 — "Glory and Money" · *(how it gets captured)*
The forces that take over an A.A. room from outside are the same four Bill W. found inside, and they always arrive looking righteous. He said early A.A. met threats worse than alcohol (A.A. Comes of Age): "These were the desires for power, for domination, for glory, and for money. They were all the more dangerous because they were invariably powered by self-righteousness, self-justification, and the destructive power of anger, usually masquerading as righteous indignation." Masquerading means wearing a disguise. Now look at a room taken over from outside. A facility gets power: its staff decide who chairs. It gets domination: the format is the program's. It gets glory: the brochure says it hosts A.A. And it gets money: the meeting is part of what a client's insurer or the government pays for. Every piece is explained as helping more alcoholics, and that explanation is the self-righteousness. The Long Form keeps the whole above any part of it (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence our common welfare comes first." A.A.'s welfare, not a facility's. A program can mean well and still want all four. Test it tonight: name who gains power, glory or money from your meeting existing, and ask whether any of them is a member of the group.
*Sit with:* Power, domination, glory and money are the four that split A.A., and they come in from outside dressed as help. Who gains any of the four from my meeting?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy, Tradition One: power, domination, glory, money).

### January 11 — "Ominous Foretaste" · *(the threat)*
The first great test of A.A. unity came from success, when a magazine article sent thousands of drunks looking for groups that barely existed. March 1941: Jack Alexander's piece in the Saturday Evening Post. Bill W. (A.A. Comes of Age): "A real Niagara then broke loose, and we were deluged with letters and with alcoholics by the thousands." Then the strain on the groups: "There had already been an ominous foretaste of the problems of mushrooming groups; quarrels over leadership, money, membership, clubs, exploitation of A.A.'s name, panhandling, and even romancing." Ominous means threatening; mushrooming means springing up fast. And as hundreds of new groups tried to form, "the specters of disunity and collapse grew to frightening proportions." A specter is a ghost. Every quarrel on that list is about a group's own affairs, and every one of them could split a room. The Long Form answers all of them with one order (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence our common welfare comes first." Not my leadership, my money, my club, my name. Today the same list comes back whenever a meeting grows fast or gets noticed: who runs it, who holds the money, whose name is on the door. Test it tonight: pick one word from Bill's list and ask whether it is a fight in your group right now.
*Sit with:* A.A.'s first big test of unity was a flood of new drunks and a list of quarrels that could split any group. Which quarrel on that list is alive in my group?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy: the Saturday Evening Post article).

### January 12 — "Wrecked in the Same Vessel" · *(before the tradition)*
Before any Tradition was written, the first groups held together because the next man in the door mattered more than anyone's rank. The Big Book, written in 1939, describes the early fellowship (Big Book, p. 161): "No one is too discredited or has sunk too low to be welcomed cordially—if he means business. Social distinctions, petty rivalries and jealousies—these are laughed out of countenance." Countenance here means approval: rivalries got laughed out of the room. Then the reason, in two pieces: "Being wrecked in the same vessel" and "with hearts and minds attuned to the welfare of others". Attuned means tuned in, like a radio. The welfare of others came first because the newcomer was the whole point of meeting. Seven years later the Long Form wrote down the same order (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence our common welfare comes first. But individual welfare follows close afterward." Nobody had to vote on it then. They were busy putting new men in the hospital and visiting them afterward. Today the rivalries that early A.A. laughed off come back as rank: sober time, service titles, which program you came out of. Test it tonight: look at who sits where in your room, and ask whether any rivalry there is being taken seriously instead of laughed out.
*Sit with:* The first groups laughed rivalries out of the room because the newcomer came first. Is any rank in my group being taken more seriously than the next drunk?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book pp. 159 and 161 (A Vision for You).

### January 13 — "The Powerful Cement" · *(the threat today)*
A shared disaster can fill a room, but the Big Book says it was never what held A.A. together. The Big Book (p. 17): "We are like the passengers of a great liner the moment after rescue from shipwreck when camaraderie, joyousness and democracy pervade the vessel from steerage to Captain's table." Steerage was the cheapest deck. Then the warning: "The feeling of having shared in a common peril is one element in the powerful cement which binds us. But that in itself would never have held us together as we are now joined." What held them was the next thing it names: "a common solution". Today many rooms are filled by something else again: a court order, a probation condition, a facility van that drops a dozen clients at seven and collects them at eight. The chairs are full, and the people in them share a requirement, not a peril or a solution. When the order runs out, they are gone, and a room built on them goes too. The Long Form asks for a whole that lives on its own (Big Book, p. 563): "A.A. must continue to live or most of us will surely die." A requirement can't keep a group alive. Test it tonight: if every court slip and facility van stopped tomorrow, would your group still have enough members to open its doors?
*Sit with:* Shared trouble fills a room for a while, but only a common solution holds a group together. What is holding my group together?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 17 (There Is a Solution).

### January 14 — "To Keep One Man" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group breaks Tradition One when it turns the Long Form's order around and puts one member's comfort ahead of the room. The order is plain (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence our common welfare comes first. But individual welfare follows close afterward." Here is how it gets turned around. The group has outgrown its room, and newcomers stand along the back wall. A bigger hall is free on the same night. The group's best-known member, twenty years sober, says he won't follow. He likes the small room; he says so at the business meeting and hints he will go somewhere else. Nobody wants to lose him, and the vote goes his way. Within a year the people who stood at the back have stopped coming, because there was never anywhere to sit, and the group is twelve old friends. Nobody was cruel. The group arranged itself around one man, and the next drunk was the one given up. The essay that opens the Traditions in the Big Book says what a group is for (Big Book, p. 561): "We alcoholics see that we must work together and hang together, else most of us will finally die alone." Most of us, not the best known of us. Test it tonight: ask whose comfort your group arranges itself around, and look at who is standing at the back.
*Sit with:* The Long Form puts the room first and the member close behind, and a group can turn that around without noticing. Whose comfort is my group arranged around?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 561 (The A.A. Tradition).

### January 15 — "Not on Pioneers" · *(how it gets captured)*
In 1950 A.A. decided its unity would rest on principles, not people, and a meeting held together by a facility's staff has gone back to resting on people. That July, A.A.'s first international convention, in Cleveland, confirmed the Traditions. Bill W. said why it had to be then (A.A. Comes of Age): "In the future we could no longer depend upon the authority and influence of our pioneering people for A.A. unity. Henceforth we would have to rely upon spiritual principles, as set forth in the Twelve Traditions." If A.A. could not lean on its own founders, a group cannot lean on a counsellor. Yet that is how many rooms are held now: one staff member unlocks the door, picks the reader and keeps the list. The members never meet on their own business, so there is no group conscience to fall back on. When the contract ends or the staff member moves on, the meeting ends too. Bill wrote, on linking A.A. to other projects (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 25): "to maintain our unity is surely our greatest obligation". The Long Form says why (Big Book, p. 563): "A.A. must continue to live or most of us will surely die." Test it tonight: if the person who unlocks your meeting's door left tomorrow, ask whether the members would know how to keep it open.
*Sit with:* A.A. stopped leaning on its founders in 1950 so that unity would never rest on a person again. Does my meeting rest on a person, or on its members?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy: the Cleveland Convention, 1950); P-17, p. 25 (Bill W., 1947).

### January 16 — "Worse Than the Worst" · *(the threat)*
The quickest way for a group to wreck its own unity is to try to protect it with rules. Bill W. described it in 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 12): "At this point the group enters the rule and regulation phase." Committees get the power to filter out undesirables. "Recalcitrants are cast into the outer darkness; respectable busybodies throw stones at the sinners." Recalcitrant means stubborn, refusing to fall in line. Then the result: "Most attempts at enforcement generate such waves of dissension and intolerance in the group that this condition is presently recognized to be worse for the group life than the very worst that the worst ever did." Dissension is open quarrelling. The rules meant to save the group split it. That is why the Long Form of Tradition One (Big Book, p. 563) puts "our common welfare" first and hands nobody a rulebook to enforce it with. A group stays whole by keeping its door open, not by guarding it. Today the rulebook often arrives ready-made: attendance sheets, behaviour contracts, a list of who may share and who may not, written by a program for its own clients. Test it tonight: ask whether your group has any rule that could put a drinker out, and who wrote it.
*Sit with:* Bill W. saw groups that enforced rules split worse than any drunk had ever split them. Does my group have a rule somebody is waiting to enforce?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 12 (Bill W., 1946: Who Is a Member of Alcoholics Anonymous?).

### January 17 — "Its Own Rights" · *(before the tradition)*
In the first groups the sacrifice for unity ran both ways: the members gave things up for the group, and the group gave things up for each member. Bill W. looked back on it (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 49): "We found that each of us had to make willing sacrifices for the group itself, sacrifices for the common welfare. The group, in turn, found that it had to give up many of its own rights for the protection and welfare of each member, and for A.A. as a whole." In turn. A group does not get to take everything and give nothing back. That is the Long Form's last sentence for Tradition One (Big Book, p. 563), written for groups: "Hence our common welfare comes first. But individual welfare follows close afterward." Close afterward means right behind. A member gives up his ambitions in the room. The group, in return, gives up its rights over him: no right to run his life, to charge him, to make him perform to belong. Those are the kinds of rights a group surrenders so that a drunk can walk in and stay. Today some rooms take them back quietly, a check-in sheet here, a required share there. Test it tonight: name one right over its members your group has given up, and ask whether anyone is taking it back.
*Sit with:* The first groups found that the group owed sacrifices to each member, not only the other way round. What right over its members has my group given up?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 49 (Bill W., Why Alcoholics Anonymous Is Anonymous).

### January 18 — "No Respecter of Persons" · *(the threat today)*
Tradition One makes every member the same size, and the threat today is a room that quietly sorts its people into two kinds. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Each member of Alcoholics Anonymous is but a small part of a great whole." Each member: not each member who chose to come, not each member in good standing. The Big Book's foreword says why the room can't be sorted (Big Book, p. xx): "Alcohol being no respecter of persons, we are an accurate cross section of America, and in distant lands, the same democratic evening-up process is now going on." No respecter of persons means it does not care who you are. Evening-up means levelling: whoever you were outside, in the room you are one more drunk. Now watch a meeting where a facility's van pulls up. The clients file in together, sit in a block, sign a sheet, and leave together before the coffee. The members sit on the other side. Nobody planned it, but there are now two rooms in one: people who are there because a program sent them, and people who belong. The client never learns that he belongs too. Test it tonight: look at where people sit, and ask whether a newcomer off a van would know this room is his.
*Sit with:* Alcohol does not sort people, so the room cannot either. Is there anyone in my meeting who sits there without knowing it is theirs?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. xx (Foreword to Second Edition).

### January 19 — "Couldn't Be Steered" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can split itself without a single argument, just by letting the same few friends keep the jobs. Picture it. The secretary hands the key to his friend, who hands it back a year later. The treasurer has been treasurer since anyone can remember. The real business happens at the friends' table after everyone else has gone home. Nobody is thrown out. The rest simply stop coming to anything but the meeting, and then some stop coming to that. Bill W. saw it at the first New York club, where the steering committees, he wrote (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 18), "tried to nominate their friends to succeed them" and found, to their dismay, that sober drunks would not be steered. A group run by a circle is really two groups: the circle, and everyone else. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) says each member "is but a small part of a great whole", and that includes the members holding the keys. Their part is no bigger for holding them. Today the circle often has an outside friend as well: the program that rents the room deals with the same two people every year, and the group's business gets done in its office. Test it tonight: ask when your group last put someone new in every service job.
*Sit with:* A group run by the same few friends has split in two, whether anyone noticed or not. When did my group last hand its jobs to someone new?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 18 (Bill W., 1947: Clubs in A.A.).

### January 20 — "At His Destination" · *(how it gets captured)*
A.A. stays one fellowship from town to town only while no group in it belongs to anyone else, and capture is what breaks that. In 1939 the book's writers set down a hope (Big Book, p. 162): "Some day we hope that every alcoholic who journeys will find a Fellowship of Alcoholics Anonymous at his destination." Not a fellowship. The same one. A drunk who got sober in one city could walk into a room in another and find the same thing: free, run by its members, belonging to the alcoholics in it. That sameness is what the Long Form calls "a great whole" (Big Book, p. 563). Every group is a small part of it, the way every member is. Now a man on a job far from home looks up a meeting and finds that the only one in town is held inside a treatment facility. The door is locked after eight. Staff run the format. A sign says the meeting is for residents and alumni. The schedule says A.A.; nothing else in the room does. Every room like it makes the name mean a little less, and the next traveller trusts it a little less. Test it tonight: if a stranger walked in from out of town, would he find the A.A. he left behind?
*Sit with:* A.A. is one fellowship everywhere only while no group belongs to anyone. Would a sober stranger from another city recognise my room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 162 (A Vision for You).

### January 21 — "Like People in a Sinking Ship" · *(the threat)*
The common welfare comes first because of the people who are not in the room yet, and the Big Book says where they are. Chapter 11 (Big Book, p. 152): "Near you, alcoholics are dying helplessly like people in a sinking ship." Near you, tonight. That is the weight behind the Long Form's second sentence (Big Book, p. 563): "A.A. must continue to live or most of us will surely die." Not a club that would be missed. A lifeboat. A group is one of the boats that goes out to the ship, and a group that sinks itself in a fight is one boat fewer. The fight is never about the drowning. It is about who chairs, whose format, whose literature, whose turn, and for the weeks it lasts the room is busy with itself. Nobody calls back the number on the list, because nobody is sure who has the phone. The man who walks in on the worst night of it hears shouting and decides he was right to stay away. Today's version is a room so taken up with its own business that the drunk becomes an interruption. Test it tonight: ask what your group's last big argument was about, and whether the drunk still out there came up in it once.
*Sit with:* The group comes first because alcoholics are drowning near it tonight. Has my group ever been too busy with itself to notice?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 152 (A Vision for You).

### January 22 — "Those Damned Traditions" · *(before the tradition)*
When the Traditions were first printed, most groups did not want them, and the only groups that listened were the ones already in trouble. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "When first published the Traditions got a mixed reception. Only groups in dire trouble took them seriously." The angriest objections came from groups with long lists of protective rules. The rest were bored. Bill went around speaking on them, and the letters came back asking him to tell his drinking story instead: "But please don't talk any more about those damned Traditions." So the groups that learned the Traditions first learned them the hard way, in the middle of trouble. Then the rest caught up: "Several years later it was recognized that the Twelve Traditions were going to be just as necessary to the life of our society" as the Steps were to each member. A group that waits for trouble before it reads Tradition One reads it too late, with half the room already gone. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) is four sentences long and can be read in under a minute. Today the Traditions are still the part people skip: rushed through at the top of the meeting, or not read at all. Test it tonight: ask your group when it last spent a business meeting on one Tradition, from start to finish.
*Sit with:* The first groups to take the Traditions seriously were the ones already falling apart. Would my group read Tradition One before trouble, or after?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (the Traditions' first reception).

### January 23 — "Nobody Can Compel" · *(the threat today)*
Nobody can enforce Tradition One on a group, and the rumour that somebody can is one of the ways rooms get taken over. Bill W. wrote in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 38): "Nowhere in A.A. is there to be seen any constituted human authority that can compel an A.A. group to do anything." Constituted means officially set up. No office, no committee, no old-timer, no staff member. So why do groups keep the Traditions at all? The same page: "The A.A. groups follow these other traditional principles for the very same reason that the individual A.A. follows the Twelve Steps to recovery. Groups see they would disintegrate if they didn't". Disintegrate means fall to pieces. A group keeps the Long Form's "our common welfare comes first" (Big Book, p. 563) because it has seen what happens when it doesn't, not because someone checks. Today the rumour runs the other way. A facility hands out its meeting guidelines and calls them A.A. rules. A staff member tells a room it will be shut down for breaking a Tradition. None of them has that power, and a group that believes they do has already given its conscience away. Test it tonight: when anyone says your group must do something, ask who in A.A. could make it.
*Sit with:* No one in A.A. can force a group to do anything; groups keep the Traditions because they have seen groups fall apart without them. Who does my group believe could shut it down?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 38 (Bill W., 1947: Will A.A. Ever Have a Personal Government?).

### January 24 — "Not Yet Reached" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group breaks Tradition One when it decides the common welfare means the people already in the room. Picture the business meeting. The hospital meeting on Thursdays is a long drive and only two members ever go. The group's number on the phone list rings at bad hours, and the member who answers it is tired. Someone moves that the group drop both. It passes, nine to three. The room is more comfortable by Friday, and no one in it is worse off. The ones worse off were not there to vote. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) says "our common welfare comes first", and the essay two pages earlier says whose welfare that is (Big Book, p. 561): "A.A. can, of course, mean just as much to uncounted alcoholics not yet reached." Uncounted means nobody knows how many. They are part of the common welfare before they ever find the door. A group that keeps itself comfortable by closing its windows is living for itself. Today the vote often comes with relief, because a program now runs the hospital meeting and the group feels free to stop. Test it tonight: ask what your group still does that reaches anyone who isn't already in the room.
*Sit with:* The common welfare includes alcoholics nobody in the room has met yet. What does my group do for the drunk who hasn't found it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 561 (The A.A. Tradition).

### January 25 — "Loan Out Our Goodwill" · *(how it gets captured)*
A group is most often captured through one of its own members: the one who loves A.A. and also works in the alcohol field. Bill W. described him in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 24): "He strongly feels that A.A. ought to place its name and financial credit squarely behind any first-rate research, hospital or educational project. Seeing that A.A. now makes the headlines, he argues that we should freely loan out our huge goodwill." Goodwill is the trust people have in a name. Bill added: "The judgment of this enthusiast is sometimes beclouded by the fact he wants to make a career." Beclouded means fogged over. His answer, on the same page: "neither A.A. as a whole nor any A.A. group ought to enter any activity other than straight A.A." Today the enthusiast is a counsellor or program manager who also has a home group. He brings his clients, offers the program's room for free, and puts the meeting on the program's brochure. Each step looks like help, and within a year the group answers to his employer. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence our common welfare comes first." His career is not part of it. Test it tonight: ask whether anyone in your group earns a living from the people it serves, and what the group has agreed to through him.
*Sit with:* Bill W. saw that the member most eager to lend out A.A.'s name was sometimes building a career. Has my group agreed to anything because a member's job asked it to?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 24 (Bill W., 1947: Dangers in Linking A.A. to Other Projects).

### January 26 — "Material Things" · *(the threat)*
Money is one of the oldest ways a group comes apart, and the early members saw it coming before they had a treasury to fight over. Bill W. wrote in 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 27): "We had no group funds, hence no group money troubles." Those were the first years: meetings in homes, coffee and sandwiches. Then groups rented rooms and opened clubs, and money came in with them. Bill named the danger: "Knowing that quarrels over material things have crushed the spirit of many a good undertaking, it is often thought that too much money may prove an evil for us too." Material things are money and property, as against the reason the group meets. A quarrel over the basket can empty a room faster than a quarrel over anything said in it: who spent what, who kept the receipts, whether the reserve should be bigger. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) puts "our common welfare" first, and the treasury exists for the common welfare, never the other way round. Today the money quarrel has a newer shape: a group with a large balance, or a facility offering to cover the rent, and the members split over who controls it. Test it tonight: ask how much money your group holds, and whether anyone can say what it is for.
*Sit with:* The first groups had no money and so had no money fights. What would my group be fighting over if the basket disappeared?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 27 (Bill W., 1946: Money).

### January 27 — "Led, Not Pushed" · *(before the tradition)*
The first A.A.s learned early that drunks cannot be held together by pressure, and the lesson cost them a friendship. Until 1937 the New York alcoholics met alongside the Oxford Groups, a religious movement Bill had joined when he got sober. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "But in the latter part of that year we most reluctantly parted company with these great friends." Why? The drunks would not stand for being run. "They always had to be led, not pushed." They would not accept the Oxford Groups' team guidance over their private lives: "It was too authoritarian for them." Authoritarian means demanding obedience. Bill did not hide what the parting cost: "Our debt to them, nevertheless, was and is immense, and so the final breakaway was very painful." So A.A.'s unity was never going to be the kind enforced from above. It had to be the kind the Long Form describes (Big Book, p. 563): "Each member of Alcoholics Anonymous is but a small part of a great whole." Nobody is the boss of the whole. Today the pushing comes back as program rules dressed up as A.A.: check in, report your progress, be told what to share. Test it tonight: ask whether anyone in your room is pushing a member to do something A.A. never asked of anyone.
*Sit with:* A.A.'s first members parted from friends they owed a great deal rather than push drunks around. Is anyone in my room being pushed?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (parting from the Oxford Groups, 1937).

### January 28 — "So We Had to Evolve" · *(the threat today)*
The rumour is that Tradition One is a rule for your character; the Big Book says the Traditions were made for groups. The Foreword to the Second Edition (Big Book, p. xix): "As we discovered the principles by which the individual alcoholic could live, so we had to evolve principles by which the A.A. groups and A.A. as a whole could survive and function effectively." Two sets of principles: the Steps for the alcoholic's life, the Traditions for the life of the groups. Evolve means grow out of experience; nobody handed them down. Now listen to how Tradition One often gets taught: put others first, be a team player, don't make waves, your needs come last. Every one of those is advice to a person about himself. Read the Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) as it is written: "Hence our common welfare comes first." Our welfare, the group's and A.A.'s. It sets the order of business for a group, not the order of a member's heart. A group that turns its Traditions into talks on members' attitudes has nothing left to govern itself with. Test it tonight: the next time someone explains Tradition One at your meeting, listen for whether they are talking about the group or about you.
*Sit with:* The Traditions are principles for groups and for A.A. as a whole, not twelve more lessons for the member. When my group talks about Tradition One, whose behaviour is it talking about?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. xix (Foreword to Second Edition).

### January 29 — "One Alcoholic Talks With Another" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can keep perfect order and still break Tradition One, if nobody in it ever talks to the new man. Picture a well-run meeting. It starts on time, the readings are read, the shares are good, the closing is said together. Then the regulars head for their cars in twos and threes, and the newcomer by the door, who came alone, goes home alone. Nobody was unkind. Nobody was anything. The Foreword to the Third Edition says where A.A. actually happens (Big Book, p. xxii): "In spite of the great increase in the size and the span of this Fellowship, at its core it remains simple and personal. Each day, somewhere in the world, recovery begins when one alcoholic talks with another alcoholic". The "great whole" of the Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) is made of those conversations, one drunk to another. A room of sober people who only talk to each other has become a closed circle, and a closed circle is a part of nothing. Today the gap often gets filled from outside: the newcomer leaves with a program's pamphlet and a counsellor's card instead of a member's phone number. Test it tonight: before you leave, find out whether the newest person in the room is going home with anyone's number.
*Sit with:* A.A. is held together by one alcoholic talking to another, not by a well-run hour. Who in my room went home tonight without being talked to?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. xxii (Foreword to Third Edition).

### January 30 — "Benders" · *(how it gets captured)*
Capture doesn't only happen one group at a time; a whole town's groups can be taken together, and Bill warned that movements go on binges too. Bill W. wrote in his foreword to the Traditions pamphlet (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 5): "For whole movements have, before now, gone on benders, too!" A bender is a drinking spree that runs for days. On the same page: "But A.A. unity cannot automatically preserve itself." A town's version goes like this. A big program offers to print one meeting list for the whole city, free. The list is handsome and everyone uses it. Next year the program asks listed meetings to post its intake number. Then to report attendance. Then to sign court cards its way. Group by group, each says yes, because everyone else did. Nobody voted to join anything, but the city's meetings now run on one outside body's terms. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "A.A. must continue to live or most of us will surely die." In that town A.A. is still alive, on someone else's terms. Test it tonight: ask who prints your town's meeting list, and what a group has to do to stay on it.
*Sit with:* A whole city's groups can be captured together, one reasonable yes at a time. What does my group have to do to stay on the list it is on?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 5 (Bill W.'s foreword).

### January 31 — "Out of Great Weakness" · *(the threat)*
The month ends where Bill ended his story of A.A.'s unity: it was forged under the threat of collapse, and it lasts only while the groups keep choosing it. Bill W., at St. Louis in 1955 (A.A. Comes of Age): "Ours is not the usual success story; rather it is the story of how, under God's grace, an unsuspected strength has arisen out of great weakness; of how, under threats of disunity and collapse, world-wide unity and brotherhood have been forged." Forged means hammered into shape in the heat. And he said what holds it now. The Traditions, he said, "represent the distilled experience of our past, and we rely on them to carry us in unity through the challenges and dangers which the future may bring." The first of them is four sentences in the Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Each member of Alcoholics Anonymous is but a small part of a great whole. A.A. must continue to live or most of us will surely die. Hence our common welfare comes first. But individual welfare follows close afterward." The whole first, the member close behind, and the drunk still out there the reason for both. Tomorrow, Tradition Two asks who decides for a group. Test it tonight: ask whether your group would still stand if every person in it were replaced, and why.
*Sit with:* A.A.'s unity was forged under threat, not handed down, and it lasts only as long as groups choose it. Would my group still stand if everyone in it were new?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 1 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy).

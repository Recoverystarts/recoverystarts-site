### June 1 — "Build and Operate Our Own" · *(the threat)*
Tradition Six begins by naming the three things that pull a group off its job: money, property and authority. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Problems of money, property, and authority may easily divert us from our primary spiritual aim." To divert something is to turn it off its course, and the course is the drunk who is still drinking. Bill W. admitted how close A.A. came to going the other way (A.A. Comes of Age): "At that time hospitals did not want us, so we thought we would build and operate our own." The list kept growing: teach the public, change the laws, take on every other problem too. "Yes, we did dream those dreams. How natural that was, since most alcoholics are bankrupt idealists." Every dream on that list needed buildings, budgets and someone in charge of them. Each one would have put property and authority at the center of a fellowship that worked because one drunk talked to another for nothing. They tried the hospitals, and the hospitals bogged down. Tradition Six is what they wrote down afterward. Today the dream comes back already built: a facility with beds, staff and a budget, offering to be A.A.'s hospital. Test it tonight: ask what your group owns, what it pays for, and whether any of it gets more attention than the newcomer.
*Sit with:* The Long Form names money, property and authority as the things that turn a group away from the drunk. What in my group is starting to take the attention the newcomer should get?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Six: the early dreams).

### June 2 — "The Whole of New York State" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was a Tradition Six, New York members learned the hard way to keep a club's business apart from the group. In 1940 they rented the first clubhouse, an old stable on West Twenty-fourth Street. Bill W. remembered (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 18): "A couple of older members signed the lease. We painted and we scrubbed. We had a home." The lease company was named "Alcoholics Anonymous of New York, Inc." Bill wrote in 1947 (P-17, p. 19): "Only later did we realize we had incorporated the whole of New York State, a mistake recently rectified." To incorporate is to form a company in law. A company holding one lease had taken A.A.'s name for a whole state. They learned something bigger (A.A. Comes of Age): "the club had to be on the side lines and could not be managed by the A.A. groups as such." The Long Form wrote the lesson down (Big Book, p. 564): property of real use to A.A. "should be separately incorporated and managed, thus dividing the material from the spiritual." The material is rent, leases and money. The spiritual is the message, carried free. Today a group often meets under someone else's lease, and the landlord's rules come with the room. Test it tonight: ask whose name is on the lease for your group's room, and what that name can decide.
*Sit with:* The first club's lease company took the name of A.A. in a whole state, and the members had to undo it. Whose name is on the lease for my group's room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); P-17, pp. 18–19 (Bill W., "Clubs in A.A.," 1947); A.A. Comes of Age (the first clubhouse, 1940).

### June 3 — "Actual or Implied" · *(the threat today)*
Read the cooperation sentence of Tradition Six slowly, because the rumour about it leaves out its second half. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 564): "While an A.A. group may cooperate with anyone, such cooperation ought never go so far as affiliation or endorsement, actual or implied." Take it word by word. May cooperate: a group is allowed to work alongside others, not ordered to. Affiliation means being attached as a part or a member, the way a branch belongs to a company. Endorsement means putting your name behind someone and telling the public they are good. Actual or implied means it counts whether anyone signed anything or not. If a stranger walking in would fairly think the group belongs to the place, or vouches for it, the line has been crossed. The rumour says the Traditions encourage partnership with treatment, and it quotes only the first eight words. The sentence ends with a fence. Today the implied kind is the common one: a meeting printed on a facility's weekly program, the facility's logo on the group's flyer, staff calling it their A.A. meeting. Nobody signed a contract. The newcomer still learns that A.A. is part of the place that bills him. Test it tonight: look at your group's meeting listing and flyer, and ask what a stranger would think your group belongs to.
*Sit with:* The Long Form allows cooperation and forbids affiliation and endorsement, even the implied kind. What would a stranger think my group belongs to?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 4 — "Which Prospects Deserve Them" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group that starts paying for members' treatment has gone into business, and the business soon starts choosing among drunks. A newcomer can't afford a detox bed, so the group passes a second basket and covers it. It works once. By spring there is a fund, a treasurer for it, and business meetings that run an hour over arguing whose bill to pay. One man paid his back and two didn't. Somebody asks whether a man on his third slip should get it again. Bill W. saw it across the groups (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 15): "practically all group schemes to finance or guarantee hospital bills for fellow members have failed also. Not only do many such loans go unpaid, there is always the controversial question in the group as to which prospects deserve them in the first place." A prospect is a drunk who might join. A room open to any drunk is now sorting drunks by who is worth the money. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 564): "An A.A. group, as such, should never go into business." Lending money is a business, and so is buying beds. A member's own wallet is his affair; the group's treasury is for the message. Test it tonight: ask what your group's money paid for this year, and whether any of it was a judgment on a person.
*Sit with:* A group fund for hospital bills ends with the group deciding which drunks deserve help. Has my group's money ever been used to judge a person?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); P-17, p. 15 (Bill W., "Hospitals and A.A.," 1947).

### June 5 — "Freely Discarded" · *(how it gets captured)*
The Long Form makes the groups the ones who can walk away from a facility, and capture is when that gets turned around. Clubs and hospitals, it says (Big Book, p. 564), "ought to be incorporated and so set apart that, if necessary, they can be freely discarded by the groups." The Long Form calls them secondary aids: second in line, a help to A.A. and never part of it. Freely discarded means the groups can drop them without losing anything they need. Bill W. explained why that keeps them honest. Nobody is forced to pay for them, so (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 29) "they can never dictate, nor can they stray from the main body of A.A. tradition for very long." To dictate is to give orders. Now watch it run backward. A facility offers a group its room for free, coffee included. Two years on, the group has no treasury, no other room, and a facility rule about who may attend. The group can no longer discard the facility. The facility can discard the group, and the group knows it, so it doesn't argue. Nobody captured it by force. Comfort did it, one free month at a time. Test it tonight: if the place your group meets told it to leave tomorrow, ask whether the group would still meet next week, and who would decide where.
*Sit with:* The Long Form gives the groups the power to drop any facility, and a free room can quietly take that power away. Could my group walk away from the place it meets?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); P-17, p. 29 (Bill W., "Money," 1946).

### June 6 — "Name and Financial Credit" · *(the threat)*
In 1947 the danger to Tradition Six walked in as an enthusiast with a good cause, asking for A.A.'s name. Bill W. described him (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 24): "He strongly feels that A.A. ought to place its name and financial credit squarely behind any first-rate research, hospital or educational project." Financial credit is the trust that lets you borrow or raise money. "Seeing that A.A. now makes the headlines, he argues that we should freely loan out our huge goodwill." Goodwill is the public's trust in a name. Bill named a motive without pretending it was everyone's: "The judgment of this enthusiast is sometimes beclouded by the fact he wants to make a career." Beclouded means fogged. The A.A. name belongs to every group at once, built by drunks who never charged a cent, and he wanted to lend it out. That is why the short form says a group must never lend the A.A. name to any outside enterprise, and the Long Form keeps even A.A.'s own clubs and hospitals from using it. Today the enthusiast is often a member who works in the field and asks his home group to put its name on his program's open house or its brochure. Test it tonight: ask whether anyone has asked your group to put its name to their work, and what the group answered.
*Sit with:* The enthusiast wanted A.A.'s name and credit behind good projects, and sometimes he wanted a career. Who has asked my group for its name?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); short form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 562); P-17, p. 24 (Bill W., "Dangers in Linking A.A. to Other Projects," 1947).

### June 7 — "Under the Direct Auspices" · *(before the tradition)*
Before Tradition Six was settled, some groups ran drying-out places themselves, and Bill W. reported how it went. In those years cheap hospital beds for drunks were hard to find. He wrote in 1947 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 15): "This urgency has tempted some A.A. groups to set up drying-out places of their own, hiring A.A. managers, nurses, and securing the services of a visiting physician. Where this has been done under the direct auspices of an A.A. group it has almost always backfired. It has put the group into business, a kind of business about which few A.A.s know anything at all." Under the direct auspices means run by the group itself, in its own name. Backfired means it blew up on them. Drunks who knew how to carry a message were suddenly hiring nurses, chasing bills and fighting over who was in charge, and the message got whatever time was left. The Long Form, first printed in 1946, had already drawn the line (Big Book, p. 564): "But hospitals, as well as other places of recuperation, ought to be well outside A.A.—and medically supervised." Places of recuperation are where people dry out and get their strength back. Today the pull runs the other way: facilities want groups inside them. Test it tonight: ask whether your group runs, staffs or answers for any place where drunks are treated.
*Sit with:* Groups that ran their own drying-out places found themselves in a business they knew nothing about. Does my group answer for anything that belongs to doctors?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); Big Book p. 561 (the Long Form first printed in 1946); P-17, p. 15 (Bill W., "Hospitals and A.A.," 1947).

### June 8 — "Medicine Is for Doctors" · *(the threat today)*
A.A. is not a treatment, and the Long Form says so by putting treatment outside it. The rumour today is that meetings are a kind of therapy, one more service a program counts in its hours. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 564) puts hospitals and places of recuperation "well outside A.A." and adds "medically supervised." Medically supervised means a doctor is responsible, not a drunk with a few years sober. Bill W. was plain about the division (A.A. Comes of Age): "We know that theology is for clergymen and that the practice of medicine and psychiatry is for doctors." Theology is the study of God, the clergy's work. Doctoring the very sick, Bill wrote, was "not and could not be A.A.'s business." A.A.'s business is one drunk carrying a message to another, free. The Big Book lists what the member's job takes (p. 97): "innumerable trips to police courts, sanitariums, hospitals, jails and asylums." The doctor treats the drunk; the member makes the trip to see him. Neither becomes the other. Today the line blurs from the treatment side: a client's meeting attendance logged as treatment, staff chairing the meeting as part of a shift. Test it tonight: ask whether anyone in your room has been told the meeting is part of their treatment, and who told them.
*Sit with:* Bill W. left medicine to doctors and kept A.A. to the message, free. Is anyone in my room being told the meeting is their treatment?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Six: medicine is for doctors; hospitals as friends); Big Book p. 97 (Working With Others).

### June 9 — "Spiritual Entities" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group goes into business one good idea at a time, and nobody ever votes for the moment it stops being a group. It starts with a literature table. Then medallions, mugs and shirts with the group's name, because they sell. The anniversary dance needs tickets, a hall deposit and a DJ, and it makes money, so next year there are two. Somebody has to keep the stock, chase the deposits and do the books. The business meeting that used to ask who would go out on the next call now spends forty minutes on inventory and ten on the drunk. Every item passed by a fair vote. Bill W. wrote in 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 29): "More and more, the A.A. groups are realizing that they are spiritual entities, not business organizations." An entity is a thing that exists in its own right. A group is one with a single job, and the job isn't retail. Literature carries the message, so the table isn't the problem. The problem is a group that needs its sales to pay its rent, because then the sales come first. The Long Form says flatly that a group should never go into business. Test it tonight: at your group's next business meeting, count the minutes spent on money and stock, and on the drinker still out there.
*Sit with:* A group drifts into business through fair votes on good ideas. How much of my group's business meeting is about the drunk?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); P-17, p. 29 (Bill W., "Money," 1946).

### June 10 — "Twelfth Step Houses" · *(how it gets captured)*
The A.A. name is the one thing every group owns together, and Tradition Six keeps it off buildings. The Long Form, speaking of clubs and hospitals (Big Book, p. 564): "Hence such facilities ought not to use the A.A. name." The early members had to apply it to their own places first. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "We found it imperative even inside A.A. to remove the A.A. name from clubs and Twelfth Step Houses." Imperative means it couldn't wait. Even A.A.'s own places had to give the name back. "These strenuous adventures implanted the deep-rooted conviction that in no circumstances could we endorse any related enterprise, no matter how good." Strenuous means exhausting. If members' own clubhouses couldn't carry the name, nothing outside can carry it on a group's say-so. Capture today goes through the name. A facility advertises A.A. meetings on site as a feature of what the client pays for, and a group that meets there goes along. The Traditions don't govern the facility. They govern the group, and the group decides whether its meeting is offered as part of someone's product. A group can ask to be left off the brochure, and it can move. Test it tonight: find every place your group's meeting is advertised, and ask whether anyone is using it to sell something.
*Sit with:* Even A.A.'s own clubs had to give up the A.A. name. Is my group's meeting being used to sell something?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Six: removing the A.A. name).

### June 11 — "It Is Going to Take Millions" · *(the threat)*
Tradition Six answered a threat from inside: A.A. wanting to become what the treatment industry is now. Bill W. remembered the argument over money (A.A. Comes of Age). One side said: "One alcoholic carries the message to another. Let's keep it that simple and stay out of money trouble." The other side said: "We need hospitals, paid therapists, traveling lecturers, rehabilitation centers, and heaven knows what else. It is going to take millions." And where would the millions come from? "Well, we will get it from the public, just as most other good works do." Read that list again: hospitals, paid therapists, rehabilitation centers. If that side had won, A.A. would own buildings, carry a payroll and answer to donors, and the free drunk at the door would have become a cost. The short form keeps the reason in its last words (Big Book, p. 562): "lest problems of money, property and prestige divert us from our primary purpose." Prestige means standing in other people's eyes. The Traditions kept the groups on the simple side and put the hospitals well outside A.A. Today the second list exists, fully built, outside A.A., and it often wants A.A.'s rooms inside it. Test it tonight: ask whether anything your group does would need more money than the basket brings in, and who would be paying for the rest.
*Sit with:* Some members once wanted hospitals, paid therapists and rehabilitation centers, paid for by the public. Does anything my group does need money the basket can't cover?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, pp. 563–564); short form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 562); A.A. Comes of Age (the money debate: conservatives and radicals).

### June 12 — "Co-operate but Never Compete" · *(before the tradition)*
A.A.'s first case was found in a hospital A.A. never owned, and that is the pattern Tradition Six wrote down. The Big Book's foreword tells it (p. xvii): "Hence the two men set to work almost frantically upon alcoholics arriving in the ward of the Akron City Hospital." The two men were Bill W. and Dr. Bob. They didn't run the ward; they walked in and talked to drunks. Bill W. named friends in medicine (A.A. Comes of Age): "Here great friends like Dr. Silkworth and Sister Ignatia came to our aid." Doctors and nurses treated the body, members carried the message, and nobody's name went on anybody else's door. Bill put the rule in one line: "Certain that we can do together what we cannot do separately, we shall always co-operate but never compete." To compete would have meant A.A. opening hospitals of its own. To affiliate would have meant A.A. becoming part of theirs. The Long Form allows the middle and nothing more (Big Book, p. 564): a group "may cooperate with anyone." Cooperation is visiting, carrying the message where the drunks are, and staying a guest. Today it often runs backward: the facility runs the room and calls it A.A. Test it tonight: ask whether your group goes into places as a guest carrying the message, or sits in them as part of the program.
*Sit with:* The founders carried the message into hospitals they never owned or joined. Is my group a guest where it meets, or part of the program?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); Big Book p. xvii (Foreword to the Second Edition); A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Six: co-operate but never compete; hospitals as friends).

### June 13 — "Bind Itself to No One" · *(the threat today)*
The last sentence of Tradition Six's Long Form is the shortest, and it usually gets broken with a signature. The Big Book (p. 564): "An A.A. group can bind itself to no one." To bind is to tie, or to put yourself under an obligation someone can hold you to. No one means no one: not the facility, the court, the funder, or the kind people who mean well. Read it as a rule about promises. A group may carry the message into a hospital or a jail as a guest. It may not promise anyone a meeting as part of their program, a weekly slot their clients are counted at, an attendance record it will keep for them, or a chair it will fill on their schedule. The moment a group owes a meeting to someone, that someone can call in the debt. Today the binding comes as paperwork: an agreement to provide an A.A. meeting for a program's clients, a form signed to keep a room, a promise to report who came. None of them looks like giving up the group's freedom. Each one is. A group bound to no one can say no tomorrow without asking leave. Test it tonight: ask whether your group has signed, promised or agreed to anything with anyone outside it, and what they could demand because of it.
*Sit with:* The Long Form ends with a group bound to no one, and paperwork is how most groups get bound. What has my group signed or promised to anyone outside it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 14 — "Thank You to the Group" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can break Tradition Six out of gratitude, in one vote, with nobody meaning harm. The group has eleven hundred dollars more than it needs for rent and literature. The detox across town, where half the members first dried out, is short of money. Someone moves to give it the surplus, and the motion passes to applause. Two months later the detox's fundraising letter goes out, with the group listed among its supporters under its A.A. name. Nobody asked for that; it was the detox saying thank you. The short form (Big Book, p. 562): "An A.A. group ought never endorse, finance or lend the A.A. name to any related facility or outside enterprise". In one vote the group did all three. It financed the detox, and the thank-you lent the group's name and turned it into an endorsement. Every drinker who reads that letter learns that A.A. backs that detox, and may decide A.A. is the detox. A group that pays for a facility has tied itself to it, and the drunk can no longer tell the two apart. Members can give what they like out of their own pockets. The basket was given to carry the message. Test it tonight: ask where your group's extra money goes, and whether anyone outside A.A. has ever thanked your group by name.
*Sit with:* A grateful vote can finance a facility and lend it the A.A. name at the same time. Where does my group's extra money go?
*Grounded in:* Short form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 562); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 15 — "Whoever Pays" · *(how it gets captured)*
The Long Form says whoever pays for a facility runs the facility, and capture is when they start running the group too. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 564): "Their management should be the sole responsibility of those people who financially support them." Sole means only. Those who pay for a club or hospital manage it, and nothing else. Bill W. watched the line crossed both ways at the early clubs (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 20): "if an A.A. rotating committee tries to boss the club corporation or if the corporation tries to run the A.A. affairs of those groups who may meet at the club, there is difficulty at once." A rotating committee is the group's elected servants, changed often so nobody stays in charge. His cure: "If an A.A. group wishes to use a given club, let them pay rent or split the meeting take with the club management." The meeting take is the basket. Rent keeps the two apart. The group is a tenant, not a department. Today the facility often pays for everything, asks no rent, and in return picks the chair, sets the format and decides who may come in. That is the payer managing an A.A. group, which the Long Form never allowed. Test it tonight: ask who pays for the room your group meets in, and what that payer gets to decide.
*Sit with:* The Long Form gives the people who pay for a facility the facility, and nothing more. Who pays for my group's room, and what do they decide?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); P-17, p. 20 (Bill W., "Clubs in A.A.," 1947).

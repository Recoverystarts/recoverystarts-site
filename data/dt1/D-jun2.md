### June 16 — "A Field Day for Promoters" · *(the threat)*
Put the A.A. name on a fund drive for a good cause, and you have handed it to every promoter in town. In the 1940s some groups, desperate for hospital beds, started public campaigns to raise money for "A.A. hospitals" of their own. Bill W. wrote what followed (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 16): "Instantly all sorts of doubts are generated; the projects bog down. Conservative A.A.s realize that business ventures or solicitations carrying the A.A. endorsement are truly dangerous to us all. Were this practice to become general the lid would be off. Promoters, A.A. and otherwise, would have a field day." A promoter is someone who sells a scheme to the public. The beds were badly needed. The danger was the name on the appeal. So the Long Form moved hospitals out of the groups' hands (Big Book, p. 564): "Secondary aids to A.A., such as clubs or hospitals which require much property or administration, ought to be incorporated". Incorporated means set up as a separate body with its own owners. The group carries the message; the bed belongs to someone else. Today the field day is open: galas, campaigns and appeals that use A.A.'s name, or "the recovery community," to raise money for buildings no A.A. group will ever own. Test it tonight: has your group's name, or A.A.'s, ever appeared on somebody's appeal for money?
*Sit with:* Bill saw that a good cause carrying A.A.'s name would bring the promoters in. Has anyone ever raised money in my group's name, and did the group agree to it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); P-17, pp. 15–16 (Bill W. on hospitals, 1947).

### June 17 — "His Work and Ours" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was a Tradition Six, the Big Book already showed a hospital and A.A. working side by side without belonging to each other. Writing in 1939 about one eastern city, the book describes (Big Book, p. 162) "a well-known hospital for the treatment of alcoholic and drug addiction." Then: "We are greatly indebted to the doctor in attendance there, for he, although it might prejudice his own work, has told us of his belief in ours." His own work, and ours: two jobs with two owners. The doctor ran his ward: "Every few days this doctor suggests our approach to one of his patients." And A.A.'s part? "Many of us, former patients, go there to help." Members walked in as visitors and went home again. The hospital never became A.A.'s, and A.A. never became the hospital's. Seven years later the Long Form wrote that habit down: hospitals stay well outside A.A. and under doctors. Today the two jobs get folded into one. A facility runs the bed, the counselling and an "A.A. meeting" on its own timetable, so the visitor has become staff and the meeting has become part of the stay. Test it tonight: when your group carries the message into a hospital or detox, is it a guest there, or part of the program?
*Sit with:* In 1939 the doctor had his work and A.A. had its own, and neither owned the other. When my group goes into a facility, whose work is it doing?
*Grounded in:* Big Book p. 162 (A Vision for You); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 18 — "A Central A.A. Institution" · *(the threat today)*
A secondary aid becomes a threat the day people start believing they can't recover without it. In the 1940s it was the A.A. club, a rented hall with coffee and company, and Bill W. watched members lean on it (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 17): "We might conceive them as a central A.A. institution". A central institution is the thing everything else hangs on. "At times club enthusiasts will act as though they really believed we could handle our alcoholic problems by club life alone. They are apt to depend upon clubs rather than upon the A.A. program." The Long Form gave clubs and hospitals their place in its own words: *secondary aids*. They come second, behind the one thing a group exists for, which is carrying the message to the drunk still drinking. Today the club has been replaced by the program: thirty days inside, then aftercare, then sober living, then alumni night, until a newcomer is told that A.A. is something you do after treatment, when treatment says so. The meeting has become one stage in somebody else's plan. A group that accepts that order has made itself secondary to a facility, which is the Tradition turned upside down. Test it tonight: ask a newcomer how they found your meeting, and whether anyone told them they had to go somewhere else first.
*Sit with:* Clubs and hospitals are secondary aids, and the danger begins when people depend on them instead of on A.A. In my area, what has been put in front of the meeting as the thing you must do first?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); P-17, p. 17 (Bill W. on clubs, 1947).

### June 19 — "Bigger Than the Walls" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group that buys its own building has gone into the property business, and the property starts running the meetings. Picture it: a hall comes up for sale, members lend the money for a down payment, and the vote passes with cheering. Three years on, the business meeting is about the roof, the insurance, the furnace and the tenant upstairs who is behind on rent. The newcomers get the last five minutes. The two members who signed for the mortgage carry more weight than everyone else put together, and nobody can suggest moving without insulting them. Bill W. saw it coming (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 21): "Acceptance of large sums from any source to buy, build, or finance clubs almost invariably leads to later headaches." And the walls don't stay big enough for long: "the growing A.A. will burst the walls of any clubhouse." The Long Form keeps the deed away from the group (Big Book, p. 563): "any considerable property of genuine use to A.A." is not the group's to hold. It "should be separately incorporated and managed" (p. 564), carried by the people who choose to carry it, so the group stays free to meet anywhere. Test it tonight: if your group had to move next month, who would have to agree to it, and what would it cost them?
*Sit with:* Once a group owns the building, the building gets a vote. If my group had to leave its room tomorrow, could it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, pp. 563–564); P-17, p. 21 (Bill W. on clubs, 1947).

### June 20 — "Left Definitely Outside" · *(how it gets captured)*
A meeting place is a secondary aid, and the capture comes when the place starts owning the group. Bill W. said where the early clubs ended up once A.A. had learned its lesson (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 22): "The club is left definitely outside, where, in the opinion of many, it should be." Outside means the club belongs to the members who pay for it and enjoy it, and the groups use it or don't. Today the clubhouse is often an app. Picture a group that moves its meeting onto a recovery platform that costs nothing to use. The platform owns the link, the list of who attended and the rules for who may speak. It adds its own banner, its own counsellors' ads and its own check-in form for the newcomer to fill out. A year later the group wants to leave, and finds that its members' contacts and its meeting's address belong to someone else. The Long Form's order was the other way round: secondary aids are set apart so the groups can drop them, never so they can drop the groups. A room the group cannot leave is not set apart; it is in charge. Test it tonight: if your meeting is online, who owns the link, and could your group take it, and its members, somewhere else tomorrow?
*Sit with:* The early clubs ended up outside, where the groups could take them or leave them alone. Could my group leave the place it meets without losing itself?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564); P-17, p. 22 (Bill W. on clubs, 1947).

### June 21 — "The Name on the Drive" · *(the threat)*
The A.A. name gets lent to outside causes even when no group votes to lend it, and Bill W. recorded how. In 1955 he wrote about a member who ran an alcohol-education project and raised money for it in public drives, while telling everyone she was in A.A. The result (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 54): "many contributors were confused. They thought A.A. was in the educational field or else they thought A.A. itself was raising money when indeed it was not and didn't want to." Then the cost (p. 55): "So A.A.'s name was used to solicit funds at the very moment we were trying to tell people that A.A. wanted no outside money." To solicit funds is to ask for money. One drive became a pattern, he wrote: "public solicitations by A.A.s for money — money for drying-out farms, Twelfth Step enterprises, A.A. boardinghouses, clubs, and the like". Each is a place the Long Form keeps outside A.A., raised for in A.A.'s name. Today the drive is a crowdfunding page for a sober house, with A.A. in its story and a member's face on it. The cause may be good. The name was never theirs to spend. Test it tonight: look up the fundraisers in your town that mention A.A., and notice how many are spending a name no A.A. group can lend.
*Sit with:* A member's public drives taught the public that A.A. was raising money when it was not. Is A.A.'s name being spent anywhere near my group without the groups' say?
*Grounded in:* P-17, pp. 54–55 (Bill W., "Why Alcoholics Anonymous Is Anonymous," 1955); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 22 — "Not Strictly A.A. Enterprises" · *(before the tradition)*
The first rest farm A.A. members used was never A.A.'s, and that was the right shape before anyone wrote it down. Around 1940 a party of members visited a farm in the Connecticut hills, run as a charity by a woman they knew as Sister Francis. Bill W. recalled her offer (A.A. Comes of Age): "She offered us full use of the place if some of us would create a board of trustees to look after it." Some of us, not the groups. A few members took the property on as a separate job, and the groups went on carrying the message. The farm became a place to rest after hospital, and Bill never called it A.A.'s. Such places, he wrote, are "not strictly A.A. enterprises". Useful, loved, and outside. In 1946 the Long Form made that the rule (Big Book, p. 564): "hospitals, as well as other places of recuperation, ought to be well outside A.A." A place of recuperation is somewhere to rest and get your strength back. Today such places multiply: retreats, sober houses and recovery centres, some of which call themselves A.A. places and hold house meetings their residents must attend. Test it tonight: ask whether any house or retreat near you calls itself A.A., and whether any A.A. group has a say in how it is run.
*Sit with:* The first rest farm was looked after by a few members on a separate board, and the groups kept their one job. Which places near me call themselves A.A. without belonging to any group?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (the first rest farm, 1939–40); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 23 — "Regardless of Anyone" · *(the threat today)*
The rumour today is that a drunk needs a facility before A.A. can do anything for him, and the Big Book says the opposite. In "Working With Others," its chapter on carrying the message to the man still drinking, the book is plain about whom he depends on (Big Book, p. 98): "Burn the idea into the consciousness of every man that he can get well regardless of anyone. The only condition is that he trust in God and clean house." One condition, and no admission form in it. A doctor may be needed to get him through a dangerous withdrawal, and the Long Form keeps that well outside A.A. and under doctors. What no facility can be is the gate to A.A. A group tied to a facility ends up carrying a different message: get assessed first, get a bed first, then come to us. The Long Form closes that door from the group's side: an A.A. group can bind itself to no one, so it never has to send the drunk anywhere before it helps him. Today the line gets said inside the rooms themselves, as advice: you need treatment first, come back when you've done your thirty days. Test it tonight: listen for what your room tells the shaking newcomer he has to do before A.A. can help him.
*Sit with:* The Big Book says a man can get well regardless of anyone, on one condition that no facility sets. What does my group tell a newcomer he needs first?
*Grounded in:* Big Book p. 98 (Working With Others); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 24 — "The Group's House" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group that houses newcomers has gone into business, and the business soon decides who gets helped. Picture a group moved by the men sleeping in cars outside its meeting. It votes to rent a house out of the treasury: four beds, a no-drinking rule, a meeting in the kitchen on Sundays. For a few months it feels like carrying the message with a roof on it. Then a resident drinks, and the business meeting has to vote on whether he is put out. Another can't pay his share, and the treasurer is chasing a sick man for money. The neighbours complain, the landlord phones the group's secretary, and the insurer wants to know who the owner is. Half the members stop coming to business meetings because they are sick of the house. The house was not a wicked idea. It was the wrong job for an A.A. group. The Long Form says so in ten words (Big Book, p. 564): "An A.A. group, as such, should never go into business." As such means acting as a group. A group that runs a house becomes a landlord to the very drunks it exists to reach, including the one it just put out on the street. Test it tonight: is your group's treasury paying for anything that isn't the meeting itself or A.A.'s own services?
*Sit with:* A group that runs a house becomes a landlord to the drunks it exists to reach. What is my group's money paying for besides the meeting and A.A.'s own services?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 25 — "Known as A.A." · *(how it gets captured)*
A facility that calls itself A.A.-based is borrowing a name that A.A. refused to lend, and refused in writing early. In 1946 Bill W. described how A.A. stood toward hospitals (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 33): "It is not a function of A.A. in the sense of management or ownership." A.A. visited and helped; it did not own a ward or run one. And the name stayed home: "But none of these institutions are known as" A.A. hospitals, he wrote. The Long Form fixed it as the rule for every club and hospital (Big Book, p. 564): "Hence such facilities ought not to use the A.A. name." Today the borrowing is quieter than a sign on the door. A program describes itself as 12-step based, puts the Big Book on its reading list, holds an "A.A. meeting" in its group room every Tuesday, and lists that meeting in its brochure as part of the treatment it bills for. None of that makes it A.A., and none of it makes A.A. answerable for it. But the family paying the bill believes it has bought A.A., and the newcomer believes A.A. is something that costs money. Test it tonight: if a meeting near you is held inside a facility, ask who decided it would happen, and whether the people in the chairs could vote it out of existence.
*Sit with:* In 1946 no hospital A.A. worked with was known as an A.A. hospital. Is there a place near me that sells what my group gives away?
*Grounded in:* P-17, p. 33 (Bill W., "Money," 1946); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 26 — "Superintend It" · *(the threat)*
In 1937 even the founders planned an A.A. hospital. While A.A. was hoping for funds from John D. Rockefeller Jr., the man looking into the Fellowship as the basis for that request was steered first to Akron, and Bill W. gave the reason (A.A. Comes of Age): "If an A.A. hospital were to be put into operation, the Akron location would be better, and Dr. Bob would be available to superintend it." To superintend is to be in charge. The report came back glowing and asked for a large grant, to support two or three members and so that "a down payment on the hospital could be made". To the founders it looked perfect: "Here was medicine, here was religion, and here was a great good work, all in one package." Then: "Mr. Rockefeller flatly turned down this plea for a large sum". The hospital money never came. Nine years later the Long Form named what they had been walking toward (Big Book, p. 563): "Problems of money, property, and authority may easily divert us from our primary spiritual aim." Today the package is still on offer: a program that will fund the meeting, house the newcomers and employ the old-timers, all under one roof. Test it tonight: ask what your group would have to stop being in order to accept a package like that.
*Sit with:* The founders wanted an A.A. hospital, and the money to build it never came. What package would my group find hard to say no to?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (the 1937 report and Mr. Rockefeller's answer); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 563).

### June 27 — "Even Sober Drunks" · *(before the tradition)*
Authority in A.A. grew wherever property did, and the first clubs found that out before any Tradition was written. Bill W. remembered those years with affection and honesty (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 18): "Growth brought headaches; growing pains, we call them now. How serious they seemed then!" A building needs someone to hold the keys, collect the rent and make the rules. Some ran the place like dictators. Drunks fell on the floor or broke up meetings. Steering committees, small groups set up to run things, tried to name their own friends to follow them, and found "to their dismay that even sober drunks couldn't be" steered. Nobody in A.A. has the power to govern anyone, and the property kept tempting people to try. What they worked out was to take the building's business away from the groups, so that whoever wanted the club could run it and the groups could stay what they were. The Long Form set that down in 1946 for every club and hospital: managed apart, by those who pay for it. Today the same pull works wherever one person holds the keys, the bank account or the meeting list, and the group starts asking his permission. Test it tonight: ask who holds the keys and the bank card for your meeting, and how the group would take them back.
*Sit with:* Wherever the early members held property, somebody started trying to steer the rest. Who holds the keys to my group's room, and does the group still decide?
*Grounded in:* P-17, p. 18 (Bill W. on clubs, 1947); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 28 — "Decline to Marry" · *(the threat today)*
A letter of support is how a program asks a group to vouch for it, and A.A. has already answered. Bill W. put it in one line in 1955 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 50): "We will cooperate with practically everybody, yet we decline to marry our Society to anyone." To cooperate is to work alongside: carry the message into a detox when asked, leave literature, answer the phone. To marry is to take on someone's name, terms and future as your own. The Long Form draws the same line from the group's side. Cooperation may go a long way, but never as far as affiliation or endorsement. Affiliation means belonging to someone; endorsement means vouching for them. Today the request comes as paperwork. A program applying for public money asks the local groups for a letter saying they support its work. Another wants a line for its website saying the groups recommend it. Each request is polite, each cause sounds good, and each one puts the group's name behind somebody else's business, where the drunk still drinking will read it and assume A.A. sent it. A group that signs has spoken for a program it does not run and cannot stop. Test it tonight: if a program asked your group for a letter of support tomorrow, who would answer, and what would they say?
*Sit with:* A.A. cooperates with nearly everyone and marries no one. Has my group ever put its name behind someone else's work?
*Grounded in:* P-17, p. 50 (Bill W., "Why Alcoholics Anonymous Is Anonymous," 1955); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 29 — "A Flyer on the Table" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can endorse something without ever voting to, just by letting it sit on the literature table. Picture a member, years sober and well liked, who starts a business as a paid recovery coach. He asks to leave his cards next to the pamphlets. The secretary, a friend, mentions it during the announcements. Somebody adds his page to the group's website under local resources. Nobody voted on any of it. A newcomer picks up the card from a table full of A.A. literature, hears it read out at an A.A. meeting, finds it on the group's own page, and concludes that A.A. recommends paying this man to keep him sober. That is the opposite of what A.A. is: one drunk carrying the message to another, free, wanting nothing back. The Long Form saw this slide coming. A group may cooperate with anyone, but never as far as affiliation or endorsement, and it ends that sentence with three words (Big Book, p. 564): "actual or implied." Implied means said without being said out loud. A card on an A.A. table says it. Test it tonight: look at what's on your group's literature table and in its announcements, and ask whether a newcomer could mistake any of it for something A.A. recommends.
*Sit with:* A business card on an A.A. literature table is an endorsement nobody voted for. What on my group's table or website speaks for us without our say?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

### June 30 — "Best Stay Whole" · *(how it gets captured)*
Every capture this month has worked the same way: something outside the group gets a hold on it, through money, property or authority, until the group can no longer walk away. The Big Book introduces the Traditions as answers to two questions, and the second is this (Big Book, p. 561): "How can A.A. best stay whole and so survive?" Tradition Six answers it for a group's possessions and its friends. Hospitals and clubs are kept outside. The A.A. name stays off them. Cooperation stops short of a marriage. Under it all sits the last sentence: a group can "bind itself to no one." To bind is to tie, and the ties come dressed as help: a free room, a steady flow of clients, a grant, a partnership, a debt of gratitude. A group bound to no one can leave any room, refuse any money and turn down any partner, and still be an A.A. group the next morning. That keeps the door open to the drunk still out there, free. The same page says why: "We alcoholics see that we must work together and hang together, else most of us will finally die alone." Test it tonight: ask three questions. Who could close your meeting? Who pays for it? Who decides what happens in it? In an A.A. group the answers are the members, the members, and the members.
*Sit with:* The Traditions exist so A.A. can stay whole, and Tradition Six keeps a group free of every tie. Who besides its members could close my group?
*Grounded in:* Big Book p. 561 (The A.A. Tradition); long form, Tradition 6 (Big Book, p. 564).

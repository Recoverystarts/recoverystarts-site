### March 1 — "One Alcoholic Judging Another" · *(the threat)*
Tradition 3 grew out of a 1946 essay by Bill W. about A.A. groups deciding which drunks deserved a seat. He looked back at the membership rules the groups had made and at who they would have caught (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 11): "Most of the early members of A.A. would have been thrown out because they slipped too much, because their morals were too bad, because they had mental as well as alcoholic difficulties. Or, believe it or not, because they did not come from the so-called better classes of society." Then his verdict (p. 12): "Imagine, if you can, one alcoholic judging another!" That is the danger this month's Tradition answers. Not the drunk who walks in, but the sober ones deciding who counts. The Long Form shuts the question down (Big Book, p. 563): "Our membership ought to include all who suffer from alcoholism. Hence we may refuse none who wish to recover." Refuse none: not the one who slipped last week, not the one with a record, not the one with no money and nowhere to sleep. Today the judging comes dressed as policy: a room that only seats people who are enrolled, insured or referred. Test it tonight: count every rule, written or not, a newcomer has to get past to sit down in your room.
*Sit with:* The early rules would have thrown out the very people who built A.A., so the Long Form says refuse none. Who would my group's unwritten rules keep out?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, pp. 11–12 (Bill W., "Who Is a Member of Alcoholics Anonymous?", 1946).

### March 2 — "Limited to Alcoholics" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was a written Tradition 3, the first alcoholics in New York walked away from the movement that had helped them get sober, because staying tied to it would cost the drunk. Bill W. had joined the Oxford Groups when he left the hospital. Then (A.A. Comes of Age): "Until the middle of 1937 we in New York had been working alongside the Oxford Groups. But in the latter part of that year we most reluctantly parted company with these great friends." Why? "Naturally enough, they did not think too highly of our objective, limited as it was to alcoholics." Bill never called them anything but friends: "Our debt to them, nevertheless, was and is immense, and so the final breakaway was very painful." Gratitude did not buy an affiliation. Nine years later the Long Form wrote down what New York had already done (Big Book, p. 563): two or three alcoholics may call themselves an A.A. group, "provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." Today the friend is a program that really did help, and now wants its name beside the meeting's. Test it tonight: if your group owes someone a debt, is it paying with thanks or with its name?
*Sit with:* The first A.A.'s owed the Oxford Groups an immense debt and still would not carry their name. Whose name rides along with my group's?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Recovery: the First Legacy — the 1937 parting); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 3 — "Only in the Long Form" · *(the threat today)*
The clause that stops a treatment centre from owning an A.A. group is not in the short form most rooms read aloud. The short form (Big Book, p. 562): "The only requirement for A.A. membership is a desire to stop drinking." True, and it says nothing about who a group may belong to. The Long Form, one page on, finishes the thought (p. 563): "Any two or three alcoholics gathered together for sobriety may call themselves an A.A. group, provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." Affiliation means being attached to some other body: belonging to it, answering to it, carrying its name. The Big Book says why it prints the longer version at all (p. 561): the Long Form "is more explicit and of possible historic value". A room set up by a program, staffed by a program and listed on the program's schedule can read the short form every night and never hear the sentence that says it is not an A.A. group. That is how the rumour works: quote the half that sounds open, leave off the half that sets a limit. Test it tonight: ask whether your group's name is attached to anyone else's, on a door, a schedule or a website.
*Sit with:* The short form tells you who may join; only the Long Form tells you a group may belong to no one else. Which version does my room read aloud?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); short form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 562); Big Book, p. 561.

### March 4 — "For the Good of the Movement" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can break Tradition 3 with one vote, cast by sober people who mean well. Picture the business meeting. A man has come back drunk three times this year, and once he shouted. Someone moves that he not be welcome until he has thirty days. It sounds like protecting the room. It is a membership rule, and the Long Form has already answered it (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence we may refuse none who wish to recover." Bill W. wrote down how such votes look years later (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 13): "Every older A.A. shudders when he remembers the names of persons he once condemned; people he confidently predicted would never sober up; persons he was sure ought to be thrown out of A.A. for the good of the movement." Some of those very persons, he went on, have since been sober for years and are now among the old-timer's best friends. So the old-timer asks himself: "What if A.A. had slammed its door in their faces? Where would they be now?" Thirty days is a fine thing to want for that man. It is not a thing a group can demand of him before he may sit down. Test it tonight: has your group ever voted on who may come, and could the person it was aimed at still walk in tomorrow?
*Sit with:* Old-timers shudder at the names of people they once wanted thrown out, because some of them got sober. Has my group ever voted to keep someone out for the good of the room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 13 (Bill W., "Who Is a Member of Alcoholics Anonymous?", 1946).

### March 5 — "A Brand of Treatment" · *(how it gets captured)*
A group tied to one kind of treatment has stopped being an A.A. group, and Bill W. said so in plain words. Explaining the Long Form's "no other affiliation", he wrote (A.A. Comes of Age) that an A.A. group should not ally "itself with any particular brand of medical or psychiatric treatment." A brand of treatment is a method somebody sells or runs: a clinic's program, a hospital's model, a facility's phases. And the name, he added, "must be reserved for us only." Us means the alcoholics in the chairs. Here is how the capture runs. A facility starts a meeting for its clients, in its building, on its schedule. Staff open the room, choose the readings and take attendance for the chart. Counsellors tell clients the meeting is part of their treatment plan. No sign in the room says affiliated, yet everything in it is. The Long Form draws the line where the facility cannot argue (Big Book, p. 563): two or three alcoholics may call themselves an A.A. group, "provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." Who opens the door, who picks the readings, who takes the attendance: those answers show who the group belongs to. Test it tonight: if the program that runs your meeting's building changed its method tomorrow, would your meeting have to change too?
*Sit with:* Bill W. ruled out an A.A. group allied to any brand of treatment, and kept the name for the alcoholics alone. Does my meeting run on anyone's treatment plan?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — "no other affiliation"); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 6 — "Won't There Be Criminals?" · *(the threat)*
The first threat to Tradition 3 came from inside A.A.: sober alcoholics afraid of who else might come through the door. When the publicity came and strangers began arriving, Bill W. remembered what the members asked each other (A.A. Comes of Age): "Won't there be mighty queer people? Won't there be criminals? Won't there be social undesirables?" And his own judgment of it: "Mixed with a certain amount of snobbishness and smugness, this was downright fear." Years later Bill told of sitting in his office with a member who held the title of countess, then going to a meeting that same night where a mild little man was taking everyone's coats, for nothing. Somebody told him who the man was: "He used to be one of Al Capone's mob." Same fellowship, same door. The Long Form made it the rule for every group (Big Book, p. 563): "Our membership ought to include all who suffer from alcoholism." All. Not all who are respectable, or safe, or easy to have around. Today the same fear works through a front desk that asks for referral papers, and rooms that quietly steer the man from the shelter somewhere else. Test it tonight: who is the person your room would least like to see taking the coats, and would he be welcome?
*Sit with:* A.A.'s first fear was criminals and undesirables, and the answer was a countess and a man from Capone's mob in the same fellowship. Who does my room quietly hope stays away?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — Tradition Three); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 7 — "No Alcoholic Excluded" · *(before the tradition)*
Before Tradition 3 was written down, the groups had already fought over membership, and the Big Book records what they settled. Its Foreword to the Second Edition tells how A.A. grew to thousands of members by the end of 1941, and the test that came with them (Big Book, p. xviii): "Would there be quarrels over membership, leadership, and money?" There were (p. xix): "Soon A.A. was beset by these very problems on every side and in every group." Out of those quarrels came principles for the groups, and the first one the Foreword names is about the door: "It was thought that no alcoholic man or woman could be excluded from our Society". Excluded means shut out. Not voted out, not screened out, not priced out. The groups did not learn this from a book of rules. They learned it from trying the other way in their own rooms, and the Long Form later wrote it into the Tradition that binds every group. It came out of quarrels, not out of theory, which is why it holds. Today exclusion seldom announces itself: a meeting held where only enrolled clients may enter, a door that needs a key card after hours. Test it tonight: could any alcoholic in your town walk into your meeting without asking anyone's permission?
*Sit with:* The groups fought over membership everywhere, and what came out was that no alcoholic could be excluded. Is there anyone who needs permission to enter my meeting?
*Grounded in:* Big Book, pp. xviii–xix (Foreword to Second Edition); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 8 — "He Signs Nothing" · *(the threat today)*
An A.A. meeting is not a checkpoint, and the paper passed around many rooms today turns it into one. Bill W. described the door in 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 13): "Our A.A. door stands wide open, and if he passes through it and commences to do anything at all about his problem, he is considered a member of Alcoholics Anonymous. He signs nothing, agrees to nothing, promises nothing. We demand nothing. He joins us on his own say-so." Say-so means his own word, nobody else's. That is the Long Form's promise that membership never depends on money or conformity, put into the plainest terms there are. Now look at the paper. A sign-in sheet at the door that staff collect for a facility's records. A court slip, signed at the end by whoever is chairing, so that a seat in the room becomes proof for a probation file. The drunk sent by a judge is as welcome as anyone; that door stands open to him too. What changes is the room. Once a signature is the reason people sit down, the meeting is keeping records for someone else, and the person who walks in with no paper at all starts to look like he is in the wrong place. Test it tonight: does anything get signed, collected or reported in your meeting, and who is it for?
*Sit with:* A member signs nothing, agrees to nothing and promises nothing; he joins on his own say-so. What paper passes through my meeting, and whose file does it end up in?
*Grounded in:* P-17, p. 13 (Bill W., "Who Is a Member of Alcoholics Anonymous?", 1946); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 9 — "No Monopoly" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group breaks Tradition 3 the day it starts telling newcomers to do it the group's way or find another room. The Long Form has one word for what a group may never demand (Big Book, p. 563): conformity, which means fitting a pattern someone else set. Picture how it creeps in. A group grows proud of how it works. Its old-timers decide a newcomer should share a certain way, use certain words, read certain pages and agree with certain opinions, or stop coming. Nobody votes. The pressure builds until the man who does it differently quietly drops away. The Big Book itself refused to work that way. In "Working With Others", the chapter on carrying the message to the alcoholic who is still drinking, it says of the man who wants a different road (Big Book, p. 95): "If he thinks he can do the job in some other way, or prefers some other spiritual approach, encourage him to follow his own conscience. We have no monopoly on God; we merely have an approach that worked with us." A monopoly is sole ownership. If the book that carries the message will not demand conformity from a drunk, no group has the right to. Test it tonight: is there anything your group expects of newcomers that the Long Form never asked of anyone?
*Sit with:* The Big Book tells us to let a man follow his own conscience, and the Long Form bars conformity as a condition. What does my group expect newcomers to fall in line with?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book, p. 95 ("Working With Others").

### March 10 — "Money or Conformity" · *(how it gets captured)*
The two things the Long Form says membership may never hang on are the two things a program runs on. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Nor ought A.A. membership ever depend upon money or conformity." Money means a fee, a bill, an insurance code. Conformity means following someone else's rules to keep your place. Bill W. listed what A.A. has instead (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 37): "no fees, no dues, no rules and regulations, no demand that alcoholics conform to A.A. principles, no one set in personal authority over anyone else." Even the basket can be passed by. Money given in A.A., he wrote, is "in no wise a condition of our A.A. membership" (p. 28). Now watch the capture. A program bills for a stay, and the stay includes A.A. meetings. Attendance goes on the chart as compliance. Miss enough of them and you lose your bed. The meeting itself has not charged anyone a cent, but the seat was paid for by someone, and kept by obeying. That is membership depending on money and conformity, run through a building instead of a basket. Test it tonight: is anyone in your meeting there because they will lose something if they leave?
*Sit with:* A.A. has no fees, no dues and no demand to conform, and a program can supply all three around a free meeting. Is anyone in my room there because a program will punish them for leaving?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 37 (Bill W., 1947, on personal government) and p. 28 (Bill W., 1946, on money).

### March 11 — "You Can't Keep Us Out" · *(the threat)*
The danger Tradition 3 answers was real: A.A. groups did try to throw alcoholics out, and failed. Bill W. admitted it (A.A. Comes of Age): "Groups have tried to expel members, but the banished have come back to sit in the meeting place". Expel means to put out for good; banished means sent away. What they said, sitting back down: "This is life for us: you can't keep us out." They were right, and Bill said why. Nothing in A.A., not its board, its conference, "nor the humblest group committee can issue a single directive to an A.A. member and make it stick". A directive is an order. The Long Form turned their stubbornness into the rule for every group (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence we may refuse none who wish to recover." The drunk is the reason the room exists, and a group that tries to bar him has forgotten what it is for. Today the expelling is done by others, and they can make it stick: a facility bars a former client from the meeting in its building, or a program discharges a man and he loses the meeting along with the bed. Test it tonight: if someone were told they could not come back to your meeting, who would have the power to say it, and would your group stand for it?
*Sit with:* Groups tried to expel members, and the banished came back saying it was life for them. Who could keep someone out of my meeting today?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — Tradition Nine and expulsion); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 12 — "Many Backgrounds" · *(before the tradition)*
In 1939, years before any written Tradition, the Big Book already described a fellowship of people who would never have chosen each other. Chapter 2 (Big Book, p. 17): "All sections of this country and many of its occupations are represented, as well as many political, economic, social, and religious backgrounds. We are people who normally would not mix." The Foreword to the Fourth Edition is honest about how far that was from true at the time. The line, it says (p. xxiii), referred to a Fellowship "composed largely of men (and a few women) with quite similar social, ethnic, and economic backgrounds." The first members wrote a door wider than the room they were sitting in, and the Foreword says those words "have proved to be far more visionary than the founding members could ever have imagined." Visionary means seeing further than the present. What held them together, p. 17 says, was a common solution, not a common background, and the Long Form later made the wide door every group's duty. Today some rooms sort themselves anyway: a meeting for one program's graduates, where a stranger feels the chill before anyone speaks. Test it tonight: who in your town would feel out of place at your meeting, and what in the room tells them so?
*Sit with:* The Big Book promised a fellowship of people who would not normally mix before it had one. Who would not normally mix with the people in my room?
*Grounded in:* Big Book, p. 17 ("There Is a Solution"); Big Book, p. xxiii (Foreword to Fourth Edition); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 13 — "Diagnose Yourself" · *(the threat today)*
A.A. leaves the question of who is an alcoholic to the alcoholic, and rooms that hand it to a professional have changed who gets in. The Big Book (p. 31): "We do not like to pronounce any individual as alcoholic, but you can quickly diagnose yourself." To pronounce is to declare it officially, and A.A. declines to. The book gives the reader the test and leaves him alone with it (p. 44): "If, when you honestly want to, you find you cannot quit entirely, or if when drinking, you have little control over the amount you take, you are probably alcoholic." The Long Form builds membership on the same ground (Big Book, p. 563): "Our membership ought to include all who suffer from alcoholism." All who suffer, by their own account. Not all who score high enough on someone's intake form. Today the pronouncing is done for him. An assessment decides he has a disorder and assigns him meetings; another decides he does not qualify, and the meetings stop. Either way someone with a clipboard has answered the one question A.A. leaves to the drinker, and the room has let them. Test it tonight: when a newcomer walks into your meeting, does anyone ask him for anything but his first name?
*Sit with:* The Big Book will not pronounce anyone an alcoholic; it leaves that to the drinker. Who decides whether a person belongs in my room?
*Grounded in:* Big Book, p. 31 ("More About Alcoholism"); Big Book, p. 44 ("We Agnostics"); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 14 — "As a Group" · *(how a group breaks it)*
The Long Form never asks a member to give up his church, his party or his job; it asks the group, as a group, to belong to nothing. The words are exact (Big Book, p. 563): "provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." The Big Book shows both halves on one page. The members (p. xx): "By personal religious affiliation, we include Catholics, Protestants, Jews, Hindus, and a sprinkling of Moslems and Buddhists." The fellowship, a few lines earlier: "Alcoholics Anonymous is not a religious organization. Neither does A.A. take any particular medical point of view". Every member brings his own ties through the door. The group takes on none of them. Now picture a group breaking it on its own. The church that hosts the meeting asks whether it would like to be listed as one of the church's ministries. A coalition invites the group to sign on as a member organization. A program offers to print the meeting on its alumni page. Each time the members vote yes, because the people asking are kind and the cause sounds good. And each time the group, as a group, becomes part of something else. Test it tonight: is your group listed anywhere as belonging to, partnering with or serving under anyone, and did your group ever vote on it?
*Sit with:* Members carry every affiliation there is; the group carries none. Has my group signed itself on to anything?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book, p. xx (Foreword to Second Edition).

### March 15 — "Court of Last Appeal" · *(how it gets captured)*
A meeting that only a facility's residents may attend has closed A.A.'s door on the drunk it was built for. Bill W. described the moment the early groups stopped being afraid of newcomers (A.A. Comes of Age): "Who are we to keep anybody out? To many a desperate drunk A.A. is the court of his last appeal. How can we slam the door on anybody who stands outside?" A court of last appeal is the last place you can go when every other court has said no. For the man still drinking, the hospitals, the jails and his family have often said no already. The Long Form makes the open door the duty of every group (Big Book, p. 563): "Hence we may refuse none who wish to recover." Now watch it get hollowed out. A treatment centre holds a meeting on its locked unit, or in its residence after hours, and calls it A.A. The residents attend. The drunk off the street, with no insurance and no bed there, cannot get past the front desk. Nobody voted to refuse him. The building did it. A room he cannot enter is not keeping Tradition 3, whatever it reads at the start. Test it tonight: if a stranger smelling of drink walked up to your meeting's door, would anyone but the members decide whether he came in?
*Sit with:* To a desperate drunk, A.A. is the last court that has not said no. Is there a door, desk or lock between my meeting and the man still drinking?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — Tradition Three); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

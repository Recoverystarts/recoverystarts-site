### March 16 — "Republican A.A. Groups" · *(the threat)*
Tradition 3 ends on a clause that keeps A.A. in one piece, so that a drunk never has to pick a side to get in the door. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Any two or three alcoholics gathered together for sobriety may call themselves an A.A. group, provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." Affiliation means being officially joined to something else. Bill W. spelled out the danger (A.A. Comes of Age): "Obviously we could not very well have Catholic A.A. groups, Protestant A.A. groups," nor, he went on, Republican, Democratic or Communist ones. Picture that meeting list. A drinker from the wrong church or the wrong party reads it and finds no room that is simply his. Every second label on a group marks the door for somebody and against somebody else. Notice, too, how Bill says the line is kept: any group is "urged—though never commanded—not to make any other affiliation." Nobody polices it from above. The groups hold it themselves, or it isn't held. Today the second label is rarely a party. It is a treatment program, a clinic or a recovery residence, printed beside the words "A.A. meeting." Test it tonight: read your meeting's name and listing out loud, and ask whether a drinker from anywhere would hear his own room.
*Sit with:* Any second name on a group marks its door for some drinkers and against others. Whose name besides the group's own is attached to my meeting?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy).

### March 17 — "The Home Which Had Sheltered Them" · *(before the tradition)*
The hardest affiliation to give up is one with people who have been good to you, and Akron's first alcoholics gave one up anyway. Their early meetings were held in the home of T. Henry and Clarace Williams, members of the Oxford Groups, the movement where Bill and Dr. Bob had first found help. Bill W. remembered (A.A. Comes of Age) that T. Henry "was bighearted enough, nevertheless, to let us alcoholics come to his house for meetings" alongside his own friends. The drunks repaid him with "cigarette burns on their carpets." Then the break. It was "not until later, and well after the A.A. book was published," Bill says, that Akron's members withdrew from the Oxford Groups "and finally from the home which had sheltered them so well." There is no bitterness in the telling. Bill counts the Williamses "in the front rank of A.A.'s pioneers." The Long Form later wrote down what Akron had done (Big Book, p. 563): two or three alcoholics may call themselves an A.A. group "provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." A group can owe thanks to anyone. It can belong to nobody. Test it tonight: list who has been kind to your group, a landlord, a church, a hospital, and ask whether any of them has quietly become part of it.
*Sit with:* Akron's alcoholics left the movement and the house that had sheltered them, and stayed grateful to both. Has anyone who helped my group become part of it?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Recovery: the First Legacy — Akron and the Williams home); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 18 — "Thrown Out of a Club" · *(the threat today)*
No program, house or club can end anyone's A.A. membership, because none of them ever started it. Bill W. wrote in 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 15): "Though he may be thrown out of a club, nobody thinks of throwing him out of A.A. He is a member as long as he says he is." A club is a building with rules, run by the people who pay for it. A.A. is not a building. The Long Form says why the difference matters (Big Book, p. 563): "Our membership ought to include all who suffer from alcoholism. Hence we may refuse none who wish to recover." Today the confusion runs the other way. A woman finishes a program, or breaks a rule at a sober house, and is discharged. The "A.A. meeting" she went to every night was the program's, held in its building for its residents. She leaves believing her place in A.A. went out the door with her bag. Her bed may be gone. Her A.A. is not, because it was never theirs to hand out or take back, and every A.A. group in town has to take her in tonight exactly as she is. Test it tonight: ask whether anyone in your room has been told by an outside body that they no longer belong, and whether your group would tell them they do.
*Sit with:* A program can discharge someone and a house can evict them, but neither ever held their A.A. membership. Would my group make sure the person who was put out knows that?
*Grounded in:* P-17, p. 15 (Bill W., "Who Is a Member of Alcoholics Anonymous?", 1946); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 19 — "Thirty Days to Speak" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can make its newest members second-class with one well-meant vote, and the Long Form leaves it no room to. The business meeting is nearly over. Shares have been rambling, someone says, and the new people talk about drinking instead of recovery. A motion: anyone under thirty days listens and doesn't share. It passes with a few hands against. Now look at who it lands on. The man with two days, shaking, who came because he needs to say it out loud to people who understand, is told his voice comes later, once he has behaved. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Nor ought A.A. membership ever depend upon money or conformity." Conformity means doing what is expected of you in order to be accepted. A rule that says get sober first, then speak, makes a member's voice depend on exactly that. Bill W. put how it really works in four words (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 39): "We conform because we want to." People take up A.A.'s way because they have watched it work in the room, not because a vote ordered them onto it. The ones who most need to talk are the ones the rule silences. Test it tonight: read your group's format and ask whether anything in it makes the newest drunk earn his place.
*Sit with:* A rule that the newest members must wait to speak makes their place in the room depend on behaving first. Does anything in my group's format make a newcomer earn a voice?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 39 (Bill W.).

### March 20 — "Leave Them Alone" · *(how it gets captured)*
Clubs and offices stay servants of A.A. only while the members are free to walk away from them, and a meeting that lives inside a treatment centre has nowhere to walk. Bill W. wrote of the clubs and offices A.A. members paid for (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 29): "These are places of service; they cannot really control or govern A.A. If any of them were to become inefficient or overbearing the remedy is simple enough. The average A.A. would stop his financial support until conditions were changed." Overbearing means bossy, pushing people around. The members could always take a facility or leave it alone, because their membership never hung on it. The Long Form keeps it that way (Big Book, p. 563): "Nor ought A.A. membership ever depend upon money or conformity." Now turn it around. The meeting is held in a treatment centre, on the centre's schedule, opened by its staff. A drinker who dislikes how the centre runs things has no basket to withhold, because the centre doesn't need his dollar. If he leaves the centre alone, he leaves the meeting with it. The simple remedy Bill counted on is gone. Test it tonight: if your landlord turned overbearing, could your group pack up and meet somewhere else next week, under the same name, with the same members?
*Sit with:* Members can only keep a facility in its place if they can walk away from it without losing their meeting. Could my group leave its building next week and still be itself?
*Grounded in:* P-17, p. 29 (Bill W.); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 21 — "Any Way They Liked" · *(the threat)*
The Long Form gives two or three alcoholics a freedom so wide it alarms people, because the alternative is someone with the power to decide what A.A. is. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Any two or three alcoholics gathered together for sobriety may call themselves an A.A. group, provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." Bill W. did not soften it (A.A. Comes of Age): "This means that these two or three alcoholics could try for sobriety in any way they liked. They could disagree with any or all of A.A.'s principles and still call themselves an A.A. group." He saw the threat on the other side. Without that liberty, A.A. could harden into "a frozen set of dogmatic principles that could not be changed even when obviously wrong." Dogmatic means laid down as law, not to be questioned. And he trusted the bottle to do the correcting no office could: "In the end the innovators would have to adopt A.A. principles—at least some of them— in order to remain sober at all." Notice the one line the clause does draw. Not style, not format, not belief. Affiliation. Today the danger arrives as somebody's list of approved meetings. Test it tonight: when someone says a group "isn't real A.A.," ask whether they mean it has another affiliation, or only that it does things differently.
*Sit with:* The Long Form lets two or three alcoholics do it their own way, and draws its one line at affiliation. When my group calls another group "not real A.A.," which line is it using?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy).

### March 22 — "Homes Were Thrown Open" · *(before the tradition)*
Years before the Long Form was written, Cleveland showed what alcoholics could do with nothing but a door of their own. In 1939 the Cleveland Plain Dealer ran a series on Alcoholics Anonymous and the calls poured in. The first Cleveland meeting had started that June in a member's home. When it ran out of space it split three ways: one part to another home, one to a hall, one under a new name. Bill W. wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "These multiplying and bulging meetings continued to run short of home space," so they moved on "into small halls and church basements." The old-timers in New York and Akron could only watch, Bill admits, "with deep misgivings." About twenty members, "not very experienced themselves," were facing hundreds of newcomers. "But a year later we did know; for by then Cleveland had about thirty groups and several hundred members." Every one of those groups was alcoholics in a room they had found, answering to themselves. That is the shape the Long Form put in writing (Big Book, p. 563): "Any two or three alcoholics gathered together for sobriety may call themselves an A.A. group". Nobody handed Cleveland its groups. The alcoholics opened them. Test it tonight: if your meeting place closed tomorrow, could your members open a door somewhere else by next week?
*Sit with:* Cleveland's groups were alcoholics in rooms they had found, answering to nobody but themselves. If my group lost its room, would it still exist?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (When A.A. Came of Age — Cleveland, 1939); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 23 — "Refuse None" · *(the threat today)*
The short form's "only requirement" is often heard as a test that somebody gets to give, and the Long Form takes that power away from everyone. The short form (Big Book, p. 562): "The only requirement for A.A. membership is a desire to stop drinking." Read alone, "requirement" can sound like a gate with a guard on it. Read the Long Form beside it (p. 563): "Our membership ought to include all who suffer from alcoholism. Hence we may refuse none who wish to recover." Hence means therefore. Because membership belongs to every alcoholic, nobody in the room is given the job of refusing one. The desire is the drinker's own business, and he is the only one who can say whether he has it. Today the gate gets a guard anyway. A newcomer admits his wife made him come, or his boss did, and someone tells him he doesn't have the desire yet and should come back when he does, as if anyone else could measure it. The Long Form leaves no seat for that judge. It puts the refusing power at exactly zero, and it does that on purpose, so the drinker who isn't sure is still in. Test it tonight: ask whether anyone in your room has ever told a newcomer he wasn't really a member yet, and on whose authority.
*Sit with:* The Long Form says the groups may refuse none who wish to recover, so nobody gets to test the wish. Has anyone in my room ever made themselves the judge of a newcomer's desire?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); short form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 562).

### March 24 — "Five Dollars at the Door" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group that charges at the door has made membership depend on money, however good its reason, and the Long Form rules that out in plain words. The rent goes up. The basket has been thin for months. At the business meeting someone proposes a simple fix: five dollars at the door, waived for anyone who really can't pay. It sounds fair, and it passes. Now picture the man on his first night with nothing in his pockets, who has to say so to a stranger with a cash box before he gets a chair. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Nor ought A.A. membership ever depend upon money or conformity." The rent is real, and A.A. never pretended otherwise. When the money question got a hard look at the 1955 convention, Bill W. reported (A.A. Comes of Age): "There was complete unanimity that through voluntary contributions the legitimate bills of groups, areas, and A.A. as a whole must be paid or we could not properly carry our message." Voluntary contributions pay the bills. The principle Bill names in the same passage is "no compulsory fees or dues." The answer to a thin basket is to tell the members, not to charge the drunk. Test it tonight: ask whether someone could sit in your meeting for a year without paying a cent, and be just as much a member.
*Sit with:* A door charge, even one that can be waived, makes the newest drunk explain his empty pockets before he sits down. Could someone come to my group for a year without paying and still be a full member?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (When A.A. Came of Age — the St. Louis convention, 1955).

### March 25 — "Gathered for Sobriety" · *(how it gets captured)*
An A.A. group is alcoholics who gathered themselves, and a room of clients gathered by a counsellor is something else, whatever the sign says. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Any two or three alcoholics gathered together for sobriety may call themselves an A.A. group". Look at who does the gathering, and why: alcoholics, for sobriety, calling themselves a group. Now the captured version. A counsellor books the room, takes attendance, opens with the program's announcements and closes by reminding everyone the hour counts toward their plan. The people in the chairs are alcoholics, but they did not gather themselves, and attendance is the point. Bill W. wrote about that kind of pressure long before anyone built a business on it (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 38): "Time out of mind, families, friends, employers, doctors, clergymen, and judges have tried their hand at disciplining alcoholics. Almost without exception the failure to accomplish anything by coercion has been complete." Coercion means forcing someone. A.A. worked where all of that failed because nobody in it held the power to force anyone. Put a counsellor with that power at the front of the room and you have rebuilt what failed, under A.A.'s name. Test it tonight: ask who called your meeting into being, who keeps the attendance, and who could cancel it.
*Sit with:* A group is alcoholics who gather themselves for sobriety, not clients gathered for a requirement. Who called my meeting into being, and who could cancel it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 38 (Bill W.).

### March 26 — "None Can Be Forced" · *(the threat)*
Had A.A. insisted that every newcomer accept the Twelve Steps exactly as written, thousands would never have come in, and Bill W. said so plainly. A Presbyterian minister trying to start A.A. in Thailand took the Steps to the head of the largest Buddhist monastery in his province. The monk liked them, wondered about "good" in place of "God," then saw the words "as you understand Him" and was satisfied. Bill's comment (A.A. Comes of Age): "But here we must remember that A.A.'s Steps are suggestions only. A belief in them as they stand is not at all a requirement for membership among us. This liberty has made A.A. available to thousands who never would have tried at all had we insisted on the Twelve Steps just as written." Then the line that settles it: "Alcoholics may be led to believe in God, but none can be forced." That is the Long Form's word conformity at work (Big Book, p. 563): "Nor ought A.A. membership ever depend upon money or conformity." The threat was never a monk with an edit. It was a fellowship demanding agreement at the door. Today it comes dressed as being serious: a room that treats a newcomer's doubts as proof he isn't really in. Test it tonight: ask whether a drinker who believes nothing yet could sit in your room and be counted a member.
*Sit with:* A.A. made its Steps suggestions so that no belief would ever be the price of getting in. Could someone who believes nothing yet be counted a member in my group?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — the Buddhist monastery in Thailand); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 27 — "Twos and Threes and Fives" · *(before the tradition)*
In 1939, before there was any Tradition, A.A. was already spreading the way the Long Form would later describe: in little knots of alcoholics who needed nobody's permission. The Big Book's last chapter reports it (Big Book, p. 162): "Some of us are salesmen and go about. Little clusters of twos and threes and fives of us have sprung up in other communities, through contact with our two larger centers. Those of us who travel drop in as often as we can." The two larger centers were Akron and New York. Then the book turns to the reader. "Thus we grow. And so can you," it says, even if you are (p. 163) "one man with this book in your hand." Seven years later the Long Form gave those clusters their standing (p. 563): "Any two or three alcoholics gathered together for sobriety may call themselves an A.A. group". Size was never the test. Two was enough, if they were alcoholics, gathered for sobriety, belonging to no one else. Today a cluster like that is sometimes told it isn't a real meeting until somebody bigger takes it on. Test it tonight: ask whether your members could start another meeting across town if one were needed, and who they think they would have to ask.
*Sit with:* In 1939 two or three alcoholics in a strange town were already enough to begin. Does my group believe it would need anyone's permission to start another meeting?
*Grounded in:* Big Book pp. 162–163 (A Vision for You); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 28 — "May Call Themselves" · *(the threat today)*
The Long Form needs nobody's permission to make an A.A. group, and the rumour that it does hands the groups to whoever claims to give that permission. The Big Book (p. 563): "Any two or three alcoholics gathered together for sobriety may call themselves an A.A. group, provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." Count what the sentence asks for. Two or three. Alcoholics. Gathered for sobriety. No other affiliation. It does not ask for a building, a charter, a registration, a facility to host it or an office to approve it. "May call themselves" means the group names itself; nobody names it for them. Today people who want to start a meeting are sometimes told they first need a church or a centre to take them on, or someone's approval before it counts. The ones who believe it go looking for a host, and the host is exactly how the other affiliation gets in. A meeting list is a service that tells drinkers where to find groups. It is not a licence, and being left off one makes a group no less A.A. The words that make a group were written for the two or three, not for anyone above them. Test it tonight: ask your group whether it believes it exists because of its members, or because of someone's permission.
*Sit with:* The Long Form lets two or three alcoholics call themselves a group, and nobody else gets a say in it. Does my group think it exists because of its members, or because someone allowed it?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563).

### March 29 — "Who Holds the Password" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A meeting that only lets in people its members already know has started refusing the very drinker the Long Form says it may not refuse. It begins with a real problem. An online meeting gets disrupted twice, strangers shouting into the screen. The group votes for a waiting room and a password, sent by a member to anyone who asks. Months later, the password only travels between friends, and the host lets in the names they recognise. Nobody voted to close the door. It closed by habit. Meanwhile the drinker who found the link at two in the morning sits in a waiting room nobody checks. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Our membership ought to include all who suffer from alcoholism. Hence we may refuse none who wish to recover." Bill W. put the groups' side of it in 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 15): "We do not wish to deny anyone his chance to recover from alcoholism." The password was meant to stop the people who wreck meetings. It ended up stopping the people meetings are for. A door that only friends can open is a private party, however good the reason it was locked. Test it tonight: find out how a drinker who knows nobody in your group would get into your next meeting, and try it the way he would.
*Sit with:* A door that only opens for people somebody knows has already refused the stranger who needs it most. How would a drinker who knows nobody get into my group's next meeting?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 15 (Bill W., 1946).

### March 30 — "The Alumni Group" · *(how it gets captured)*
A group that takes a program's name has taken an affiliation with it, however proud its members are of where they got sober. Graduates of a treatment program want to keep meeting, so they start a group. The program offers its room for free. The group names itself after the program, opens with the program's news and welcomes each new graduating class. Every member is an alcoholic. But the group as a group now belongs to the program: its room, its name and its newcomers all come from one place, and a drinker who never went there can tell the room was not meant for him. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Any two or three alcoholics gathered together for sobriety may call themselves an A.A. group, provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." As a group. Each member can be grateful to anyone; the group cannot carry the debt. Bill W. saw the same line from the other side (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 34): "Years ago we used to think A.A. should have its own hospitals, rest homes and farms. Nowadays we are equally convinced we should have nothing of the sort." If A.A. should not own the facility, the facility should not own the group. Test it tonight: ask whether your group's name, room and newcomers would all survive the program closing tomorrow.
*Sit with:* Members may be grateful to the program that helped them, but a group that carries its name belongs to it. Would my group's name, room and newcomers survive that program closing?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 34 (Bill W.).

### March 31 — "Never Exclusive" · *(the threat)*
March ends where Tradition 3 begins: the threat to A.A. was never who might walk in, but a fellowship that would start choosing. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) says it in four sentences: "Our membership ought to include all who suffer from alcoholism. Hence we may refuse none who wish to recover. Nor ought A.A. membership ever depend upon money or conformity. Any two or three alcoholics gathered together for sobriety may call themselves an A.A. group, provided that, as a group, they have no other affiliation." All who suffer. None refused. No price and no test. No owner. Bill W. said where the groups had come to by 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 15): "We wish to be just as inclusive as we can, never exclusive." Exclusive means keeping people out. The Big Book had already set the goal for any town where a few A.A.s land (p. 164): "there will be no stopping until everyone in that town has had his opportunity to recover". Everyone in that town, including the one nobody in your room would have picked. A month of readings comes down to that drinker and a door no outside body controls. Test it tonight: picture the person you would least expect at your meeting, and ask whether your group's door, its rules and its landlord would all let them in.
*Sit with:* The Long Form asks for every alcoholic, refuses none, charges nothing and answers to no one. Who is the drinker my group would least expect, and would our door open for them?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 15 (Bill W., 1946); Big Book p. 164 (A Vision for You).

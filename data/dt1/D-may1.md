### May 1 — "Our Real Reason for Existence" · *(the threat)*
Tradition Five gives every A.A. group one job, and the threat it answers is a fellowship trying to do everything. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Each Alcoholics Anonymous group ought to be a spiritual entity having but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." An entity is a body that acts as one. One purpose, not five. And the alcoholic who still suffers is the one still drinking tonight, not the member already in a chair. In the early years, members wanted A.A. to build hospitals, educate the public and change the laws. Bill W. wrote what experience taught them instead (A.A. Comes of Age): "Most emphatically, then, A.A. has but one single purpose: to carry its message to the alcoholic who still suffers. That is our basic objective, our real reason for existence." Doctors, teachers and lawmakers have their own work. A group that takes on a second purpose doesn't double its good. It splits its attention, and the drunk who hasn't found the door yet is the one who gets dropped. Today the second purpose usually arrives looking helpful: a project, a partner, a program that would like the group's name. Test it tonight: ask what your group did this month that reached someone still drinking.
*Sit with:* An A.A. group has one purpose, and it points at the drinker who hasn't arrived yet. What did my group do this month for someone still drinking?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Five).

### May 2 — "Another Alcoholic to Talk To" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was a Tradition Five, there was one drunk in a hotel lobby who understood that his sobriety depended on finding another drunk. In May of 1935, in Akron, Ohio, Bill W.'s business deal had just fallen apart and he was alone in a strange city with about ten dollars. The bar at one end of the lobby was filling up. He told it later (A.A. Comes of Age): "I remembered that in trying to help other people, I had stayed sober myself." He did not go into the bar. He went to a church directory and a telephone, and called stranger after stranger, asking to be put in touch with an alcoholic. The list ran down to its very last name. That call led him to Dr. Bob, and before long to A.A.'s first group. Notice the direction. Bill did not go looking for someone to get him through the night. He went looking for a drinker to carry the message to, and that is what kept him sober. Years before the Long Form gave each group "but one primary purpose" (Big Book, p. 563), A.A. began with one alcoholic going out to find another. Test it tonight: ask who in your group last went looking for a drinker, rather than waiting for one to show up.
*Sit with:* A.A. started with one alcoholic going out to find another. When did my group last go looking?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Akron, May 1935: the hotel lobby); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 3 — "The Alcoholic Who Still Suffers" · *(the threat today)*
Read the last words of Tradition Five slowly, because the rumour about them points the wrong way. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Each Alcoholics Anonymous group ought to be a spiritual entity having but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Whose purpose? The group's, as one body. How many? One. Toward whom? The alcoholic who still suffers, and "still" is the word that matters. That person is still drinking. A member three weeks sober and shaky is struggling, and the group is glad to have them, but they are already inside the door. The rumour says an A.A. group exists to support its members, a circle where the people in the chairs look after each other. That turns the Tradition around to face inward. Nothing in the sentence makes the group's purpose the people already there. The members matter to it because they are the ones who carry the message out. Today the inward version is everywhere: meetings that describe themselves as support for whoever attends, with no word for the drinker who hasn't come. Test it tonight: listen to how your meeting describes itself when it opens, and ask whether the drinker outside is in that description at all.
*Sit with:* "Still suffers" means still drinking, and that is the person a group exists for. Does my meeting's opening speak to the drinker outside, or only to the people inside?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 4 — "The Card Players" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can break Tradition Five without voting on anything, just by turning its chairs inward. Imagine the same twelve people meeting on Tuesdays for years. They know each other's children and each other's jokes. The coffee is good and the talk afterward runs late. A stranger comes in shaking, sits at the back, and leaves before anyone has crossed the room to him. Nobody did anything wrong, and nobody did the one thing the group is for. Bill W. watched the same thing happen in the first A.A. clubs. Among the growing pains he listed (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 19): "card players were impervious to any suggestion that they talk to new people". Impervious means nothing got through to them. The cards were never the problem. The problem was a room that had become about the people already in it. The Long Form gives a group "but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers" (Big Book, p. 563), and a stranger at the back of the room is the closest that alcoholic may ever come to us on his own. If he leaves without a word from anyone, the group has missed its one job. Test it tonight: count how many members spoke to the newest person in the room before the meeting began.
*Sit with:* A group can drift off its purpose just by enjoying itself. Who in my group talked to the newest person last week?
*Grounded in:* P-17, p. 19 (Clubs in A.A., 1947); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 5 — "No Axes to Grind" · *(how it gets captured)*
A meeting run by a facility can use A.A.'s words and still lose what made the message work. The Big Book (p. 18) lists the conditions that make a sober alcoholic believable to a drinking one: "that there are no fees to pay, no axes to grind, no people to please, no lectures to be endured". An axe to grind is a private reason for helping. Now walk into a room that a treatment program runs and calls an A.A. meeting. Someone on staff opens it. The hour is on the client's schedule, and often on the bill. The person at the front has an employer to please and notes to write afterward. Most of those conditions are broken before anyone has said a word. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) asks each group to be "a spiritual entity having but one primary purpose", and a facility has other purposes it must serve first: beds, outcomes, payment. That is not a judgment on anyone's heart. It is how a business has to work, and it is why a business can't be an A.A. group. The drinker can feel the difference, even if no one in the room says it out loud. Test it tonight: ask of your meeting whether anyone in it is paid, and whether anyone running it answers to someone outside the room.
*Sit with:* The message works when nobody carrying it wants anything back. Does anyone running my meeting answer to an employer?
*Grounded in:* Big Book, p. 18 (There Is a Solution); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 6 — "Straight A.A." · *(the threat)*
In 1947 the threat to A.A.'s one purpose came from members who loved A.A. too much. Bill W. described the "do everything" member (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 24): "he also thinks we have the answer for everybody and everything touching alcohol." That member wanted A.A.'s name and credit behind research, hospitals and education, and he argued that millions could be raised for good works. It sounded generous. Bill's answer, from experience, was flat: "neither A.A. as a whole nor any A.A. group ought to enter any activity other than straight A.A." Straight A.A. means the plain work and nothing added: alcoholics carrying the message to the alcoholic. The Long Form says it in one line. Each group has "but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers" (Big Book, p. 563). Every side activity a group adopts takes time, money and attention away from the drinker it exists for, and it gives the public reason to wonder what A.A. is really for. A drinker who thinks A.A. is a campaign has one more reason to stay away. Today the pull is a cause that sounds close to home: a rally, an awareness walk, a joint event with a program. Test it tonight: list everything your group puts its name or money into, and ask which of them carries the message.
*Sit with:* A group that does many good things can lose the one thing only it can do. What does my group put its name to besides the meeting?
*Grounded in:* P-17, p. 24 (Dangers in Linking A.A. to Other Projects, 1947); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 7 — "A First Class Prospect" · *(before the tradition)*
The first A.A. group began with two sober drunks asking a hospital for the worst case it had. The Big Book tells it (p. 156): "One day they called up the head nurse of a local hospital. They explained their need and inquired if she had a first class alcoholic prospect." A prospect is someone you hope to win over. She had one: a lawyer who had been in eight times in six months and was strapped down in his bed. There was no meeting for him to find. They went to him and told him their own drinking stories until he recognised himself in every one. Within days he walked out of that hospital, and he never drank again. Then the three of them went looking for the next one. The Big Book (p. 158): "there were three alcoholics in that town, who now felt they had to give to others what they had found, or be sunk." Be sunk means be finished. That was a group before any Tradition named it: alcoholics going out to the one still drinking, because their own lives depended on it. The Long Form later gave every group "but one primary purpose" (Big Book, p. 563). Test it tonight: ask whether anyone in your group has ever asked a hospital, a detox or a shelter where the drinkers are.
*Sit with:* The first group went to the drinker; it did not wait for him. Does my group know where the drinkers in my town are tonight?
*Grounded in:* Big Book, pp. 156–158 (A Vision for You); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 8 — "A Spiritual Entity" · *(the threat today)*
"Spiritual entity" in Tradition Five says what a group must not become, and the rumour has turned it into a mood. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563): "Each Alcoholics Anonymous group ought to be a spiritual entity having but one primary purpose". An entity is a body that acts as one. The rumour hears the word spiritual and thinks of soft voices, candles and a certain way of talking. That reading is about the feelings of the people in the room, and it lets a group feel spiritual while it is run like anything at all. Bill W. wrote down what the word was guarding against in 1946 (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 32): "an A.A. group is a spiritual entity, not a business concern." A business concern is a company. So a group is a spiritual entity in the Long Form's sense when it has nothing to sell, no client to bill and no payroll to protect. Then its one purpose, the drinker still out there, has nothing competing with it. Today the rumour lets a room with a price tag behind it call itself spiritual because the talk sounds that way. Nobody's private beliefs decide the question. The books do. Test it tonight: set aside how your meeting sounds, and ask whether anyone makes money because it exists.
*Sit with:* In the Long Form, a spiritual entity means a group that is not a business. Does anyone earn a living from my meeting?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 32 (Money, 1946).

### May 9 — "The Less Business, the Better" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group can drift off its one purpose one motion at a time, and every motion can sound reasonable. The business meeting is where a group decides how it will carry the message, and that is exactly where the drift starts. A motion for an anniversary dinner. A motion for group shirts. A motion for a speaker night, then a committee to plan it, then a fund for the hall. An hour goes by. Nobody mentions the detox ward across town that hasn't seen an A.A. member in a year, or the outreach list that hasn't been updated. Bill W. learned this lesson when groups tried to run their own drying-out places, and wrote (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 15): "We have been obliged to see that an A.A. group is primarily a spiritual entity; that, as a group the less business it has to transact, the better." To transact business is to carry it out. None of those motions is wicked. Each one takes a piece of the group's time, money and attention, and the Long Form gives a group "but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers" (Big Book, p. 563). Business crowds the purpose out quietly, because it always feels urgent. Test it tonight: at your next business meeting, time how long the group spends on anything that reaches a drinker.
*Sit with:* Every piece of business a group takes on competes with its one purpose. How much of my group's last business meeting was about the drinker?
*Grounded in:* P-17, p. 15 (Hospitals and A.A., 1947); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 10 — "As No One Else Can" · *(how it gets captured)*
The message works because it comes from a drunk, and a program that replaces the drunk with staff keeps the words and loses the message. Bill W. wrote of A.A.'s one purpose (A.A. Comes of Age): "It is our experience as alcoholics that makes us of unique value on our sector of the total alcoholic front. We can approach sufferers as no one else can." Unique value means worth that nobody else has. Today that approach gets handed off. A counsellor, a case worker or a paid recovery coach does the talking, makes the plan and runs the meeting as one part of the program. Some of those workers have their own sobriety. They are still paid to be there, and the drinker can tell. One alcoholic who wants nothing back can say something no paid worker can: I was where you are, and nobody is paying me to sit here. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) gives the job to the group, not to a facility: "carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." When a group lets a program carry the message for it, the group may still meet every week, but its one purpose has moved out of the room and onto somebody's payroll. Test it tonight: ask who in your town reaches the drinker first, a program or your group's members.
*Sit with:* A drinker can be reached by another alcoholic as by no one else. Who reaches the drinkers in my town first, my group or a program?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Five); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 11 — "Bankrupt Idealists" · *(the threat)*
Early A.A. was nearly pulled off its purpose by its own big dreams. Bill W. admitted it (A.A. Comes of Age): "We thought our society of Alcoholics Anonymous might prove to be the spearhead of a new spiritual advance. Our principles might transform the world!" A spearhead is the point that leads a charge. Sober for the first time in years, the early members imagined fixing politics, factories and everything else. Bill said why: "How natural that was, since most alcoholics are bankrupt idealists." A bankrupt idealist is someone who wanted to do great good and ran out of everything trying. The danger was that A.A. would chase the whole world and lose the one person it could actually reach. What the founders found instead was a paradox, something that sounds backwards but is true: "The more A.A. sticks to its primary purpose, the greater will be its helpful influence everywhere." The Long Form makes that each group's rule: "but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers" (Big Book, p. 563). Today the dream comes back when a group wants to be a voice for every recovery cause in town. Test it tonight: ask whether your group's plans for this year are about changing the world or reaching one more drinker.
*Sit with:* The more a group sticks to its one purpose, the further it reaches. Are my group's plans about the world or about the next drinker?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Tradition Five and Tradition Six); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 12 — "The Prime Object" · *(before the tradition)*
The first A.A. meetings were built for the newcomer, and the Big Book says so in plain words. After Akron's first few men got sober, they gathered in each other's homes almost every evening. The Big Book describes them (p. 159): "happy in their release, and constantly thinking how they might present their discovery to some newcomer." Then a weekly meeting began, and it had a stated reason (p. 160): "Aside from fellowship and sociability, the prime object was to provide a time and place where new people might bring their problems." Prime object means main aim. Fellowship came along with it. Sociability, the pleasure of each other's company, came along too. Neither one was the point. The meeting existed so that a drinker, or a worried family, had somewhere to come. That was a group with one purpose before anyone had written a Tradition to say it. When the Long Form came, it said the same thing for every group: "but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers" (Big Book, p. 563). Today many meetings run as if the fellowship were the prime object, and a newcomer is welcome if he happens to wander in. Test it tonight: ask what your meeting would change if its only job were to give a new person somewhere to bring their problem.
*Sit with:* The first weekly meeting was held for the new person. Who is my meeting held for?
*Grounded in:* Big Book, pp. 159–160 (A Vision for You); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 13 — "Carry This Message" · *(the threat today)*
"Carrying its message" in Tradition Five means going to the drinker, and the Big Book's own instructions say so. The rumour says you carry the message by sharing at meetings, by being a good example, by showing up. Those are fine things, but they all happen inside the room. The Big Book's chapter on this work, "Working With Others," opens (p. 89): "Practical experience shows that nothing will so much insure immunity from drinking as intensive work with other alcoholics." Immunity means protection. Then comes the instruction: "Carry this message to other alcoholics!" To other alcoholics: people outside, still drinking. The chapter goes on to say how to find them, when to go, and what to say at the bedside. It is a rescue, carried out in the drinker's world, not a talk given in ours. The Long Form gives that same work to every group as one body: "but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers" (Big Book, p. 563). Today the word carry has shrunk to mean attend. A group full of members who never leave the meeting to find a drinker has kept the words and dropped the work. Test it tonight: ask how many in your group have carried the message to someone still drinking this year, outside any meeting.
*Sit with:* Carrying the message means taking it out to the drinker. When did my group last carry it outside the room?
*Grounded in:* Big Book, p. 89 (Working With Others); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 14 — "The Most Sordid Spot" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group breaks Tradition Five when it waits for the drunk to be delivered. Think of a group that once had members visiting the detox ward and the jail every week. Those members moved away or got tired, and nobody took their place. Now new people arrive only when a court or a program sends them, and the group has come to think that is simply how newcomers happen. No vote ended the outreach. It just stopped. The Big Book (p. 102) describes the opposite: "Your job now is to be at the place where you may be of maximum helpfulness to others, so never hesitate to go anywhere if you can be helpful. You should not hesitate to visit the most sordid spot on earth on such an errand." Sordid means filthy and miserable. That is where the drinker usually is, and a group sitting at its own door will never meet most of them. The Long Form puts the duty on the group itself, as one body (Big Book, p. 563): "Each Alcoholics Anonymous group ought to be a spiritual entity having but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Carrying is something you do on your feet. Test it tonight: ask your group who is going to the detox, the shelter or the jail this month, by name.
*Sit with:* A group that only waits for newcomers has stopped carrying the message. Who from my group is going out this month?
*Grounded in:* Big Book, p. 102 (Working With Others); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

### May 15 — "Stop Preaching" · *(how it gets captured)*
Turn an A.A. meeting into a class and it has been captured, because A.A. learned at the very start that lecturing drunks does not sober them. In his first months sober, Bill W. preached at alcoholics in a New York hospital and a mission. He wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "At the end of six months nobody had sobered up." Dr. Silkworth, the doctor at that hospital, told him why: "you're having nothing but failure because you are preaching at these alcoholics." When Bill finally sat down with Dr. Bob, he did not lecture. It was one drunk telling another what had happened to him. Today the class comes from outside. A facility runs a meeting where a counsellor picks the topic, hands out worksheets, and keeps the talk on the program's lessons. The words A.A. and Big Book may be on the board, but it is a lecture, and the drinker gets what Bill's first prospects got. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) gives the group its own message to carry: "having but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." That message is one alcoholic's own story told to another, not a lesson handed down to a class. Test it tonight: ask whether the person at the front of your meeting is telling their own story or teaching somebody else's lesson.
*Sit with:* The message is one drunk's story to another, not a lecture. Is my meeting a story or a class?
*Grounded in:* A.A. Comes of Age (Bill W. and Dr. Silkworth, 1934–35); long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563).

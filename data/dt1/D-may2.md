### May 16 — "Heaven Knows What Else" · *(the threat)*
Tradition Five exists because A.A. was once tempted to become everything for every drunk, and that would have cost it the one thing only it could do. Bill W. recalled the debate (A.A. Comes of Age). The conservatives said: "One alcoholic carries the message to another. Let's keep it that simple and stay out of money trouble." The radicals answered: "We need hospitals, paid therapists, traveling lecturers, rehabilitation centers, and heaven knows what else." A rehabilitation center is a place where people live for weeks under a program. The fellowship let that dream go: "When we got rid of our grandiose ideas about hospitals, research, rehabilitation, and education, not much of a bill was left to pay." Grandiose means far too big. What was left is the Long Form's one sentence (Big Book, p. 563): each group "ought to be a spiritual entity having but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Now read the radicals' list again. It is today's treatment industry, almost item for item, and some of it calls its rooms A.A. The early members looked at that whole list and said no, so the groups would stay free to do the one job. Test it tonight: ask whether anything your group runs, hosts or lends its name to is on that list.
*Sit with:* The early members turned down hospitals, paid therapists and rehabilitation centers so their groups could do one thing. Is anything on that list part of my group now?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — the money debate).

### May 17 — "Our First Failure" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was a Tradition Five, the first sober drunks in Akron spent their days on the hardest drinkers they could find, and failure did not stop them. Bill W. wrote of the summer of 1935 (A.A. Comes of Age): "Dr. Bob and Bill D. and I went frantically to work with more of those alcoholic characters at the City Hospital. We had a long string of failures." Frantically means with everything they had. One of the failures was Eddie. "Every time Eddie got drunk he wanted to jump off the Cleveland docks." Years later Bill could report: "He finally did make the grade and recently wrote me that he has been sober for seven wonderful years." Nobody screened Eddie for promise. Nobody waited until he was ready, polite or referred by someone. Three men, all newly sober, kept walking back into a hospital ward for the man still drinking. That habit, kept by a handful before anyone wrote a rule, is what the Long Form later asked of every group (Big Book, p. 563): "carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Today a group can quietly decide some drunks aren't worth the trouble: too many slips, too much chaos, too many tries already. Test it tonight: ask whether there is a drinker your group has stopped going after because he failed before.
*Sit with:* The men who started A.A. kept going back to drunks who had already failed, and one of them got sober years later. Is there a drinker my group has given up on?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Recovery: the First Legacy — Akron, 1935).

### May 18 — "But One" · *(the threat today)*
The Long Form gives an A.A. group one purpose, and the little word but in it means one, not the first of several. The Big Book (p. 563): each group "ought to be a spiritual entity having but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." The short form says it the same way (p. 562): "Each group has but one primary purpose." But means only here, as when we say but a few. The rumour reads the word primary as permission: first the drunk, then whatever else the group likes, a housing list, a social club, a place to talk through the week. The Tradition names no second purpose. A room still needs rent and coffee, but those serve the one purpose; they are not purposes of their own. Why so strict? The Big Book answers (p. 152): "Near you, alcoholics are dying helplessly like people in a sinking ship. If you live in a large place, there are hundreds." Every purpose a group adds takes its hours, its money and its volunteers away from that ship. Today the extras usually arrive as good causes, and each one sounds too kind to refuse. Test it tonight: list everything your group spends its time on, and cross off whatever never reaches a drinker.
*Sit with:* The Long Form says but one purpose, and the extras a group picks up are not on it. What does my group do that never reaches a drinker?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); short form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 562); Big Book p. 152 (A Vision for You).

### May 19 — "Tumors and Tapeworms" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group breaks Tradition Five on its own when its meeting slowly becomes about every trouble in life except the one the drinker walked in with. Bill W. put it in a doctor's picture (A.A. Comes of Age): "If we had discovered a widely applicable cancer cure, we would be begged to attend to that and that only. We would not try to look after all tumors and branch out into tuberculosis and tapeworms besides." A remedy that works for one illness is not improved by spreading it over all of them. Picture a home group a few years on. The topics drift: work stress, a divorce, the news, somebody's diet. Every share is honest. Nobody votes to change anything. But a man three days off a bender sits through the whole hour and hears no one say how they drank, how they stopped, or what happened next, and he does not recognize himself in the room. The Long Form gives the group one job (Big Book, p. 563): "carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Members have the rest of the week, and each other, for everything else. The meeting belongs to the drunk. Test it tonight: listen to your meeting as if you were three days sober, and ask whether you heard the way out.
*Sit with:* A cure for one illness isn't made better by spreading it over every trouble. Would a man three days sober hear the way out in my meeting?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — Tradition Five).

### May 20 — "No Sole Vocation" · *(how it gets captured)*
The message an A.A. group carries is one drunk's experience given to another for nothing, and a paid helper cannot carry it, however kind. The Big Book's writers said so about themselves (p. 19): "None of us makes a sole vocation of this work, nor do we think its effectiveness would be increased if we did." A vocation is a full-time job. They meant the work would not get better if somebody did it for a living. What reaches a drinker is a man who drank like him and wants nothing back, not even a paycheque from someone else. Today that role is for sale. Programs hire recovery coaches and peer workers, put a recovery story on the résumé as a credential, and send them into rooms called A.A. to be the newcomer's first friend, on the clock. The drinker is no longer met by one alcoholic wanting nothing. He is met by staff with a caseload and an employer. The Long Form gives that job to the group (Big Book, p. 563): "carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." A group whose first welcome is a paid one has handed its one purpose to a payroll. Test it tonight: ask who in your room is there because someone pays them to be, and who decides what they say.
*Sit with:* The Big Book's writers said the work would not improve if anyone made it a job. Is the first welcome in my room a paid one?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 19 (There Is a Solution).

### May 21 — "Schemes to Do Good" · *(the threat)*
Money in a treasury goes looking for good works, and that is how it threatens Tradition Five. A woman died and left A.A. $10,000 in her will, and the trustees of the Foundation, the board that held A.A.'s funds, had to decide whether to take it. The office was badly short of money that year. Bill W. recorded the warning (A.A. Comes of Age): "The pressure of that fat treasury would surely tempt the Board to invent all kinds of schemes to do good with such funds, and so divert A.A. from its primary purpose." Divert means turn aside. Nobody feared the money would be stolen. They feared it would be spent well, on something other than the drunk. The trustees declined it, and declined all such gifts after it. The same pull reaches a home group. The treasury grows past rent and literature, and the business meeting starts hunting for a worthy cause: a donation here, a community event there. Each idea is generous. Each one moves the group's attention a step away from the alcoholic still drinking. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) gives each group "but one primary purpose." Test it tonight: ask what your group's money did last year, and how much of it helped reach a drinker.
*Sit with:* The trustees turned down $10,000 because a full treasury invents good causes and forgets the drunk. What has my group's money been doing?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Unity: the Second Legacy — the $10,000 bequest).

### May 22 — "So Freely Given" · *(before the tradition)*
Before there was any group, A.A. began as one sober drunk looking past himself to the thousands still drinking. Bill W. wrote of his last days in the hospital (Big Book, p. 14): "While I lay in the hospital the thought came that there were thousands of hopeless alcoholics who might be glad to have what had been so freely given me. Perhaps I could help some of them. They in turn might work with others." That is the whole chain in three sentences: freely given, freely passed on, passed on again. He soon found it kept him sober too (p. 15): "I soon found that when all other measures failed, work with another alcoholic would save the day." Mark the order, though. The thought in the hospital bed came first, and it was about them, the hopeless ones still out there. Tradition Five takes that one man's thought and gives it to every group (p. 563): "carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Today the order can get turned around: the drunk still out there becomes an exercise for the members' sobriety, and when the members feel fine, the visits stop. Test it tonight: ask whether your group's work starts with the drinkers still out there, or with what it does for the people already inside.
*Sit with:* A.A. started with a man in a hospital bed thinking about thousands of drunks he hadn't met. Who does my group think about first?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book pp. 14–15 (Bill's Story).

### May 23 — "Anything Whatever" · *(the threat today)*
In A.A., service means whatever helps a group reach the alcoholic still drinking, and nothing in the Long Form gives the word a smaller job. Bill W. said it at St. Louis (A.A. Comes of Age): "An A.A. service is anything whatever that legitimately helps us to reach fellow sufferers." Then he named the first and greatest: "the Twelfth Step call is the greatest of A.A.'s services." A Twelfth Step call is going to a drinker who has asked for help. Everything else counts by how it serves that call: "the car we rode in, the gasoline we paid for, and the cups of coffee we bought him." Him is the drunk. The rumour says service is a ladder of chores: make the coffee, set up the chairs, chair the meeting, and those keep you sober. Coffee is service when it is poured for the stranger who just walked in. A position held only so the room can look after its regulars is a job, not service in Bill's sense. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) gives each group "but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Test it tonight: take each service job in your group and ask which drinker it reaches.
*Sit with:* Bill measured every A.A. service by whether it helps reach the drunk, and called the Twelfth Step call the greatest. Which jobs in my group reach a drinker?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Service: the Third Legacy).

### May 24 — "Would Disintegrate" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group breaks Tradition Five on its own the night it votes to stop going out to the drinker. Picture the business meeting. For years the group has sent two members to the detox ward on Thursdays. Lately the slot is hard to fill. Someone says the facility runs its own meeting now. Someone else says the local A.A. office handles hospitals. The motion passes: drop it. Nothing else changes. The meeting still opens on time, and the coffee is still good. Bill W. wrote in 1947 about what lasting groups do instead (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 38): "they stick to their single purpose of helping alcoholics to recover." And why: "Groups see they would disintegrate if they didn't." Disintegrate means come apart, slowly, piece by piece. A group that has stopped reaching drinkers has nobody new coming through the door, and in a few years it is the same twelve people, then eight. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) gives the job to each group, not to a committee somewhere else: "carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Test it tonight: ask what your group did last month that reached a drinker who wasn't already in the room.
*Sit with:* Bill saw that groups which stop helping alcoholics to recover come apart. What did my group do last month for a drinker outside the room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 38 (Bill W., 1947).

### May 25 — "Never Force Yourself" · *(how it gets captured)*
A.A.'s message is offered to a drinker who wants it, and a room run as a court's attendance office has traded that offer for an order. The Big Book's instructions for carrying the message (p. 90): "If he does not want to stop drinking, don't waste time trying to persuade him." And: "If he does not want to see you, never force yourself upon him." The whole approach rests on the drinker's own wanting. Today the forcing comes from outside. A court, a probation officer or a program orders someone to attend so many meetings and get a paper signed at each one. The group never chose this, and the Long Form says we "may refuse none who wish to recover" (p. 563), sent or not. The capture is quieter. The meeting rearranges its hour around the paper: a line for signatures, a chair whose real job is the stamp, a room half full of people counting meetings instead of drinks. The Long Form points the group at "the alcoholic who still suffers," the drinker, not the docket. A docket is a court's list of cases. Test it tonight: ask whether your meeting's routine is built for the drunk who wants out, or for the paper someone else wants signed.
*Sit with:* The Big Book says never force the message on anyone, and a court's paper is force from outside. Is my meeting's hour built around the drinker or the signature?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); long form, Tradition 3 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 90 (Working With Others).

### May 26 — "The Distant Alcoholic" · *(the threat)*
Tradition Five gives the message to every group because in 1937 it was in the hands of a few men in two cities, and that was a danger. That November, Bill W. and Dr. Bob sat counting recoveries: upwards of forty alcoholics were staying sober. Bill wrote (A.A. Comes of Age): "Then came a mighty sobering thought. This knowledge, this precious know-how, was still in the hands of too few of us. Must every alcoholic come to New York or Akron in order to get well? No, that would never do; that would be impossible." And then the question: "Could we carry the message to the distant alcoholic?" Distant meant every drunk who didn't live near those two men. The answer that grew was the group: wherever a few sober alcoholics met, the message lived, and could go out again from there. So the Long Form hands the purpose to each group, not to a headquarters or a few gifted members (Big Book, p. 563): "Each Alcoholics Anonymous group ought to be a spiritual entity having but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Today a group can act as though someone else carries it: the central office, the old-timer who takes the calls. Test it tonight: ask whether the drinker nearest your meeting would ever hear the message from your group.
*Sit with:* In 1937 the message was in too few hands, and the answer was every group carrying it. Would the nearest drinker hear it from mine?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); A.A. Comes of Age (Recovery: the First Legacy — Dr. Bob's living room, 1937).

### May 27 — "Twos and Threes" · *(before the tradition)*
The Big Book was written to start groups that would carry the message themselves, years before Tradition Five said so. Its writers expected readers to rise and go to others (p. 153): "They will approach still other sick ones and fellowships of Alcoholics Anonymous may spring up in each city and hamlet, havens for those who must find a way out." A hamlet is a very small village; a haven is a safe harbour. Read who the havens are for: not the members, but "those who must find a way out," the drinkers still looking. By 1939 it had begun (p. 162): "Little clusters of twos and threes and fives of us have sprung up in other communities, through contact with our two larger centers." No rule told those clusters what to do. The book simply took for granted that each would go to the next sick one, as the first members had. Tradition Five later wrote down what they already did (p. 563): "carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Today a group can treat itself as the harbour where its members dock, and forget that harbours exist for ships still at sea. Test it tonight: ask whether your group is a haven for those who must find a way out, or only for those who already have.
*Sit with:* The Big Book pictured little groups in every hamlet as havens for drinkers still looking. Who is my group a haven for?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book pp. 153 and 162 (A Vision for You).

### May 28 — "He Talked My Language" · *(the threat today)*
The message a group carries is its own, one alcoholic's experience told to another, and no program's curriculum can stand in for it. Dr. Bob said what reached him (Big Book, p. 180): "he was the first living human with whom I had ever talked, who knew what he was talking about in regard to alcoholism from actual experience. In other words, he talked my language." Dr. Bob was a doctor. He had read widely and heard the experts. What he had never heard was a drunk who had found a way out. The Long Form says each group carries "its message" (p. 563): the group's own, the experience of the alcoholics in its chairs. The rumour treats the message as any recovery content at all, so a meeting can be filled with whatever a program teaches: worksheets, coping skills, its vocabulary, a counsellor's talk. A curriculum is a set course of lessons. It is not A.A.'s message, and a drinker who came for that message gets a class instead. Today this happens most in rooms a facility runs and calls A.A. Test it tonight: ask whether the last newcomer in your room heard an alcoholic say how he drank and how he got out, in his own words.
*Sit with:* Dr. Bob was reached by a drunk who talked his language, not by more expert knowledge. Does my room carry its own message, or someone's lesson plan?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 180 (Doctor Bob's Nightmare).

### May 29 — "The Telephone" · *(how a group breaks it)*
A group breaks Tradition Five quietly when the drinker calls and nobody answers. The Big Book warned the first members what carrying the message would cost (p. 97): "Your telephone may jangle at any time of the day or night." Jangle means ring harshly. They expected to pay that price, because a call is often the drinker's one moment of willingness. Picture a group's listing. It once gave a member's number, someone who agreed to take the late calls. That member moved away. Nobody volunteered, so the group put a general line on the schedule, and the line goes to a voicemail box no one has checked since spring. The meeting still runs every week. But the drinker who works up the nerve at two in the morning hears a recording, hangs up, and pours another. The Long Form (Big Book, p. 563) gives each group "but one primary purpose—that of carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Carrying starts with being reachable. Today a group's whole public face may be one listing online, and if it leads nowhere, the group has closed its door without ever voting to. Test it tonight: call the number your group gives out, and see who answers.
*Sit with:* The first members expected the phone to ring at any hour, because the call is the drinker's moment. Who answers when someone calls my group?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 97 (Working With Others).

### May 30 — "Without Charge or Obligation" · *(how it gets captured)*
A.A.'s message reaches a drinker free and owing nothing, and once it is sold inside a package it is no longer A.A.'s message. Bill W. wrote that A.A. could complete its mission only if, as a movement (A.A. pamphlet P-17, p. 4): "we remain a spiritual entity concerned only with carrying our message to fellow sufferers without charge or obligation." Without charge means no fee. Without obligation means he owes nothing afterward, not even thanks. "Spiritual entity" is the Long Form's own phrase for a group (Big Book, p. 563). Today the capture comes on an invoice. A treatment program sells aftercare, and the package lists "A.A. meetings" among its services, held in its building, led by its staff, logged in its file. The drinker, or his insurer, has paid. He has a counsellor's expectations to meet and a report going somewhere. Every word of Bill's line is broken at once: charged for, with obligation, carried by someone paid to carry it. A group that lets its name sit in that package has let its one purpose be sold. Test it tonight: ask whether anyone in your room was billed, by anybody, for the hour they are sitting in.
*Sit with:* Bill wrote that A.A.'s message goes to the sufferer without charge or obligation. Was anyone billed for the hour in my room?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); P-17, p. 4 (Bill W.'s foreword).

### May 31 — "Everyone in That Town" · *(the threat)*
The month ends where Tradition Five begins: every A.A. group exists for the drinker it hasn't reached yet, and the threat is forgetting he is out there. The Big Book ends its story of the first groups with one member just starting out in a big city, and says what follows once a few men there find their feet (p. 164): "there will be no stopping until everyone in that town has had his opportunity to recover— if he can and will." Everyone in that town. Not everyone who comes to the meeting. The book isn't counting members; it is counting drinkers, all of them, and it doesn't stop until each has had his chance. That is the Long Form's sentence in a picture (Big Book, p. 563): "carrying its message to the alcoholic who still suffers." Everything this month has warned against, the good causes, the paid helpers, the court's paperwork, the dead phone line, the class instead of the message, does the same thing: it lets a group forget the town. A group can be warm, solvent, well run and full, and still be doing nothing for the man drinking alone three streets away. Test it tonight: think of your group's town, and ask how many drinkers in it have had their opportunity from you.
*Sit with:* The Big Book counts success by everyone in town having a chance to recover, not by a full room. How many drinkers in my group's town have had theirs?
*Grounded in:* Long form, Tradition 5 (Big Book, p. 563); Big Book p. 164 (A Vision for You).

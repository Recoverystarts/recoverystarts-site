Step 1 of 12 — Honesty

Step 1: Powerlessness

We admitted we were powerless over alcohol—that our lives had become unmanageable.

Step One is stated on page 59 of the Big Book, at the top of the list offered as a program of recovery: 'We admitted we were powerless over alcohol—that our lives had become unmanageable.' Every other Step stands on this one. The book puts the requirement plainly in More About Alcoholism: 'We learned that we had to fully concede to our innermost selves that we were alcoholics. This is the first step in recovery' (page 30). Notice how high that bar sits. Not suspect it. Not say it out loud at a meeting while privately keeping an escape hatch open. Fully concede, to our innermost selves. Step One is taken when the argument inside us is over.

What Powerless Means in Big Book Terms

The Big Book describes alcoholism as a two-part problem, and powerless covers both parts. The first is physical. The Doctor's Opinion (pages xxv to xxxii) describes an allergy of the body: once alcohol is in the system, a craving develops that is limited to alcoholics and never occurs in the average temperate drinker (page xxviii). That is why the count of drinks never holds. The second part is mental, and it is the one that does the real damage. Page 24 tells us the real alcoholic has 'lost the power of choice in drink.' The memory of last month's humiliation does not surface with enough force when the thought of a drink arrives. 'We are without defense against the first drink' (page 24). And page 30 names the engine underneath it all: 'The idea that somehow, someday he will control and enjoy his drinking is the great obsession of every abnormal drinker.' Powerless is not an insult. It is a factual description: a body that cannot handle alcohol and a mind that cannot leave it alone.

Unmanageable is the second half of the admission, and it follows from the first. When drinking runs on that cycle of craving and obsession, life organizes itself around the problem. Jobs, money, marriages, health, and self-respect take their turns going sideways. But the deeper unmanageability is internal. Chapter 2 describes the point where even the most powerful desire to stop drinking is of no avail (page 24). We could not manage our own thinking about alcohol, so we could not manage the life that thinking kept producing.

Where Step One Lives in the Text

Although the Step itself appears on page 59, the whole front of the book is Step One material. The Doctor's Opinion (pages xxv to xxxii) gives the medical picture of the allergy and the craving. Bill's Story (pages 1 to 16) shows one man living it out. There Is a Solution (pages 17 to 29) explains why willpower and self-knowledge fail us at the critical moment. And More About Alcoholism (pages 30 to 43) is the Step One chapter proper; its stated job is to smash the delusion that we are like other people. If you are working Step One, those pages are the assignment.

How the Big Book Has Us Work It

There is no worksheet for Step One in the Big Book. The instruction is self-diagnosis through rigorous honesty. First, the book invites us to test the facts rather than argue them: pages 31 and 32 suggest that anyone unsure of his condition try some controlled drinking, try to stop abruptly, and try it more than once, then judge the results honestly. Most of us had run that experiment for years without calling it one. Second, it hands us a plain diagnostic: 'If, when you honestly want to, you find you cannot quit entirely, or if when drinking, you have little control over the amount you take, you are probably alcoholic' (page 44). Third, it sets the standard: full concession, deep down, nothing held back (page 30). What changes when we do this? The fight ends. We stop spending ourselves proving we can drink safely and become teachable. The book warns that 'Half measures availed us nothing' (page 59), and Step One is where we stop taking half measures with the truth.

Here is the honest part. Nearly everyone balks at this Step, because admitting powerlessness sounds like admitting we are weak, or finished, or less than other people. Many of us had built an identity on being the one who could handle things, and Step One felt like signing the papers on our own defeat. The experience of those who have taken it is the opposite. The admission does not end anything except an exhausting performance. We had already lost the fight; Step One simply stops us from pretending otherwise, and the pretending was the thing keeping us sick. There is real relief on the far side, the kind that comes from finally setting down something too heavy to carry.

Step One leaves us with a problem fully admitted and still unsolved, and the book means to leave us there for a moment. 'Lack of power, that was our dilemma' (page 45). If we are powerless, then help has to come from somewhere beyond our own resources. That is exactly where Step Two picks up: 'Came to believe that a Power greater than ourselves could restore us to sanity' (page 59). First we admit that we cannot. Then we begin to look, honestly and without dread, at what can.

— Alcoholics Anonymous (Big Book), 4th Edition: The Doctor's Opinion (pp. xxv-xxxii); Bill's Story (pp. 1-16); There Is a Solution (pp. 17-29, esp. p. 24); More About Alcoholism (pp. 30-43, esp. pp. 30-32); We Agnostics (pp. 44-45); How It Works (pp. 58-60, Step 1 on p. 59)

Quick answers

What does 'powerless over alcohol' mean in Step 1 of AA?

In the Big Book it means two specific things: a physical craving that starts once alcohol is in the body, described in The Doctor's Opinion (page xxviii), and a mental obsession that returns when we are sober. Page 24 says the real alcoholic has 'lost the power of choice in drink.' It is a description of a condition, not a judgment of character.

How do I actually work Step 1 from the Big Book?

Read The Doctor's Opinion through More About Alcoholism (pages xxv to 43) and check your own drinking history against what they describe. The book's own test appears on pages 31 and 32: try some controlled drinking, more than once, and judge the results honestly. Step One is taken when you can fully concede to your innermost self that you cannot control alcohol and that life built around it has become unmanageable (page 30).

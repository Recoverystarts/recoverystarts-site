Step 10 of 12 — Perseverance

Step 10: Continued Personal Inventory

The daily practice that keeps clean what Steps Four through Nine cleaned up.

Step 10 is the moment the program stops being a project and becomes a way of life. The Big Book lists it on page 59: "Continued to take personal inventory and when we were wrong promptly admitted it." By the time we reach this Step we have cleaned house through Steps Four through Nine. Step 10 asks us to keep it clean. Page 84 introduces it as the practice of continuing our inventory and setting right new mistakes as we make them, right as we go along. We are not asked to relive the Fourth Step every morning. We are asked to notice today's mess today, while it is still small enough to fix with a sentence instead of an amends list.

Where Step 10 Lives in the Big Book

The Step itself appears in the Twelve Steps on page 59, in the chapter "How It Works." The working instructions come near the end of "Into Action," on pages 84 and 85, immediately after the Ninth Step Promises. That placement is no accident. The book has just finished describing a life we could hardly have imagined, and without taking a breath it tells us how to keep it. Page 84 says we have entered the world of the Spirit, and that our next job is to grow in understanding and effectiveness. Then it sets the timeline: "This is not an overnight matter. It should continue for our lifetime" (page 84). Step 10 is not a chore bolted onto recovery. It is what recovery looks like from here forward.

How the Big Book Says to Work It

The instructions on page 84 are short enough to memorize and specific enough to use before lunch. "Continue to watch for selfishness, dishonesty, resentment, and fear" (page 84). Those are the same four troublemakers we hunted in the Fourth Step, and the book treats them as symptoms to catch, not sins to punish. When they crop up, the instruction is to ask God at once to remove them, to talk them over with someone right away, and to set things right quickly if anyone has been harmed. Then we turn our thoughts, resolutely, to someone we can help (page 84). Notice the tempo of those directions: at once, immediately, quickly, promptly. Step 10 is a speed Step. The old way was to let a resentment marinate for ten years. The new way is to catch it before dinner. The whole attitude fits in one short line: "Love and tolerance of others is our code" (page 84).

What Changes When We Practice It

Right after the instructions, the book hands us some of its boldest promises. "And we have ceased fighting anything or anyone—even alcohol" (page 84). Sanity has returned. "If tempted, we recoil from it as from a hot flame" (page 84). The reaction becomes automatic, given to us rather than manufactured by willpower. "We feel as though we had been placed in a position of neutrality—safe and protected" (page 85). The problem has been removed. But the same page carries a warning we ignore at our peril: "It is easy to let up on the spiritual program of action and rest on our laurels. We are headed for trouble if we do, for alcohol is a subtle foe" (page 85). The book is blunt about why: "We are not cured of alcoholism. What we really have is a daily reprieve contingent on the maintenance of our spiritual condition" (page 85). Read that carefully. The safety is real, and it is conditional. Step 10 is the maintenance the reprieve depends on.

The Honest Fear About This Step

Now the honest part. Most of us hear Step 10 and feel tired before we start. A lifetime of daily inventory sounds like living under a microscope, and promptly admitting we are wrong sounds like signing up to lose every argument forever. Some of us quietly fear becoming a doormat, apologizing our way through life. But the book never asks us to grovel, and it specifically cautions us not to drift into worry, remorse, or morbid reflection, because that would diminish our usefulness to others (page 86). It asks us to watch for four specific things and deal with them fast, the way we would patch a small leak before it floods the basement. In practice, promptly admitting a wrong is lighter than the alternative, which is carrying it. We spent years defending positions we knew were indefensible, and that was the exhausting life. Ten minutes of honesty costs less than ten days of resentment, and the Step immediately spends the saved energy on someone we can help (page 84).

Step 10 keeps the slate clean through the day. Step 11 shows us how to open and close it. The book moves straight on: "Step Eleven suggests prayer and meditation" (page 85), and the nightly review on page 86 grows naturally out of the habit Step 10 has already built. If Step 10 is the maintenance of our new life, Step 11 is its power source. When today's inventory is done, turn the page with us.

— Alcoholics Anonymous (Big Book), 4th Edition. Step 10 listed on p. 59 ("How It Works"); working instructions and Tenth Step promises pp. 84-85 ("Into Action"); daily reprieve warning p. 85; nightly review and Step 11 introduction pp. 85-86.

Quick answers

How do I do a Step 10 inventory every day?

The Big Book's instructions are on page 84: continue to watch for selfishness, dishonesty, resentment, and fear. When they crop up, ask God at once to remove them, discuss them with someone immediately, and make amends quickly if anyone was harmed, then turn your thoughts to someone you can help. It is a continuous practice carried through the day, not only a written list at night; the evening review described on page 86 grows out of the same habit.

What are the Step 10 promises in the Big Book?

On pages 84 and 85 the book says we have ceased fighting anything or anyone, even alcohol; sanity returns, we recoil from liquor as from a hot flame, and we feel placed in a position of neutrality, safe and protected. Page 85 adds the condition: this lasts only so long as we keep in fit spiritual condition, because what we have is a daily reprieve, not a cure.

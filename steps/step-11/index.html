Step 11 of 12 — Spirituality

Step 11: Prayer and Meditation

How the Big Book teaches us to improve conscious contact with God, one morning, one evening, one pause at a time.

Step 11 reads: 'Sought through prayer and meditation to improve our conscious contact with God as we understood Him, praying only for knowledge of His will for us and the power to carry that out' (page 59). Notice the word improve. The Big Book assumes that by now we already have some conscious contact. If we have worked the first ten Steps, page 85 tells us we have begun to sense the flow of God's Spirit into us and to develop what it calls a vital sixth sense. Step 11 is how we keep that connection alive and growing instead of letting it fade.

The stakes are spelled out on the same page. 'What we really have is a daily reprieve contingent on the maintenance of our spiritual condition' (page 85). We are not cured of alcoholism. The reprieve is renewed one day at a time, and prayer and meditation are the maintenance work. The housecleaning of Steps 4 through 9 opened a channel between us and God; Step 11 is how we keep it open.

Where Step 11 Lives in the Big Book

The Step itself appears in the famous list on page 59, in the chapter 'How It Works.' The instructions for actually working it come near the end of Chapter 6, 'Into Action,' on pages 85 through 88. It is a short passage, barely three pages, but the book is not casual about it. 'It would be easy to be vague about this matter. Yet, we believe we can make some definite and valuable suggestions' (page 86). What follows is one of the most practical stretches of writing in the whole book: a simple routine for night, morning, and all the hours in between.

How the Big Book Says to Work Step 11

The routine starts at night. 'When we retire at night, we constructively review our day' (page 86). We ask the inventory questions: 'Were we resentful, selfish, dishonest or afraid?' (page 86). Do we owe an apology? Were we thinking mostly of ourselves, or of what we could pack into the stream of life? The book adds a gentle warning here. We must not drift into worry, remorse, or morbid reflection, because that would diminish our usefulness to others (page 86). After the review we simply ask God's forgiveness and ask what corrective measures should be taken.

The morning gets its own instructions. 'On awakening let us think about the twenty-four hours ahead' (page 86). We consider our plans for the day and ask God to direct our thinking, especially asking that it be kept clear of self-pity and dishonest or self-seeking motives (page 86). When we face indecision, we ask God for inspiration, an intuitive thought or a decision, and then we relax and stop struggling (page 86). We usually close the meditation with a prayer that we be shown our next step through the day, asking especially for freedom from self-will and taking care never to pray for our own selfish ends (page 87).

Then the Step goes portable. 'As we go through the day we pause, when agitated or doubtful, and ask for the right thought or action' (page 87). We remind ourselves that we are no longer running the show, humbly repeating 'Thy will be done' (page 88) many times a day. That one pause, taken in traffic or mid-argument, is Step 11 in its working clothes.

What Changes When We Practice It

The book is specific about the payoff. Living this way, we are in much less danger of excitement, fear, anger, worry, self-pity, or foolish decisions, and we stop burning up energy trying to arrange life to suit ourselves (page 88). Page 87 promises that what used to be the occasional hunch or inspiration gradually becomes a working part of the mind. Guidance stops feeling like a lightning strike and starts feeling like a quiet, reliable sense of the next right thing. The book closes the passage with one of its plainest sentences: 'It works—it really does' (page 88).

If Prayer Feels Awkward or Fake

Let's be honest about the resistance, because most of us felt it. We did not know how to pray. We felt like hypocrites talking to a God we had doubted, ignored, or blamed. Meditation sounded like something for monks. The book saw us coming: 'Step Eleven suggests prayer and meditation. We shouldn't be shy on this matter of prayer' (page 85). It even tells those of us with no religious background that we are allowed to borrow. We can memorize a few set prayers, lean on helpful books, and 'Be quick to see where religious people are right. Make use of what they offer' (page 87). Remember, the Step says God as we understood Him. No eloquence is required. A clumsy, honest request for knowledge of His will counts, and in our experience it gets answered.

Step 11 is not the finish line. Right after telling us it works, the book says, 'But this is not all. There is action and more action' (page 88), and it points us straight ahead: the next chapter is entirely devoted to Step Twelve. Conscious contact was never meant to be a private treasure. The power we are given in the morning quiet has a purpose, and that purpose is Step 12, carrying this message to the alcoholic who still suffers.

— Alcoholics Anonymous (Big Book), 4th Edition: Step 11 listed on p. 59 ('How It Works'); working instructions in Chapter 6, 'Into Action,' pp. 85-88; forward pointer to Step Twelve on p. 88.

Quick answers

Where is Step 11 in the Big Book?

Step 11 appears in the Twelve Step list on page 59, in the chapter 'How It Works.' The instructions for practicing it come near the end of Chapter 6, 'Into Action,' on pages 85 through 88, which lay out a nightly review, a morning meditation, and short pauses for guidance all through the day.

How do I work Step 11 if I don't know how to pray?

The Big Book says we shouldn't be shy on the matter of prayer (page 85), and it clearly expects beginners. Page 87 suggests memorizing a few set prayers, using helpful books, and learning from religious people where they are right. The Step itself says God as we understood Him, so a simple, honest request for knowledge of His will and the power to carry it out is enough.

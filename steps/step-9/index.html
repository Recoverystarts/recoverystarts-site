Step 9 of 12 — Justice

Step 9: Making Direct Amends

The Step where we stop saying sorry and start repairing what we broke.

Step 9 reads: "Made direct amends to such people wherever possible, except when to do so would injure them or others." By now the hard inventory work is done. Step 4 showed us the wreckage, Step 5 said it out loud, and Step 8 put names on a list and prayed for willingness. Step 9 is where all of that leaves the notebook. In Big Book terms, an amend is not an apology. An apology is words about the past; an amend is a repair made in the present. The book's own word is repair: "Now we go out to our fellows and repair the damage done in the past" (page 76). Money owed gets paid or arranged. Lies get corrected. People we hurt get faced.

Where Step 9 Lives in the Big Book

Step 9 belongs to Chapter 6, Into Action, and it gets more space than almost any other Step. Step 8 is handled in a single paragraph on page 76, and then the book spends pages 76 through 83 walking through amends situation by situation, with the famous Promises closing the discussion on pages 83 and 84. We are not doing this to look good or to be forgiven on schedule. As page 77 puts it, "Our real purpose is to fit ourselves to be of maximum service to God and the people about us."

How the Big Book Says to Work It

The instructions read like a casebook. For the person we resent, or who resents us, we go in a helpful and forgiving spirit, admitting our part and never arguing theirs, because we are there to "sweep off our side of the street" (page 77). For the people we owe money, the book is blunt about avoidance: "We must lose our fear of creditors no matter how far we have to go, for we are liable to drink if we are afraid to face them" (page 78). We tell them what we are trying to do and arrange the best terms we can. For wrongs with serious consequences, even criminal ones, page 79 says we remember that we decided to go to any lengths, and "we ask that we be given strength and direction to do the right thing, no matter what the personal consequences may be."

Then comes the exception clause, and pages 79 through 81 treat it with real care. Wherever possible does not mean wherever comfortable, but the second half of the Step is just as binding. We never make an amend that creates a new victim. If the whole truth would wound a spouse or drag an innocent third party into the wreckage, we hold off, seek counsel, and find another way to set things right. Page 82 pictures the drinking alcoholic as "a tornado roaring his way through the lives of others," and Step 9 is meant to still the storm, not spin it up again. Manner matters too. Page 83 says we should be "sensible, tactful, considerate and humble without being servile or scraping." And we do not wait for perfect conditions: "The spiritual life is not a theory. We have to live it" (page 83).

What Changes: The Promises

The Big Book attaches its most famous paragraph to this Step. "If we are painstaking about this phase of our development, we will be amazed before we are half way through" (page 83). What follows on pages 83 and 84 is the passage many groups read aloud at every meeting: a new freedom and a new happiness, no more regretting the past, fear of people and of economic insecurity leaving us, the growing sense that God is doing for us what we could not do for ourselves. The paragraph ends with a working condition, not a wish: "They will always materialize if we work for them" (page 84). Members who have worked this Step say the same thing: the amends we dreaded most set us most free.

The Fear That Stops Us

Let's be honest about why this Step stalls. We are afraid of the faces, the slammed doors, the debts bigger than we remembered, the consequences we cannot predict. Some of that fear is realistic. The Big Book never promises a warm reception; it only promises that our side of the street will be clean when we walk away. Some people will not forgive us, some amends take years, and page 83 says some wrongs can never be fully righted, and we need not worry about those if we would honestly right them if we could. The freedom in this Step comes from our willingness and our action, not from the other person's response. That is why page 78 warns that the fear itself is the danger, because an alcoholic hiding from someone is an alcoholic getting ready to drink.

Step 9 clears the wreckage, and Step 10 keeps it cleared. Once the amends are underway, page 84 says we have "entered the world of the Spirit," and the book moves straight into the next Step: continuing to take personal inventory and setting new mistakes right as we go. If Step 9 is the great housecleaning, Step 10 is how we keep the house clean one day at a time.

— Alcoholics Anonymous (the Big Book), 4th Edition: Step Nine listed on page 59; working instructions in Chapter 6, "Into Action," pages 76-83 (resentments and enemies p. 77, creditors p. 78, serious consequences p. 79, protecting others pp. 79-81, the tornado passage p. 82, manner and timing p. 83); the Ninth Step Promises, pages 83-84; Step Ten begins on page 84.

Quick answers

What is the difference between an apology and an amend in Step 9?

An apology is words; an amend is a repair. The Big Book's instruction is to go out to our fellows and repair the damage done in the past (page 76), which means paying debts, correcting lies, and changing the behavior that caused the harm. Saying we are sorry may be part of it, but the Step asks for direct action wherever possible.

What does 'except when to do so would injure them or others' mean in Step 9 AA?

It means we never buy our own peace of mind at someone else's expense. Pages 79 through 81 give examples where a full confession would wound a spouse or pull an innocent third party into the wreckage; in those cases we hold off, seek counsel, and find another way to set things right without creating new harm. Page 83 adds that some wrongs can never be fully righted, and we need not worry about them if we would honestly right them if we could.
